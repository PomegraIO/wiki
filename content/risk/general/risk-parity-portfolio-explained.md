*A **risk parity portfolio** allocates capital across assets—typically bonds, stocks, and alternatives—so that each position contributes the same amount of volatility to the overall portfolio, rather than receiving equal dollar amounts. By weighting positions inversely to their volatility, the strategy aims to reduce the dominating influence of equities while leveraging less volatile assets to capture returns across multiple risk sources.*

## The Case for Equal Risk, Not Equal Dollars

Traditional portfolios often weight assets by dollar allocation: 60% stocks, 40% bonds. But dollars and risk are not equivalent. If stocks swing ±15% annually and bonds ±5%, a 60/40 portfolio's volatility is driven overwhelmingly by equities, even though their dollar share is only 60%. When markets panic, stocks and bonds still correlate upward, magnifying losses. Risk parity reverses this logic: allocate dollars inversely to volatility, so that a low-volatility asset like bonds gets a larger capital allocation than high-volatility equities—but after leverage, both contribute identically to portfolio risk.

In a typical risk parity construction, bonds might receive 40% of capital (with 2× leverage to boost their risk), stocks 30%, commodities 20%, and alternatives 10%, weighted so that each segment contributes roughly 25% of portfolio volatility. The appeal is intuitive: if bonds and stocks each drive an equal slice of overall swings, portfolio return becomes less hostage to equity performance alone.

## How Leverage Makes Equal Risk Possible

Leverage is essential to risk parity. Bonds are intrinsically less volatile than stocks—a 20-year Treasury bond typically moves 2–4 times less than an equity index. To make a bond position contribute as much risk as a stock position, the portfolio manager borrows to amplify the bond position. If bonds contribute, say, 10% annual volatility naturally, 2× leverage raises that to 20%, matching an equity segment's volatility profile.

This leverage is usually structured through [repurchase agreement](/repurchase-agreement/) (repo) funding or derivative overlays. A fund borrows cash at the [federal funds rate](/federal-funds-rate/) (or SOFR benchmark) to buy additional bonds, creating synthetic long exposure without owning shares outright. The cost of this borrowing—the funding spread—is a material drag on returns, especially when central banks raise short-term rates. In a low-rate environment, repo financing is nearly free; in a tight one, it erodes 50–100 basis points per year.

## Asset Classes in a Risk Parity Framework

Risk parity portfolios typically blend four major asset classes, each chosen for return and [diversification](/diversification/) properties:

**Equities**: Provide long-term growth. In a risk parity framework, equity positions are usually 30–40% of nominal capital, with lower leverage applied (or none).

**Fixed Income**: Bonds deliver stable cash flows and respond to inflation differently than stocks. They receive larger nominal allocations (40–50%) but 1.5–2× leverage to match equity volatility.

**Commodities**: Offer inflation hedges and supply-driven returns uncorrelated to financial assets. Their allocation is typically 15–25% of notional capital, often as [futures contract](/futures-contract/) exposure rather than physical holdings.

**Alternatives**: Including [real estate investment trust](/real-estate-investment-trust/) (REITs), infrastructure, and hedge-fund strategies. These contribute diversifying return streams and risk factors.

## When Risk Parity Works and Fails

Risk parity thrives in choppy, multi-directional markets where stocks, bonds, and commodities cycle through different leadership phases. In the 2010s, before the Federal Reserve's 2022–2023 tightening, risk parity funds returned 6–9% annually while delivering lower drawdown (peak-to-trough loss) than a traditional 60/40 portfolio. The strategy forced portfolio managers to "buy the dip" in equities and maintain bond exposure through rallies—a disciplined countercyclical posture.

The strategy stumbles when equities and bonds move sharply together—a rare but devastating scenario. In March 2020, as COVID-19 spread, stocks crashed and bonds sold off simultaneously (despite Federal Reserve intervention), both suffering losses. Leveraged bond positions amplified these losses, and many risk parity funds posted double-digit declines. Similarly, in 2022, persistent inflation eroded bond returns while equities were weak; a balanced risk parity portfolio gained little while carrying leverage costs.

The strategy also underperforms in strong equity bull markets. When stocks rally 20–30% annually while bonds flat-line, a risk parity portfolio's forced rebalancing into equities (to maintain equal risk) ensures it lags a simple 100% equity bet. This is not a bug but a feature: risk parity trades maximum upside for smoother, lower-volatility returns.

## Rebalancing: The Hidden Complexity

Equal risk contribution is not a static condition. As months pass and asset volatilities shift, stocks might become 35% of portfolio risk while bonds drop to 20%. To restore balance, the fund sells stock and buys bonds—a selling-high, buying-low dynamic that can boost returns. However, rebalancing entails transaction costs, [tax-loss harvesting](/tax-loss-harvesting/) friction, and the risk of whipsaw (rebalancing just before an asset class rallies). Large funds rebalance quarterly or monthly; smaller or more tactical funds may drift in their allocations.

## Leverage and Financing Costs

Risk parity's reliance on leverage makes it sensitive to funding conditions. In a [credit-spread](/credit-spread/) crisis—when repo rates spike and counterparty risk rises—a leveraged portfolio can face forced unwinding or margin calls. The 2008 financial crisis and 2020 March liquidity seizure illustrated this vulnerability. Even in normal times, leverage is not free. A fund borrowing at SOFR + 50bp to amplify bond positions and earning 4% on bonds nets only 3.5% after financing, a drag that accumulates.

This cost structure matters most to smaller funds or those operating with tight [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) budgets. A risk parity fund charging 50–100bp in fees and incurring 50–75bp in leverage costs must deliver outperformance just to break even after fees.
