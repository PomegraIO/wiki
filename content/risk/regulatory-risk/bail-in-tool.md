*The **bail-in tool** is a [resolution](/)-authority's power to write down or convert a failing bank's [debt](/corporate-bond/) to [equity](/common-stock/), so the bank absorbs losses and recapitalises without a government rescue. Where once [creditors](/accounts-payable/) expected taxpayer protection, bail-in makes them absorb losses in a defined, orderly way, shifting [credit risk](/credit-risk/) from the public balance sheet back to those who lent to the bank.*

## The pre-2008 alternative

Before the financial crisis, when a bank failed, regulators had two choices: a government-funded rescue or a chaotic bankruptcy. Bear Stearns and AIG got rescues; Lehman Brothers got bankruptcy, which spread contagion across the global [financial system](/). [Depositors](/accounts-payable/) lost money, [counterparties](/credit-risk/) seized [collateral](/), and fear froze [credit](/credit-risk/) markets. No one wanted a repeat.

The bail-in tool was designed as a third way. Instead of asking taxpayers to pay or letting creditors fight over crumbs in court, a [resolution](/)-authority could—with legal authority—convert [debt](/corporate-bond/) to [equity](/common-stock/) quickly and orderly. The bank would recapitalise overnight, operations would continue, and [creditors](/accounts-payable/) would absorb losses as equity holders. No public money.

## How a bail-in works

When a [resolution](/)-authority decides a bank cannot be saved and must fail, it invokes the bail-in power. The authority freezes the bank's shares, wipes out the [equity](/common-stock/) (which happens instantly), and then converts unsecured [debt](/corporate-bond/) to [equity](/common-stock/) according to a legal ranking called the creditor hierarchy.

First in line are [deposits](/accounts-payable/) up to the insurance limit (usually €100,000 in Europe, $250,000 in the US). These are protected and do not participate in the bail-in.

Next is any [debt](/corporate-bond/) legally subordinated to senior unsecured [bonds](/corporate-bond/)—this gets written down or converted first.

Then comes senior unsecured [debt](/corporate-bond/), which gets impaired next.

Last are [secured creditors](/accounts-payable/) (those holding [collateral](/)), though their [collateral](/), by law, comes out first.

The authority converts enough [debt](/corporate-bond/) into new [equity](/common-stock/) to plug the hole left by losses and restore [solvency](/). Old [equity](/common-stock/)-holders are usually wiped out entirely; [debt](/corporate-bond/)-holders become the new [equity](/common-stock/)-holders, often heavily diluted. The bank restarts with a cleaner [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/).

## The [total-loss-absorbing-capacity](/total-loss-absorbing-capacity/) connection

A bail-in only works if the bank has enough convertible [debt](/corporate-bond/) to absorb losses. This is why regulators mandate [total-loss-absorbing-capacity](/total-loss-absorbing-capacity/) (TLAC) for the biggest banks. TLAC is the stock of bail-in-eligible instruments; the bail-in tool is the mechanism that deploys them.

Without TLAC, a [resolution](/)-authority might find itself unable to bail in enough to recapitalise the bank, even if bail-in were legally available. TLAC ensures the tool can actually work.

## Losses to creditors are real

A bail-in is not a soft restatement of value. [Debt](/corporate-bond/)-holders take real losses. If you hold €1 million of unsecured [debt](/corporate-bond/) in a bank undergoing bail-in, and the authority must bail-in half the bank's unsecured [debt](/corporate-bond/) to recapitalise, you might receive €500,000 in new [shares](/common-stock/) that are worth much less than your original [bonds](/corporate-bond/). You lose the spread, the liquidity, the seniority—everything.

This is why [credit ratings](/credit-rating/) on bail-in-eligible [debt](/corporate-bond/) are lower than on senior unsecured [bonds](/corporate-bond/), and why the yield is higher. Investors price in the risk. In the early 2010s, many [financial institutions](/), pension funds, and [asset managers](/), burned by pre-crisis assumptions of implicit state backing, were reluctant to hold bail-in-eligible [debt](/corporate-bond/). The [market](/stock-market/) had to price a new risk premium.

## Speed and certainty

One advantage of bail-in over bankruptcy is speed. A court-supervised Chapter 11 (in the US) or equivalent insolvency proceeding can take years and erode bank [value](/intrinsic-value/) as clients flee and [counterparties](/credit-risk/) unwind. A bail-in, by contrast, can be executed over a weekend—write down [equity](/common-stock/), convert [debt](/corporate-bond/), and reopen Monday. The bank survives as a going concern; losses are crystallised and known.

This speed reduces [contagion](/systemic-risk/). Clients do not have years to worry about whether the bank will survive. Either it does (post-bail-in, with new [equity](/common-stock/) owners) or it does not. Uncertainty is killed.

## Sovereign [debt](/corporate-bond/) and bail-in limits

Bail-in works cleanly when a bank fails but the state remains solvent. What happens when both fail together? In the 2010s eurozone crisis, countries like Cyprus bailed in bank [depositors](/accounts-payable/) when government money ran out—a painful demonstration that bail-in can reach retail customers if the [fiscal situation](/fiscal-consolidation/) is dire. Most bail-in law now tries to protect [depositors](/accounts-payable/) up to insurance floors, but the risk remains if a country is insolvent.

Also, if a bank holds large amounts of its own government's [sovereign debt](/sovereign-debt/), a bail-in of the bank can weaken the sovereign (banks sell the [bonds](/bond/)), which can weaken the banks further. This [spiral](/), seen in Europe post-2008, shows that bail-in operates within the bounds of broader fiscal and monetary stability.

## Bail-in has not been tested at scale

The tool has been used in smaller cases (Cyprus, some European regional banks) but never on a truly systemic bank like JP Morgan or HSBC. No one knows whether a bail-in of a major global bank could trigger unforeseen [cascades](/systemic-risk/). [Counterparties](/credit-risk/) holding the bank's [debt](/corporate-bond/), [depositors](/accounts-payable/) above the insurance floor, or uninsured [creditors](/accounts-payable/) might suddenly lose confidence in other banks. Central banks and regulators would likely have to stand ready with [liquidity](/loan-origination-fees/) support to prevent panic.

Bail-in is not, then, a complete solution to the too-big-to-fail problem. It is a significant tool, and it has shifted risk back to creditors, but it is not bulletproof.
