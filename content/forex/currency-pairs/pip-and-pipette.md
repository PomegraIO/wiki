*A **pip** (short for "percentage in point" or "price interest point") is the smallest standardised price increment in a forex pair. For most currency pairs, one pip equals 0.0001. If EUR/USD moves from 1.0850 to 1.0851, that is a one-pip move. A **pipette** is one-tenth of a pip (0.00001), a finer granularity offered by modern trading platforms for tighter execution.*

## The pip as a unit of measurement

The pip is to forex what the penny is to US dollars or the basis point is to interest rates—a standardised unit that allows traders to communicate price movements clearly. Instead of saying "EUR/USD moved from 1.085000 to 1.085015," a trader says "EUR/USD moved up 15 pips."

For most major [currency pairs](/currency-pair-quoting-conventions/), one pip is 0.0001—the fourth decimal place. This is the historical standard. EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/CHF, USD/CAD all move in pips of 0.0001.

The Japanese yen is the exception. JPY pairs like USD/JPY and EUR/JPY are quoted to two decimal places (e.g., USD/JPY at 150.50), so one pip is 0.01—the second decimal place. A move from 150.50 to 150.51 is one pip in USD/JPY, even though the absolute decimal movement is larger.

## Why pips matter: direct link to profit and loss

The pip is not merely linguistic; it translates directly into monetary profit and loss. In forex, position size is typically denominated in units of the base currency.

Consider a trade: you buy 100,000 euros at EUR/USD 1.0850. Each pip move is worth $10 (100,000 units × 0.0001 × 1 dollar per pip ≈ $10). If the rate rises to 1.0860, you have gained 10 pips, or $100 profit. If it falls to 1.0840, you have lost 10 pips, or $100 loss.

The formula is: **Profit/Loss = Position Size × Pip Movement × Pip Value**.

This is why traders obsess over pips. On a [leveraged](/leverage-ratio-forex/) position, a move of 50 pips can mean tens of thousands of dollars. A trader managing risk must know pip values for the pairs they trade.

## Pip value varies by pair and position size

The pip value is not constant across all pairs or all position sizes. In USD/JPY (quoted to two decimal places), one pip is 0.01, so the pip value for 100,000 units is different than in EUR/USD.

For EUR/USD at 1.0850, buying 100,000 euros gives a pip value of roughly $10. For GBP/USD at 1.2650, buying 100,000 pounds gives a pip value of roughly $12.65 (because each pound is worth more dollars). For USD/JPY at 150.50, buying 100,000 units of dollars (selling yen) gives a pip value of roughly 1,000 yen, or—at current exchange rates—about $6–7.

Professional traders keep pip-value calculators at hand or on their trading platform. Brokers often provide one built-in. The calculation must be done before the trade, not after—knowing your pip value tells you whether the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) or potential move justifies taking the position.

## Pipettes and fractional pips

Historically, forex quotes were limited to four decimal places (or two for yen). But as [electronic trading](/algorithmic-trading/) advanced and spreads compressed, the need arose for finer pricing. Enter the **pipette**: one-tenth of a pip, or 0.00001 for most pairs.

A quote of EUR/USD 1.08507 includes a pipette (the final digit). Pipettes allow dealers and platforms to quote tighter [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and give traders finer-grained execution. A spread of 0.8 pips (0.00008) is now common; in the pre-pipette era, spreads were always whole pips.

For the retail trader, pipettes are mostly invisible—they see the pipeline on their platform and trade without thinking about it. For a [broker](/broker/) or algorithm, pipettes matter because they allow more granular pricing and tighter risk management.

## Spread in pips: the cost of trading

The [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) is typically quoted in pips. A "2-pip spread" on EUR/USD means the [broker](/broker/) is buying at 1.0850 and selling at 1.0852 (or any similar pair 2 pips apart). On a [market order](/market-order/), you are crossing that spread and losing that amount.

On a major pair with tight spreads, you might pay 1–2 pips per round-trip trade (buy and sell). On an [exotic pair](/exotic-currency-pairs/) with poor liquidity, you might pay 10–20 pips or more. For a trader with a view on a move of 30 pips, paying 20 pips in spread cost means you need the move to go 40+ pips in your favour just to break even.

## Historical evolution: why four decimal places?

The four-decimal convention (0.0001) originated in the pre-electronic era when currency quotes were written by hand and four decimal places struck a balance between precision and readability. The US dollar, being the numeraire for most pairs, naturally anchored the convention. The Japanese yen, quoted in smaller units (often in the hundreds), naturally settled on two decimals.

When the euro launched in 1999, it inherited the four-decimal standard. Today, it is nearly universal for non-yen pairs, enforced by market habit and platform standardisation.

## Pips in risk management and position sizing

Professional traders use pips to size and hedge positions. A trader with a stop-loss 50 pips away from entry and a target 150 pips away is using a 1:3 risk/reward ratio. By sizing the position so that a 50-pip loss is acceptable (say, $500), the trader knows the maximum loss per trade and the portfolio risk.

Algorithms use pip-based rules too. A trend-following system might set a trailing stop 30 pips below the highest price reached, or a mean-reversion system might exit when price retraces 10 pips from the entry.

Expressing risk in pips rather than dollars makes sense because pips are pairs and position-size agnostic. "Stop 50 pips" is instantly intelligible to any trader; "stop $500" requires knowing the pair and position size to have meaning.

## Pips and quoted versus unquoted pairs

Most quotes include pips. But some derivatives, like [currency futures](/futures-contract/) or [forward contracts](/forward-contract/), are quoted in different units. A Eurodollar futures contract might be quoted in basis points of yield, not pips. A forward rate might be expressed in absolute terms, not pip changes.

When trading across different product types or markets—spot forex versus futures, for example—traders must be careful not to confuse pip-based prices with other conventions. A miscalculation can lead to vastly oversized positions.
