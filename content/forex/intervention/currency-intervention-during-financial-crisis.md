*Central banks intervene in currency markets regularly, buying or selling to smooth exchange-rate moves or defend a policy target. But **currency intervention during a financial crisis** is something else entirely. When bank runs, asset-price collapses, or sovereign default fears sweep across markets, exchange rates spike or plummet in hours. Normal gradual intervention becomes useless. Central banks switch to emergency protocols: massive coordinated sales, dollar liquidity facilities, and direct currency-swap lines with other central banks. The goal shifts from managing orderly depreciation to preventing free fall, capital flight, and cross-border financial contagion.*

## How Normal Intervention Differs From Crisis Mode

In calm conditions, a [central bank](/central-bank/) might sell its currency gradually if it's overvalued, hoping to nudge the [exchange rate](/spot-exchange-rate/) down without disrupting markets. Daily sales might total $50 million to $500 million—noticeable but manageable. The market absorbs these flows; other traders and [market makers](/market-maker-trading/) continue normal business.

In a crisis, this approach collapses. A sovereign default rumor can trigger $5 billion of outflows in minutes. The [currency](/us-dollar/) falls 5, 10, or 15 percent in hours. All at once, foreign [creditors](/accounts-payable/) panic, corporations need dollars to pay [foreign debt](/debt-financing/), and domestic savers rush to move money abroad. Routine intervention—selling a few hundred million—is swamped by the exodus.

Central banks respond by shifting gears: they deploy their full arsenal, coordinate with peers, and accept that they must absorb enormous flows or let the [currency](/us-dollar/) fall sharply. The second option risks banking collapse (if domestic [interest rates](/interest-rate/) spike in response to currency weakness) or [sovereign default](/sovereign-default/) (if a nation can't service foreign-currency debt). So they choose the first: burn through reserves if needed, borrow dollars from the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) or other central banks, and inject liquidity.

## The Role of Central Bank Swap Lines

The most powerful tool deployed during crises is the **currency swap line**. The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) or [European Central Bank](/european-central-bank/) agrees to lend dollars or euros to a foreign central bank, receiving the foreign currency as collateral. The foreign central bank then injects those dollars into its own banking system, reassuring banks that dollar liquidity is available.

During the 2008 financial crisis, the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) extended swap lines to 14 foreign central banks. The total authorized credit exceeded $500 billion. The point was not to defend the [exchange rate](/spot-exchange-rate/) directly—the Fed doesn't care if the yen or pound falls—but to prevent a dollar shortage abroad from triggering bank failures in Tokyo or London.

A swap line is potent because it signals that the central bank's toolkit is unlimited. Banks can borrow the needed currency for 24 hours at a fixed rate, reassuring them that they won't fail due to a temporary [liquidity](/liquidity-risk/) crunch. Panic subsides; runs slow.

## Coordinated Intervention: The G7 Response

When a crisis threatens to spread across multiple countries, central banks of the G7 or G10 issue joint statements and coordinate intervention. The goal is to prevent [contagion](/systemic-risk/): if one nation's [currency](/us-dollar/) collapses, others may follow as traders flee the region or asset class wholesale.

**Example of coordination**: In September 2000, the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/), ECB, and other major central banks intervened together to support the euro, which had fallen to $0.82. The intervention message was clear: we are all in this; capital flight will not determine the outcome. The euro stabilized; panic subsided.

Coordinated statements alone often suffice. The announcement that major central banks stand ready, willing, and backed by unlimited foreign-currency reserves can stop a run faster than actual sales. [Counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/) fears ease. Borrowing costs fall. The crisis momentum breaks.

## Direct Market Operations in Crisis Mode

When panic is acute, central banks may buy or sell currencies directly in the spot [forex](/stock-market/) market in enormous size. The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) might sell $100 million per minute to stop the [dollar](/us-dollar/) from spiking during a flight-to-safety episode, or a [central bank](/central-bank/) might buy its own [currency](/us-dollar/) by the billions to slow a sell-off.

These operations are temporary. The central bank accepts that its effectiveness is limited if the underlying crisis persists. But it buys time: hours or days for other policy (rate changes, [fiscal](/austerity/) action, [debt restructuring](/debt-restructuring/)) to kick in.

## Capital Controls: The Backstop

In the most severe crises, some central banks impose [capital controls](/capital-flows/)—restrictions on outflows of foreign currency or bonds. These are controversial and economically costly, but they halt a terminal run. If a nation is hemorrhaging reserves and the [currency](/us-dollar/) is in freefall, controls can stabilize the situation long enough for negotiation, [debt restructuring](/debt-restructuring/), or policy reform.

Malaysia imposed controls in 1998 during the Asian financial crisis. While painful, they prevented full [sovereign default](/sovereign-default/) and allowed the economy to rebuild. Argentina, by contrast, maintained an open [capital account](/capital-flows/) while its [currency peg](/currency-risk/) crumbled in 2001, leading to a sharper crash and deeper crisis.

## Real-World Scenarios: 2008 and COVID-19

**2008 financial crisis**: As Lehman Brothers collapsed, the [dollar](/us-dollar/) soared (a flight-to-safety move). Foreign banks couldn't fund [dollar](/us-dollar/) lending; [credit](/credit-spread/) markets froze. The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) cut [interest rates](/interest-rate/) to near zero and offered massive swap lines to foreign central banks. Within weeks, dollar funding stress eased. The [currency](/us-dollar/) settled into a stable range.

**COVID-19 crisis (March 2020)**: Stock markets crashed; the [dollar](/us-dollar/) spiked. Central banks deployed swaps, [quantitative easing](/quantitative-easing/), and coordinated rate cuts. The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) again extended swap lines; the ECB cut [interest rates](/interest-rate/). By April, panic had eased. [Currency volatility](/currency-volatility/) fell. Markets resumed.

In both cases, the intervention was multi-pronged: rate cuts, [liquidity](/liquidity-risk/) injections, swaps, coordinated messaging. The [exchange rate](/spot-exchange-rate/) moved, but orderly—no free fall, no contagion, no cascading defaults.

## Limits and Trade-Offs

Intervention during crises has limits. If the underlying crisis is real (e.g., a nation truly is insolvent or its banking system is fundamentally broken), intervention merely delays the reckoning. Burning through [foreign currency reserves](/interest-rate/) to prop up a [currency](/us-dollar/) that will eventually collapse anyway is wasteful.

Central banks also face a trade-off: supporting the [currency](/us-dollar/) may raise [inflation](/inflation/) later (if the intervention is large or prolonged) or deepen the crisis (if it delays necessary [debt restructuring](/debt-restructuring/) or policy reform).

Yet in the immediate moment, when [counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/) and panic dominate, intervention stabilizes psychology and buys time for problem-solving. Most economists conclude this is worthwhile.
