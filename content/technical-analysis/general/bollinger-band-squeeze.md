*A **Bollinger band squeeze** occurs when the upper and lower bands of a Bollinger Band indicator converge sharply, signaling that volatility has compressed to historically low levels. This quiet period typically precedes a significant price move, though the direction of the eventual breakout depends on other market factors and confirmation signals.*

## How Bollinger Bands measure volatility

Bollinger Bands consist of a simple moving average (middle line) flanked by two outer bands, each placed two standard deviations away from that average. When price swings are small and irregular, the standard deviation shrinks, pulling the bands closer together. Conversely, when price jumps sharply, the standard deviation widens and the bands spread apart. A squeeze is simply the compression end of that cycle—the bands hugging the center line because recent price action has been unusually muted.

The width of the bands themselves becomes a volatility indicator. Traders measure the "bandwidth" (the distance between upper and lower bands) or use specialized oscillators like the Bollinger Bandwidth indicator, which normalizes that width over time. When bandwidth falls into the bottom quintile or decile of its range, the squeeze is officially on.

## Why squeezes precede big moves

Low volatility does not persist forever. Market participants eventually need liquidity, new information arrives, or long-dormant buyers and sellers step back in. The energy built up during a quiet period tends to release all at once, resulting in a directional thrust that can cover significant distance quickly. A squeeze is less a guarantee and more a *setup*—a warning that the market is winding up and about to snap.

Not every squeeze leads to a dramatic move; some end with a modest retracement followed by consolidation. But statistically, significant price swings are more common following squeeze conditions than in normally volatile periods. This makes the squeeze valuable for traders who want to position ahead of potential moves or set [stop-loss](//) levels in anticipation of volatility expansion.

## Interpreting the breakout direction

The squeeze itself does not tell you whether the breakout will be up or down. A trader must look to other signals: the trend leading into the squeeze (an uptrend may suggest an upside break), momentum indicators such as RSI or MACD (divergence can hint at reversal), order flow patterns, support and resistance levels, or broader market context. Some traders wait for the price to pierce the band before committing, confirming the direction before entering.

Others use the squeeze as a trigger to tighten [stop-loss](//) orders or prepare trade plans for both scenarios. The key is treating the squeeze as a volatility **signal**, not a directional forecast. Confirmation must come from additional technical or fundamental reasoning.

## The retest and follow-through

Once price breaks through a band (usually the upper or lower, depending on direction), it often retraces slightly and tests the band again. This retest can serve as a second entry point or a validation of the breakout. If price bounces off the band on the retest and continues in the original direction, conviction is higher. If price reverses back through the band and re-enters the squeeze zone, the setup has failed and traders should exit or wait for a clearer signal.

Volume is critical at this stage. A breakout accompanied by volume surge is more reliable than one on light participation. Low volume breakouts are prone to reversals and whipsaws.

## Risk and limitations

Squeezes can be deceptive. A very tight squeeze sometimes produces only a modest move before volatility contracts again. Conversely, a looser squeeze (band width at moderate lows, not absolute lows) might precede a larger move. There is no universal threshold—it depends on the asset, timeframe, and market regime. A daily squeeze on a stock is different from a 4-hour squeeze on a currency pair.

Relying on the squeeze alone without confirmation from volume, price structure, or momentum is a common mistake. False breakouts spike through a band and reverse sharply, trapping traders who jumped in on the squeeze alone. This is why many traders combine the squeeze with other oscillators or wait for the retest before committing capital.

## Practical application across timeframes

Squeezes work on any timeframe—5-minute, daily, weekly. A squeeze on a longer timeframe (weekly, monthly) is often more significant because it reflects an extended period of low volatility and can precede a larger structural move. Short-term squeezes (minute or hour) happen frequently and often resolve quietly. Traders using longer timeframes tend to wait for a squeeze that has lasted several weeks or more and appears near key support or resistance levels.

The Bollinger Band Squeeze is a **volatility** signal, not a price signal. It tells you when the market is quiet and primed to move, but not where or how far. Combining it with trend, momentum, volume, and price structure turns a squeeze observation into a tradable setup.
