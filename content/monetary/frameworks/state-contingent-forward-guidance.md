*The **state-contingent forward guidance** ties a [central bank's](/central-bank/) future policy decisions to explicit economic conditions—thresholds for [inflation](/inflation/), [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/), or output—rather than to calendar dates. It commits the [central bank](/central-bank/) to keep [interest rates](/interest-rate/) accommodative until specific economic milestones are reached, making policy predictable without surrendering flexibility.*

## From calendar to conditions

Early [forward guidance](/forward-guidance/) was often calendar-based: the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) might say it expected rates to remain "low for an extended period" or pledge not to raise rates before a specific date. The challenge with calendar guidance is that it anchors expectations to a date regardless of economic conditions. If a recession arrived three months before the scheduled rate rise, the [central bank](/central-bank/) would face pressure to either break its promise or keep rates lower than conditions warranted, damaging credibility either way.

State-contingent guidance inverts this logic. Instead of promising low rates until June 2025, the [central bank](/central-bank/) commits: "We will keep rates at current levels until [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) falls below 6.5 per cent *and* [inflation](/inflation/) rises above 2 per cent." Now policy automatically adjusts if the economy moves faster or slower than expected. If unemployment crashes sooner, rates rise sooner. If recovery stalls, rates stay low. The [central bank](/central-bank/) honours its commitment without appearing to shift policy inconsistently.

## Anchoring expectations through clarity

The appeal of state-contingent guidance is that it reduces uncertainty about future policy while maintaining consistency with economic fundamentals. Markets and households know that tightening will not occur until stated conditions are met. This anchors inflation expectations around the [central bank's](/central-bank/) target, because agents understand that the [central bank](/central-bank/) will not prematurely squeeze the economy.

During the recovery from the 2008 financial crisis, the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) initially provided calendar-based guidance ("rates low through 2013"), which proved too rigid as recovery dynamics shifted. In 2012, the [Fed](/federal-reserve/) shifted to state-contingent guidance: rates would remain low as long as [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) stayed above 6.5 per cent and inflation did not exceed 2.5 per cent. This transparent rule reduced surprise when conditions approached the thresholds and policy tightening loomed.

The [European Central Bank](/central-bank/) and other major central banks have followed suit, using forward guidance tied to inflation trajectories, GDP growth, or purchasing conditions in [financial markets](/stock-market/). The shift reflects a broad acceptance that state-contingent guidance is more credible and less vulnerable to accusations of policy flip-flopping than calendar-based promises.

## The mechanics and challenges

State-contingent guidance requires the [central bank](/central-bank/) to specify thresholds clearly. How much below 6.5 per cent unemployment triggers liftoff? Is it 6.4 per cent or 6.0 per cent? What if [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) falls rapidly but the [central bank](/central-bank/) believes the labour market slack remains large? The [central bank](/central-bank/) then faces a dilemma: follow the letter of the guidance and raise rates, or invoke new information to justify postponing liftoff and appear to have moved the goalposts.

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) faced precisely this problem in 2014–2015. Unemployment fell to 5.3 per cent—below the stated threshold—yet the [Fed](/federal-reserve/) delayed rate increases because it believed the labour market remained slack and [inflation](/inflation/) remained below target. Critics argued the [Fed](/federal-reserve/) was abandoning its explicit guidance; defenders said new data on participation rates and underemployment warranted a more nuanced reading of slack.

Another challenge is specifying multiple conditions. If the rule is "raise rates when unemployment is below 5.5 per cent *and* inflation is above 2 per cent," what happens if unemployment hits 5.5 per cent but inflation is still 1.8 per cent? Does the [central bank](/central-bank/) raise or wait? Overly complex thresholds confuse expectations; overly simple ones can be gamed or become obsolete if economic relationships shift.

## Flexibility within commitment

Proponents argue that state-contingent guidance preserves the flexibility policymakers need. If a financial crisis erupts mid-trajectory toward liftoff, the [central bank](/central-bank/) can pause tightening because the state of the world has changed materially. The guidance itself said policy would adjust to conditions; extraordinary conditions justify extraordinary responses.

However, this flexibility cuts both ways. A [central bank](/central-bank/) that frequently invokes new information or revised estimates to postpone promised tightening may find its credibility eroding. Markets eventually stop believing the commitment and price in earlier rate increases than the guidance suggests. Conversely, a [central bank](/central-bank/) that rigidly adheres to thresholds despite new evidence becomes mechanical and unresponsive, the opposite problem.

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) tried to thread this needle by explicitly acknowledging that thresholds should be understood as "guideposts" rather than automatic triggers, and that the [Fed](/federal-reserve/) would apply "considerable judgment" in interpreting conditions. This language simultaneously preserved flexibility and attempted to maintain credibility by signalling thoughtful reassessment rather than ad hoc reversals.

## International variations

The [Bank of England](/central-bank/) has used [inflation](/inflation/) forecasts as state contingencies: as long as inflation is forecast to remain below target in the medium term, policy remains accommodative. This approach is forward-looking rather than backwards-looking, allowing pre-emptive adjustment if forecasts shift. The [Reserve Bank of Australia](/central-bank/) has tied guidance to specific GDP and [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) milestones, and then relaxed or tightened them when structural economic changes (such as a drop in the neutral [interest rate](/interest-rate/)) warranted.

The challenge for smaller economies is that state-contingent guidance must account for external shocks and [currency](/currency-risk/) moves that large central banks can partially insulate themselves from. A central bank in a commodity-exporting country might target thresholds that a commodity shock renders obsolete within months, forcing embarrassing reversals.
