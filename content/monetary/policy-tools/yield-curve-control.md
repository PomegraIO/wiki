*Yield curve control (YCC) is a form of [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) in which a [central bank](/central-bank/) explicitly commits to purchasing government [bonds](/bond/) in whatever quantity is necessary to keep [long-term bond yields](/yield-curve/) at or below a target level. It is a promise to be an unlimited buyer at a pre-announced [price](/yield-to-maturity/), effectively pegging the [yield curve](/yield-curve/) and constraining the cost of long-term government borrowing.*

For the broader toolkit of targeting yields through asset purchases, see [Quantitative Easing](/quantitative-easing/).

</div>

## How it works

Under yield curve control, a central bank announces that it will purchase [government bonds](/treasury-bond/) of a specific [maturity](/yield-to-maturity/)—say, ten-year bonds—at a yield target of 0.5% (or whatever level the central bank chooses). This is not a suggestion or forecast; it is an explicit commitment to buy unlimited quantities at that target. If market participants try to drive the [yield](/yield-to-maturity/) above the cap, the central bank steps in and buys, pushing the price up (and yield down) until the target is met again.

The result is a flattening or pinning of that portion of the [yield curve](/yield-curve/). Longer-term borrowing costs become predictable and stable. A government borrowing at ten-year maturities faces a known cost. Corporations and [homeowners](/residential-real-estate/) refinancing [mortgages](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) benefit from locked-in long-term [rates](/interest-rate/).

The central bank is not obligated to buy; it only buys if the market yields pop above the target. This distinction is important—YCC is a tool of influence, not market manipulation, because it harnesses the market's own incentive to avoid losses. If borrowers and lenders know the central bank will cap yields, they have no reason to bid yields higher; the market willingly stays below the cap.

## Why central banks adopt it

Yield curve control is appealing when policy goals require keeping long-term [rates](/interest-rate/) low but [policy rates](/interest-rate/) are already at or near zero. A central bank cannot cut the [Federal Funds rate](/interest-rate/) or the [policy rate](/interest-rate/) below zero without triggering deposit flight and other disruptions. But [long-term rates](/yield-curve/) can move upward on their own, driven by market expectations of future growth or inflation. YCC prevents that upward drift.

YCC is also useful when a central bank wants to protect fiscal sustainability. A government with high [debt](/sovereign-debt/) faces rising borrowing costs if long-term [yields](/yield-to-maturity/) climb. By capping yields, the central bank keeps the government's interest expense predictable and manageable. This can be especially important during crises when investors flee government [bonds](/bond/).

A third motivation is managing expectations. By explicitly targeting a path for the [yield curve](/yield-curve/), the central bank sends a clear signal about its intentions for the next several years. This reduces uncertainty and encourages [investment](/asset-allocation/) and spending based on stable long-term borrowing costs.

## Precedent and adoption

Japan was the first major economy to systematize yield curve control. The Bank of Japan adopted YCC in 2016, targeting the 10-year [yield](/yield-to-maturity/) at around 0% (later adjusted to 0.5%). Faced with persistently low inflation and [interest rates](/interest-rate/) already at zero, the BoJ's leadership wanted to signal that long-term rates would remain anchored. The policy has remained in place, modified occasionally, for nearly a decade.

The [Reserve Bank of Australia](/central-bank/) adopted YCC in 2020 during the COVID-19 crisis, capping 3-year yields. The [Reserve Bank of New Zealand](/central-bank/) followed suit with similar targets. Even the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) arguably conducted a form of YCC during and after the 2008 financial crisis through its "Operation Twist"—though it framed the policy more modestly as [asset purchases](/quantitative-easing/) and duration management rather than explicit yield targeting.

## The purchase commitment

The power of YCC lies in the credibility of the central bank's commitment to purchase. If markets believe the central bank will indeed buy unlimited quantities to defend the target, the policy is self-reinforcing—investors will not bid yields higher because they know losses are assured. But if markets doubt the commitment (perhaps fearing the central bank will abandon YCC if purchases become enormous), the policy fails. Yields will climb despite the announced target, and the central bank's credibility suffers.

This is why central banks deploying YCC typically adjust other policies to reinforce the signal. The [Bank of Japan](/central-bank/) paired YCC with [forward guidance](/forward-guidance/) promising to maintain the policy for an extended period. Central banks also may relax constraints on how many [bonds](/bond/) they can hold, or how much their balance sheet can expand, to prove they can sustain the commitment.

## Tensions with other goals

Yield curve control creates a dilemma for central banks that must also manage inflation. If inflation rises significantly, the real [yield](/yield-to-maturity/) on [bonds](/bond/) (the [yield](/yield-to-maturity/) minus the inflation rate) becomes negative or very low. Investors may demand higher [nominal yields](/yield-to-maturity/) to compensate. If the central bank holds the cap firm, it must purchase large quantities of [bonds](/bond/) to prevent yields from climbing. This expands the balance sheet and injects more [liquidity](/liquidity-risk/) into the system, potentially worsening inflation.

The [Bank of Japan](/central-bank/) has faced this bind repeatedly. As inflation ticked upward in 2022–2023, pressure mounted to raise the YCC cap or abandon the policy altogether. The BoJ initially widened the band within which yields could fluctuate, then gradually raised the cap, signalling a eventual exit. This erosion of the YCC commitment itself became a form of [monetary tightening](/quantitative-tightening-runoff/).

## Distortions and side effects

YCC can distort asset [allocation](/asset-allocation/) and financial markets in several ways. By capping long-term [yields](/yield-to-maturity/), it makes [bonds](/bond/) unusually attractive relative to other assets, driving investors into [equities](/common-stock/) or riskier [assets](/balance-sheet/) to chase returns. This can inflate valuations in [stock markets](/stock-market/) and create [concentration](/concentration-risk/).

It also widens [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) because investors, starved for safe yield on government [bonds](/bond/), demand higher [premiums](/option-premium/) on corporate and private [credit](/credit-rating/). Banks and [financial institutions](/bank-of-america/) may be squeezed if they depend on stable [lending](/interest-rate/) margins.

The policy can also trap the central bank. Once adopted, YCC becomes a cornerstone of financial-market expectations. Withdrawing it—raising the cap or abandoning the target entirely—can be disruptive, sending [yields](/yield-to-maturity/) upward sharply and destabilizing [asset](/balance-sheet/) prices. The central bank may feel locked into the policy even if economic conditions have changed.

## Exit and reversibility

Exiting yield curve control is difficult. The most successful approach is gradual: the central bank slowly raises the target [yield](/yield-to-maturity/) or widens the band around it, signalling in advance that the policy is being phased out. This gives markets time to adjust expectations and reduces shock. The Bank of Japan's slow adjustment of its YCC targets illustrates the approach.

Abrupt exit—suddenly announcing an end to YCC—can cause severe [bond](/bond/) market disruption and sharp [yield](/yield-to-maturity/) spikes, as investors scramble to revalue [long-term rates](/yield-curve/) without the artificial cap. Most central banks, once committed to YCC, find gradual exit more credible and less destabilizing than a clean break.

## Relationship to other policies

Yield curve control often coexists with [quantitative easing](/quantitative-easing/), which expands the balance sheet, and with [credit easing](/credit-easing/), which alters its composition. YCC is primarily a tool for managing the term structure of [interest rates](/interest-rate/), while QE addresses the quantity of [liquidity](/liquidity-risk/). Together, they allow a central bank to pursue multiple goals: anchoring long-term [rate](/interest-rate/) expectations via YCC while adjusting overall monetary conditions via QE.

Some economists view YCC as a step toward [monetary financing](/monetary-policy/) of fiscal deficits—the central bank guarantees cheap funding for the government, blurring the line between [monetary](/monetary-policy/) and [fiscal policy](/monetary-policy/). Others see it as a pragmatic tool for managing [financial stability](/systemic-risk/) when rates are zero and traditional tools are exhausted.
