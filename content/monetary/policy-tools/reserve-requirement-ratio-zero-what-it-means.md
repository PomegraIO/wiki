*In 2020, the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) dropped its reserve requirement ratio to zero. Other major central banks—the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan—have done the same or gone negative in recent years. On the surface, eliminating reserve requirements seems to unleash unlimited lending. In reality, **zero reserve requirements do not mean banks are free to lend without constraint**; they reflect a shift in how central banks manage money supply and bank stability.*

## What Reserve Requirements Traditionally Did

A reserve requirement ratio mandates that commercial banks hold a minimum percentage of customer deposits in reserves—either as cash in their vaults or as deposits at the central bank. The U.S. traditionally required banks to hold between 3% and 10% of deposits in reserve, depending on the size of the bank and the type of deposit.

The economic logic is dual-purpose:

1. **Safety net**: Reserves ensure a bank can meet customer withdrawal demands without fire-selling assets.
2. **Monetary policy tool**: By raising or lowering reserve requirements, the central bank can tighten or loosen the money supply. A lower requirement allows banks to lend more; a higher requirement forces them to hold more idle cash.

For example, if the Federal Reserve lowers the reserve requirement from 10% to 5%, a bank holding $1 billion in deposits can lend out an additional $50 million it was previously required to hold as reserves. That capital flows into the broader economy, increasing the money supply.

## Why Central Banks Moved to Zero

### The pandemic and excess liquidity

In March 2020, the Federal Reserve dropped the reserve requirement to zero. The reasoning was straightforward: the COVID-19 pandemic had triggered financial uncertainty, and the Fed wanted to remove any constraint that might discourage lending. Banks holding $1 billion in deposits no longer had to set aside reserves; all of it could theoretically be loaned.

The move was intended to be emergency-level accommodation, but it has persisted into 2026 and become normalized in major economies.

### Technological change and real-time settlement

Reserve requirements were developed in an era of physical cash and slow settlement. A bank needed reserves to handle unexpected deposit outflows and clearing delays. Modern payment systems—especially real-time gross settlement systems like FEDWIRE—allow banks to transfer funds instantly among themselves and the central bank. The technological rationale for maintaining a safety buffer has weakened.

### Excess reserves already in the system

After quantitative easing (QE) programs, particularly post-2008, the financial system was flooded with reserves. Banks were holding vast excess reserves beyond minimum requirements. The reserve requirement became a non-binding constraint—banks were holding far more than required anyway. Lowering it to zero acknowledged reality rather than changing behavior.

### Shift toward other constraints

Central banks have increasingly relied on other tools to manage lending and bank stability:

- **Capital adequacy ratios**: Banks must maintain minimum ratios of equity to assets (Basel III standards). A bank with strong capital is constrained by equity requirements, not reserve requirements.
- **Liquidity coverage ratios**: Banks must hold enough liquid assets to survive 30 days of stressed outflows. This is more dynamic than a fixed reserve requirement.
- **Open market operations**: The central bank adjusts the money supply and interest rates through buying and selling securities, not through reserve requirement changes.

## What Banks Actually Must Hold Now

Zero reserve requirements do not mean banks hold zero reserves or can lend infinitely. Banks still maintain substantial liquid holdings due to:

### Internal risk management

Banks calculate their own optimal liquidity buffers. A bank holding deposits from millions of customers knows from historical data roughly what percentage will be withdrawn daily. It maintains reserves above this expected level to cover unexpected surges or market stress.

### Regulatory capital requirements

Under Basel III (which the U.S. implemented through [Dodd-Frank Act](/dodd-frank-act/) rules), banks must maintain a minimum **common equity tier 1 (CET1) capital ratio** of 4.5% of risk-weighted assets, plus a capital conservation buffer of 2.5%. Large banks face even stricter requirements. Capital is equity, not cash reserves, but it functions as a loss-absorption cushion. A bank cannot deploy all its equity to make loans; it must retain a floor.

### Liquidity coverage ratio (LCR)

Banks must hold enough high-quality liquid assets (cash and government securities) to survive a 30-day liquidity stress scenario. For a large bank, this is often far more stringent than a reserve requirement ever was. A $1 trillion bank might be required to hold $30–50 billion in instantly accessible liquid assets.

### Net stable funding ratio (NSFR)

Banks must maintain a stable funding profile over a one-year horizon. This requires holding liquid assets and attracting deposits with longer tenures, rather than relying on short-term borrowing. It is a constraint on the liability side as much as the asset side.

### Stress testing and the Fed's annual assessment

The Federal Reserve conducts annual stress tests of large banks, modeling losses under severe recession, stock market crashes, and interest rate shocks. Banks must demonstrate they would retain adequate capital and liquidity. This dynamic test is arguably more rigorous than a fixed reserve requirement.

## The Paradox: More Lending or More Constraint?

Zero reserve requirements seem to invite more lending. But in practice:

- **Banks are capital-constrained, not reserve-constrained.** After 2008, regulators raised capital requirements dramatically. Banks spend more effort managing equity ratios than historical reserve requirements.
- **Market rates matter more than reserve policy.** When the Fed raises the federal funds rate (the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans), banks face a higher cost of funds and become more cautious about lending, regardless of reserve requirements.
- **Competition and profitability drive lending.** A bank lends when it can earn a spread (the difference between what it pays depositors and what it charges borrowers) while staying within capital and liquidity limits. Reserve requirements were often not binding.

The empirical reality: the U.S. money supply has not exploded since the Fed zeroed reserve requirements. Growth in the [M1](https://m1/) money supply (cash and checkable deposits) surged in 2020–2021 due to fiscal stimulus and QE, not reserve requirement changes. Since late 2021, money growth has moderated despite zero requirements, as the Fed raised interest rates and reduced its balance sheet.

## The European Experience

The [European Central Bank](/european-central-bank/) and the Bank of England have pushed reserve requirements even further. The ECB charges banks a negative interest rate (−0.5% in 2023) on reserve balances above a certain threshold, effectively penalizing hoarding of reserves. This is a more aggressive tool than zero requirements.

The logic: if banks are holding reserves "too conservatively," a negative rate incentivizes them to lend or invest. But negative rates have not caused reckless lending; instead, banks have absorbed the cost or passed it to depositors through lower deposit rates.

## The Question of Monetary Transmission

Does zero reserve requirements weaken the Fed's ability to control the money supply?

Not significantly. The Fed's primary tools are now:

1. **Open market operations**: Buying and selling securities to influence the money supply and interest rates.
2. **Quantitative easing**: Expanding the balance sheet to inject liquidity in crises.
3. **Interest on reserves**: The Fed pays interest on reserve balances, attracting banks to hold reserves even without requirements.
4. **Discount window**: The Fed lends directly to banks at a penalty rate, functioning as a safety valve.

Reserve requirements were one among several levers. Their removal has not eliminated the Fed's influence over lending and money growth.
