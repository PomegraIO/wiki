*Targeted Longer-Term Refinancing Operations (TLTRO) are loans issued by the [European Central Bank](/central-bank/) to [commercial banks](/bank-of-america/) at subsidized [rates](/interest-rate/), conditional on the banks meeting targets for lending to non-financial firms and households. The policy harnesses banks as the transmission mechanism for [monetary stimulus](/monetary-policy/), using incentives rather than outright mandates to direct credit to the real economy.*

## The ECB's credit transmission problem

Traditional [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) works by cutting the [policy rate](/interest-rate/), which encourages banks to lend at lower [rates](/interest-rate/) to borrowers. But during the eurozone sovereign [debt crisis](/sovereign-debt/) of 2011–2015 and again during the COVID-19 pandemic, this transmission broke down. Even as the [ECB](/central-bank/) lowered its [policy rate](/interest-rate/) toward zero, many [banks](/bank-of-america/) were reluctant to extend new credit. They faced capital constraints from legacy losses, regulatory pressure to shore up [capital ratios](/capital-adequacy/), and fear that lending in stressed countries would expose them to [default risk](/credit-rating/).

The result was a disconnect: the central bank was trying to stimulate through cheap [funding](/interest-rate/), but [banks](/bank-of-america/) were hoarding liquidity rather than lending it to businesses. Non-financial firms and households in peripheral eurozone countries found credit hard to come by, even as policy [rates](/interest-rate/) fell. TLTRO was designed to break this log-jam by making it in banks' financial interest to lend.

## How TLTRO operates

Under TLTRO, the [ECB](/central-bank/) offers [banks](/bank-of-america/) the ability to borrow from the central bank at a fixed [rate](/interest-rate/) for a period of typically two to four years. The rate is subsidized—often well below the [ECB's policy rate](/interest-rate/) or even negative. But the subsidy comes with strings: the [bank](/bank-of-america/) must achieve a target for net lending to non-financial corporations and households. If the bank meets its lending target, it gets the full subsidy. If it falls short, the [interest rate](/interest-rate/) it pays rises, eventually climbing back to market levels or higher.

For example, under TLTRO-III (launched in 2019), banks borrowing under the facility initially faced a [rate](/interest-rate/) of -0.5% (the ECB paid them to borrow). But achieving the lending targets locked in that favorable rate. Failure to lend enough meant the rate would step up toward a penalty.

The lending target is typically calibrated to a baseline—say, the net lending each [bank](/bank-of-america/) had done in the prior year, or a share of the overall [eurozone](/central-bank/) credit market. This prevents the scheme from forcing indiscriminate lending; it asks each [bank](/bank-of-america/) to grow lending only modestly beyond its recent behaviour.

## Why conditional lending matters

TLTRO differs from unconditional [central bank](/central-bank/) lending in a crucial way: it harnesses incentives rather than force. The [ECB](/central-bank/) cannot legally command [banks](/bank-of-america/) to lend; it can only influence their calculus by changing the cost of funds. TLTRO makes cheap central-bank funding contingent on meeting lending benchmarks, so a [bank](/bank-of-america/) that hoards liquidity instead of lending must pay a higher effective [rate](/interest-rate/). Over a multi-year facility, this penalty compounds and becomes substantial.

From a policy perspective, TLTRO also aims to bypass the distribution problem that other central-bank tools face. [Quantitative easing](/quantitative-easing/) and [yield curve control](/yield-curve-control/) work by affecting asset prices and the cost of capital generally. They rely on households and firms to respond by borrowing and spending. But if [banks](/bank-of-america/) are the bottleneck—if credit is rationed despite low [rates](/interest-rate/)—then pricing signals alone are insufficient. TLTRO addresses credit supply directly by subsidizing [banks](/bank-of-america/) that do the lending.

## The carrot and the stick

TLTRO combines a carrot (cheap borrowing) with a stick (loss of the subsidy). The ECB has periodically adjusted both. During the COVID-19 pandemic, for instance, the [ECB](/central-bank/) lowered the [rate](/interest-rate/) substantially and loosened the lending targets, recognizing that forced lending to zombie firms would be counterproductive. The carrot was made more generous.

Conversely, as [inflation](/inflation/) rose in 2022–2023, the [ECB](/central-bank/) began raising [rates](/interest-rate/) and tightening lending targets, reversing some of the stimulus. Banks were offered less favorable terms, reducing the incentive to borrow and lend heavily.

This flexibility—the ability to adjust both price and conditions—gives TLTRO adaptive qualities that fixed [policy rates](/interest-rate/) lack.

## Controversies and critiques

TLTRO has drawn several criticisms. The first concerns [moral hazard](/loss-aversion/). By providing cheap funding conditional only on meeting lending targets, the facility may encourage [banks](/bank-of-america/) to extend credit to borrowers who do not fully deserve it. Bankers, assured of cheap funding, may lower their [credit](/credit-rating/) standards and lend to marginal firms just to hit the target. This could sow the seeds of later [default](/credit-rating/) and losses.

The [ECB](/central-bank/) has tried to mitigate this by requiring [banks](/bank-of-america/) to report on the quality of new lending, and by stipulating that loans funded under TLTRO must meet the [bank's](/bank-of-america/) normal underwriting standards. But enforcement is imperfect, and regulators may face pressure to wink at looser standards if hitting lending targets is seen as part of the policy's mandate.

A second critique concerns [financial stability](/systemic-risk/). If TLTRO encourages rapid credit growth in an overheating economy, it could amplify booms and busts. Policymakers argue that fiscal authorities and prudential regulators (separate from the central bank) should manage macroeconomic cycles, and that the central bank's job is to ensure credit flows at reasonable cost. But the line between "ensuring credit flows" and "stimulus" is blurred; aggressive TLTRO pricing could overshoot and inflate asset prices.

A third concern is distributional. TLTRO essentially transfers resources (in the form of cheap funding) from the central bank to [banks](/bank-of-america/), which then pass (some of) the benefit to borrowers. But [banks](/bank-of-america/) capture part of the subsidy in the form of wider [lending margins](/interest-rate/). Moreover, the facility targets [bank](/bank-of-america/) lending, so non-[bank](/bank-of-america/) channels of credit (capital markets, [private equity](/private-equity-fund/)) are left out. This can reinforce [banking sector](/bank-of-america/) dominance in credit allocation, with all the concentration and systemic risks that implies.

## Comparison to other tools

TLTRO is operationally distinct from [quantitative easing](/quantitative-easing/), though both are forms of stimulus. QE expands the central bank [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) and lowers long-term [yields](/yield-to-maturity/) broadly. TLTRO expands the central bank [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) too, but it is more targeted—the central bank is explicitly trying to lower the cost of credit to non-financial borrowers through [banks](/bank-of-america/), rather than relying on asset-price effects to do the work.

TLTRO also differs from [credit easing](/credit-easing/), which involves the central bank purchasing [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) or other [credit instruments](/credit-rating/) directly. Under TLTRO, the central bank lends to [banks](/bank-of-america/), which then decide how to allocate the proceeds. Under [credit easing](/credit-easing/), the central bank is the direct purchaser. TLTRO is less interventionist in that sense, delegating the lending decision to [banks](/bank-of-america/).

## Empirical results

Evaluating TLTRO's effectiveness is complicated by the simultaneity of other policies. The [ECB](/central-bank/) has deployed TLTRO alongside [asset purchase programmes](/quantitative-easing/), [forward guidance](/forward-guidance/), and changes to [reserve requirement](/reserve-requirements/) rules. Teasing out TLTRO's contribution alone is difficult.

Available research suggests TLTRO has modestly increased lending in participating [eurozone countries](/central-bank/), particularly to small and medium-sized firms that might otherwise be rationed out of credit markets. However, the effect is often smaller than policymakers hoped. Some [banks](/bank-of-america/) increase lending only modestly to just meet the target; others use TLTRO funding to reduce reliance on deposits or interbank markets rather than to expand new lending.

The design of lending targets—what counts as "new lending" and how strictly the baseline is enforced—also matters significantly. Loose definitions and lenient enforcement can mean TLTRO delivers less stimulus than intended; tight definitions can force lending into unproductive channels.
