*Shadow banking—the network of non-bank intermediaries like [money-market funds](/money-market-fund/), [repurchase agreements](/repurchase-agreement/), and finance companies—creates credit and near-money that bypasses traditional [bank](/broker/) regulation and doesn't appear in standard [M1](/m1/) or [M2](/m1/) measures. This hidden money-creation capacity can amplify [credit cycles](/credit-cycle/), increase [systemic risk](/systemic-risk/), and complicate [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) transmission.*

## Defining Shadow Banking and Near-Money

Shadow banking encompasses all financial intermediation outside formal banking that creates, transfers, or substitutes for [money](/m1/). This includes [money-market funds](/money-market-fund/), investment funds, finance companies, insurance firm investment portfolios, [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/), and the [repo market](/repurchase-agreement/).

The defining trait is credit creation without holding a [bank charter](/federal-deposit-insurance-corporation/). A traditional bank takes deposits and makes loans; loans create new money in the [M2](/m1/) aggregate. A shadow banker (say, a private finance company) borrows from investors via bonds or short-term commercial paper, then lends to businesses. The credit is created, but it doesn't show up cleanly in the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/)'s published [money supply](/m1/) measures.

Near-money is anything highly liquid that functions like money but isn't [central bank](/central-bank/) [reserves](/reserve-requirements/) or physical currency. A [money-market fund](/money-market-fund/) share is near-money: you can redeem it instantly for cash, you earn interest, and many firms treat it as a liquid asset. But it's not counted in [M1](/m1/) (which includes only currency and demand deposits); it's sometimes approximated in [M2](/m1/) or broader aggregates.

This invisibility is the problem. Central banks calibrate [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) using [M1](/m1/), [M2](/m1/), and [credit](/credit-cycle/) aggregates. If the shadow banking system expands dramatically—creating trillions in credit outside those measures—the central bank's policy stance may be tighter or looser than intended, because actual [liquidity](/liquidity-risk/) in the financial system is larger or smaller than the reported numbers suggest.

## How Money-Market Funds Expand Credit

Money-market funds are the largest shadow banking sector, with roughly $5 trillion in assets globally and $3 trillion in the United States.

A money-market fund pools investor money and buys very short-term debt: [Treasury bills](/treasury-bill/), commercial paper (corporate short-term IOUs), and [repurchase agreements](/repurchase-agreement/) (repos). Investors get a share that trades at a stable $1 net asset value (with a small yield), giving them a savings account–like experience backed not by [bank deposits](/federal-deposit-insurance-corporation/) but by a diversified portfolio of short-term assets.

From the fund's perspective, this creates credit demand. When a corporation issues $1 billion in 3-month commercial paper and sells it to a money-market fund, the corporation has raised cash without going to a traditional bank. The fund profits from the difference between the interest rate it earns (say, 5%) and the tiny yield it pays shareholders (say, 4.8%).

In a boom, corporations love this funding: it's cheaper than bank [credit lines](/loan-origination-fees/), there's no [credit committee](/credit-risk/) scrutiny, and they don't have to maintain [credit ratings](/credit-rating/). Money-market funds love the yields. Deposits flow into money-market funds (especially when [bank deposit](/federal-deposit-insurance-corporation/) rates lag Fed rate hikes), and the funds buy more corporate paper. Credit expands.

But there's a fragility: money-market fund shares are redeemable on demand. If investors panic and try to withdraw en masse, the fund must sell assets quickly or ask for a waiver from regulators (which happened in 2008 and March 2020). If the fund's assets are illiquid or depreciate, it can "break the buck"—fall below the $1 net asset value—and spark a run.

When that happens, credit transmission stops. Companies can't roll over their commercial paper, and the funding dries up.

## Repurchase Agreements and the Repo Market

The [repo market](/repurchase-agreement/) is another massive shadow banking channel, with daily volumes exceeding $1 trillion in the United States alone.

A repo is a simultaneous sale and forward purchase. A dealer sells securities (often [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) or [mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/)) to a lender (a money-market fund, a pension, or another bank), with an agreement to repurchase them tomorrow at a slightly higher price. The difference is the financing cost—the "repo rate."

From the lender's perspective, they're lending cash (secured by the securities). From the dealer's perspective, they're borrowing cash by pledging collateral. This is incredibly efficient: a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) manager can borrow cheaply (say, at 0.5%) to fund a [leveraged](/leverage-ratio-forex/) [bond](/bond/) purchase yielding 3%, earning 2.5% on equity.

But again, the system depends on continuous rolling. A dealer must find a lender willing to roll the repo every night. If overnight rates spike (as they did in September 2019) or if lenders suddenly demand more collateral, dealers must find new financing or dump assets.

The repo market is primarily bank-financed (the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) itself participates as a lender), but the non-bank side is enormous and interconnected. A disruption in repo rolls can seize up credit to hedge funds, [private equity](/private-equity-fund/) firms, and securities dealers in hours.

## Interconnection and Systemic Risk

The shadow banking system isn't separate from the formal banking system; it's intertwined.

Banks are major participants in repo markets, money-market fund lenders, and prime brokers to hedge funds. When shadow banking expands in a boom, banks expand alongside it, taking [counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/) (exposure to the creditworthiness of the shadow banker). When a money-market fund face a run, banks may have to absorb deposits or provide emergency lending.

This interconnection is why the 2008 crisis spiraled. Lehman Brothers was a shadow banking powerhouse (repo dealer, [prime broker](/hedge-fund/) to hedge funds, commercial paper dealer). When it failed, the repo market and money-market funds panicked simultaneously. Traditional banks, which had relied on [wholesale funding](/capital-flows/) and short-term [credit lines](/debt-financing/), found those markets freezing.

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) had to step in with extraordinary liquidity facilities to prevent a complete credit collapse.

## Money Supply Measurement and Policy Implications

Standard [M1](/m1/) (currency plus demand deposits) grew only 10–15% from 2000 to 2020, suggesting modest [monetary expansion](/quantitative-easing/). But shadow banking credit grew far faster—roughly 50% over the same period. If you account for near-money (money-market funds, [repo](/repurchase-agreement/) collateral, prime-brokerage credit lines), the effective money supply expansion was much larger than official figures.

This creates a measurement problem for [central banks](/central-bank/). If the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) targets [M2](/m1/) growth at 4% annually but shadow banking is creating an additional 3–4% of credit, actual financial system [liquidity](/liquidity-risk/) is growing at 7–8%, not 4%. Policy is inadvertently looser than intended.

Conversely, during a [credit cycle](/credit-cycle/) tightening (as happened in 2018–2019 and 2022–2023), shadow banking contraction can amplify the policy effect. When money-market funds shrink or [repo](/repurchase-agreement/) rates spike, credit to non-banks falls sharply, potentially overshooting the [central bank](/central-bank/)'s intended tightening.

## Regulation Post–2008

After the 2008 crisis, regulators tightened rules on money-market funds (including disclosure requirements and redemption gates) and expanded [capital requirements](/capital-adequacy/) on banks' shadow-banking exposures. The [Dodd-Frank Act](/dodd-frank-act/) created new oversight.

But full regulation remains elusive. Hedge funds, private equity funds, and finance companies are still lightly regulated compared to banks. They don't hold [central bank](/central-bank/) [deposits](/federal-deposit-insurance-corporation/), don't benefit from [central bank](/central-bank/) [overnight window](/federal-funds-rate/) access, and can be starved of funding in a stress. This creates resilience (they can't over-leverage as much) and fragility (they're vulnerable to sudden redemptions or credit cutoffs).

The balance of consensus is that some shadow banking is healthy (it diversifies credit creation, reduces dependence on banks, and provides competition). But without transparency and [stress testing](/stress-testing/), it becomes a source of hidden leverage and [systemic risk](/systemic-risk/).
