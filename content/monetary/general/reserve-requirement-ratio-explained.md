*The **reserve requirement ratio** is the percentage of customer deposits a bank must hold in reserve—not lend out—at all times. It is the lever that directly limits money creation in a fractional-reserve banking system. When the ratio is 10%, a bank that takes in $100 of deposits can lend out $90, creating a cascade of lending that multiplies the money supply. Raise it to 20%, and lending shrinks. In recent years, several major central banks have slashed it to zero, acknowledging that modern supervision has made the ratio obsolete.*

## How Fractional-Reserve Banking Creates Money

To understand the reserve requirement, you must first grasp fractional-reserve banking.

When a customer deposits $100 at a bank, the bank does not lock it in a vault. It keeps a fraction (say, 10%) and lends out the rest. That $90 loan gets spent and re-deposited elsewhere, where another bank keeps 10% and lends $81. The $81 gets spent and re-deposited, and the cycle repeats. Through successive rounds, the original $100 in deposits becomes $1,000 in total deposits (and loans) across the system—not because money was printed, but because each deposit was partially re-lent.

The multiplier is: **Money multiplier = 1 / Reserve requirement ratio**

At 10% reserve, the multiplier is 10×. At 20%, it's 5×. At 0%, it is infinite (in theory)—though in practice, other constraints (profitability, loan demand, capital requirements) limit lending before the reserve requirement ever becomes the bottleneck.

## Why the Reserve Requirement Exists

The requirement serves two purposes:

**Monetary control:** By changing the reserve ratio, a central bank can expand or contract the money supply. Lowering it from 10% to 5% lets banks lend more of each deposit, multiplying the money supply. Raising it from 10% to 15% forces banks to keep more cash idle, shrinking available credit. This is direct and mechanical.

**Safety and confidence:** Requiring banks to hold a minimum cushion protects depositors if a bank faces unexpected withdrawals. If a bank has lent out 95% of deposits and a sudden wave of withdrawals hits, it will be unable to pay without emergency borrowing or asset sales. The reserve requirement ensures a buffer—though in modern systems, this buffer is small and is backstopped by [central-bank](/central-bank/) liquidity facilities (discount windows, repo markets) and deposit insurance.

## The Reserve Requirement in Practice: A Worked Example

Suppose a central bank sets the reserve requirement at 10% and a bank receives a $1,000 deposit.

- Bank must reserve: 10% × $1,000 = $100
- Bank can lend: $900
- The $900 is spent and re-deposited at another bank
- That bank reserves: 10% × $900 = $90
- That bank lends: $810
- The $810 is spent and re-deposited at a third bank
- And so on.

The original $1,000 eventually supports $10,000 in total deposits. But each successive round is smaller: $1,000, then $900, then $810, then $729, converging on $10,000 in the mathematical limit.

If the central bank had set the ratio to 20% instead:

- Round 1: Reserve $200, lend $800
- Round 2: Reserve $160, lend $640
- Limit: $5,000 in total deposits

Higher reserve requirement → smaller money multiplier → less lending → less money creation.

## Capital Requirements vs. Reserve Requirements

Modern banking is regulated by both—and they are often confused.

**Reserve requirements** specify the fraction of deposits that must be held as cash or highly liquid assets. They are a direct constraint on fractional-reserve lending.

**Capital requirements** specify the fraction of a bank's total assets that must be financed by equity (owner capital) rather than debt (deposits and borrowing). They protect creditors if loan losses materialise. A bank might have a 10% capital ratio, meaning equity funds 10% of assets and borrowing funds 90%.

These are separate. A bank can satisfy capital requirements but still be constrained by reserve requirements, or vice versa. In modern banking, capital requirements are usually the tighter constraint—banks hold far more capital than the legal minimum to signal safety to depositors and to avoid costly funding shortfalls.

## Why Central Banks Have Slashed Reserve Requirements to Zero

Starting in the 2008 financial crisis, major central banks discovered that reserve requirements were inefficient tools.

**Problem 1: Blunt force.** Changing the reserve requirement affects all banks at once and uniformly. It is hard to fine-tune; a 1% move in the ratio affects the entire money multiplier. [Interest-rate policy](/federal-funds-rate/) and [quantitative easing](/quantitative-easing/) are more surgical.

**Problem 2: Opportunity cost is already a brake.** Modern banks manage reserves carefully. Even with a 10% requirement, banks do not lend every last dollar above it. They hold extra reserves to buffer unexpected withdrawals and to manage payment flows. The *voluntary* buffer—driven by prudence and cost-benefit analysis—often exceeds the legal minimum. Lowering a requirement from 10% to 5% may not unleash much new lending if banks choose to hold 8% anyway.

**Problem 3: Substitutes are better.** [Interest-rate policy](/federal-funds-rate/), where the central bank targets short-term rates, is more responsive and easier to adjust. [Open-market operations](/quantitative-easing/) and [quantitative easing](/quantitative-easing/) can be dialled up or down in real time. Modern central banks prefer these.

**Problem 4: Capital, not reserves, is the real constraint.** Basel III capital rules (applied post-2008) imposed strict [capital adequacy](/capital-adequacy/) requirements, making capital the binding constraint for growth. Loosening reserve requirements had little effect.

**The shift:** In 2020, the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) eliminated the reserve requirement entirely (set it to 0%). The [European Central Bank](/european-central-bank/) cut its requirement to 1% and essentially stopped using it as a policy tool. [People's Bank of China](/central-bank/) (the only major central bank still using reserve requirements actively) adjusts them occasionally for policy, but even there, the adjustment is driven more by tradition and political signalling than economic need.

## Reserve Requirements Today: A Tool in Retreat

For most major central banks, the reserve requirement is now a historical relic. Liquidity is managed through:

- **Discount-window lending:** Banks needing cash can borrow from the central bank at a penalty rate.
- **Reverse-repo operations:** The central bank deposits cash overnight and borrows securities, absorbing excess liquidity.
- **Interest on reserves:** The central bank pays interest on reserves banks hold, discouraging excess lending when inflation is high (or paying negative rates to encourage lending during crises).

China remains a partial exception: it uses the reserve requirement (called the reserve requirement ratio, or RRR) as an active policy tool, raising and lowering it to ease or tighten credit. But even there, the effectiveness has faded as other policies have become more important.

## What Reserve Requirement Ratios Still Do Exist

For reference:

- **United States:** 0% (eliminated March 2020, permanently, under the Dodd-Frank Act).
- **Eurozone:** 1% on all deposits with [European Central Bank](/european-central-bank/) members.
- **United Kingdom:** 0% (never had a formal requirement; [Bank of England](/central-bank/) uses other tools).
- **Japan:** 0–1.3%, depending on bank size (rarely adjusted).
- **China:** 11.5% for large banks, lower for others; actively adjusted.
- **India:** 4% (adjusted periodically by [Reserve Bank of India](/central-bank/)).
- **Brazil:** Varies by bank size; adjusted for credit policy.

Countries with emerging or less-developed financial systems often retain higher reserve requirements, because they have fewer alternative tools to control the money supply and because weaker banking supervision makes the reserve buffer more critical.

## The Bottom Line: Reserve Requirements in the Modern Monetary System

The reserve requirement ratio is a straightforward idea: it limits fractional-reserve lending by forcing banks to hold cash. In theory, it is a primary tool for controlling the money supply. In practice, modern central banks have moved away from it, preferring [interest-rate policy](/federal-funds-rate/) and [quantitative easing](/quantitative-easing/).

For students of monetary systems, the reserve requirement remains important conceptually—it explains how fractional-reserve banking multiplies deposits into a larger money supply. For anyone watching central-bank policy today, it is largely irrelevant. Watching what the Fed does with interest rates or bond purchases tells you far more about money growth than watching a reserve ratio that no longer exists.
