*When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) or another [central bank](/central-bank/) adjusts its policy rate, that change does not instantly ripple through the entire economy. Instead, the adjustment propagates through multiple layers of the financial system—first into [interbank lending](/interbank-offered-rate-benchmark/) and wholesale markets, then gradually to retail lending rates (mortgages, auto loans) and deposit yields. The speed and magnitude of this transmission reveal whether [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) is truly effective.*

## The chain of transmission

A [central bank](/central-bank/) directly controls only its own lending rate to banks—the [discount rate](/discount-rate/) or the rate it pays/charges on overnight reserves. When the Federal Reserve raises its target range for the fed funds rate from 2% to 2.5%, it influences banks' behaviour through two mechanisms.

First, banks now have a higher opportunity cost to holding reserves. The Fed offers to pay 2.5% on overnight deposits; a bank will not lend to another bank at 2% if it can earn 2.5% in risk-free Fed deposits. This pushes [interbank lending](/interbank-offered-rate-benchmark/) rates higher immediately.

Second, [mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/) and long-term bonds trade in real-time markets. If the Fed signals a higher [policy rate](/interest-rate/) path, bond traders expect higher [Treasury](/treasury-bill/) yields and adjust prices downward. Banks' bond portfolios fall in value, and the incentive to hold low-yielding older loans diminishes. Banks raise pricing on new originations to match the higher opportunity cost of capital.

## From wholesale rates to retail pricing

[Interbank lending](/interbank-offered-rate-benchmark/) and [Treasury](/treasury-bond/) yields adjust almost instantly. Retail rates lag by weeks or months because banks must manage deposit funding, loan origination pipelines, and customer retention.

**Deposit rates**: Banks might increase the yield on savings accounts and [certificates of deposit](/certificate-of-deposit/) only weeks after a central bank rate rise. But competition varies by region and institution. If a bank faces abundant cheap deposits, it has less urgency to raise rates; if it faces deposit outflows, it must raise rates quickly to retain savers.

**Mortgage rates**: Lenders adjust [fixed-rate mortgage](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) pricing daily based on [mortgage-backed security](/mortgage-backed-security/) yields. A 50-basis-point [policy rate](/interest-rate/) increase may translate to a 40–60 basis-point rise in 30-year mortgage rates within weeks.

**Auto and personal loans**: Banks review pricing less frequently on smaller loan categories. A rate rise may take 4–8 weeks to filter through, and smaller banks may move more slowly than large ones.

**Credit cards and lines of credit**: Many credit cards carry a floating rate pegged to the [prime rate](/prime-rate/) (which banks adjust when the Fed moves). These can respond within days.

## Why transmission is imperfect

Not every central bank rate change produces a proportional change in retail rates. Several frictions matter:

**Bank profitability and net interest margin**: If the [policy rate](/interest-rate/) rises, banks earn more on lending but also must pay higher rates to depositors. The spread between their lending and borrowing costs—their net interest margin—can widen or narrow depending on the [yield curve](/yield-curve/) slope and the mix of fixed vs. floating liabilities. A steep [inversion](/yield-curve/) can actually compress bank margins even as the [policy rate](/interest-rate/) rises.

**Credit risk and spreads**: If a rate rise triggers recession fears, banks may widen [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) on riskier borrowers (less creditworthy households and firms), raising the true cost of credit beyond the [policy rate](/interest-rate/) alone. Conversely, if inflation falls and recession risk recedes, spreads might narrow, partly offsetting a rate cut.

**Deposit stickiness**: Some depositors are rate-insensitive. Retirees with small savings balances may not shop for higher deposit yields; they might leave money at a 0.1% rate even as the [policy rate](/interest-rate/) rises. This lets banks delay raising deposit rates and preserves net interest margin.

**Quantitative expectations**: If the central bank signals future rate cuts, banks may be slower to pass on the full rate increase, expecting to raise rates again soon anyway.

## Evidence on transmission lags

Academic research shows that full transmission from the [policy rate](/interest-rate/) to retail rates typically takes 6–12 months. However, this varies by rate type. Short-duration rates (credit cards, variable-rate loans) respond fastest; long-duration rates (30-year mortgages) respond more slowly and partially because they are also influenced by longer-term inflation expectations, not just the current [policy rate](/interest-rate/).

Asymmetries are common: banks may quickly pass on [rate cuts](/interest-rate/) to borrowers (to remain competitive and attract new loans) but lag on raising rates when the [policy rate](/interest-rate/) rises (to avoid appearing greedy and losing customers). This asymmetry complicates [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) transmission when the central bank is tightening.

## Cross-border and currency considerations

[Central banks](/central-bank/) in different countries set different [policy rates](/interest-rate/), creating [interest rate](/interest-rate/) differentials. High [policy rates](/interest-rate/) in one country attract [capital flows](/capital-flows/) and tend to strengthen that currency. Transmission is further complicated when multinationals and banks operate across borders: a US [policy rate](/interest-rate/) rise affects dollar [interbank lending](/interbank-offered-rate-benchmark/) immediately, but transmission to eurozone firms depends on exchange rate movements and their [funding](/debt-financing/) currency mix.

## Why central banks care about transmission

If transmission is weak or delayed, [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) loses effectiveness. The central bank may need to move rates more aggressively, overshooting the desired inflation target or growth rate. Conversely, if transmission is fast and powerful, small rate moves accomplish the policy goal. Central bank communication and [forward guidance](/forward-guidance/) aim partly to improve transmission by shaping expectations; if markets expect a rate cut coming in six months, banks may lower rates now even before the central bank moves, tightening financial conditions ahead of the decision.
