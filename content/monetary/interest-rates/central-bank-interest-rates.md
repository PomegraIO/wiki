*A **central bank interest rate** is the baseline borrowing cost that a nation's monetary authority sets to influence [inflation](/wiki/inflation/), [employment](/wiki/unemployment-rate/), and economic growth. The most famous is the U.S. [Federal Funds Rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/), which anchors global rates.*

## The Federal Funds Rate—America's baseline

The **Federal Funds Rate** is the [interest rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/) at which [banks](/wiki/federal-reserve-banks/) lend reserve balances to each other overnight. The [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/) does not directly set this rate; instead, it announces a *target range* (e.g., 5.25%–5.50%) and uses [open-market operations](/wiki/open-market-operations/)—buying and selling Treasury securities—to steer the market rate into that band. The Fed Funds Rate is the fulcrum: mortgage rates, credit card rates, auto loans, and corporate [debt](/wiki/debt-equity-swap/) all price off it with spreads. When the Fed raises the Funds Rate by 0.75%, mortgage rates typically climb 0.50–0.75% within weeks as lenders reprice risk and pass through costs.

## Monetary policy transmission: how central banks move the real economy

Central banks can't directly control inflation or employment—they influence them through the [interest-rate channel](/wiki/monetary-policy-transmission/). When the Fed hikes rates:

1. Banks pay more to borrow overnight, raising [cost of capital](/wiki/cost-of-debt/).
2. Companies and households face higher [mortgage](/wiki/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/), car, and credit-card rates.
3. Borrowing becomes expensive; consumption and [capital expenditure](/wiki/capex-budgeting/) fall.
4. [Unemployment](/wiki/unemployment-rate/) rises as firms hire less; [inflation](/wiki/inflation-targeting/) eventually declines.

This lag is long (12–18 months) and variable. A hike in January may not meaningfully slow [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) until September, and by then the Fed has already raised again. This lag creates the boom-bust cycle: central banks tighten too much, triggering recession, then reverse course. The [2020 crisis](/wiki/lehman-brothers-collapse/) saw the Fed slash rates to zero and buy trillions in [Treasury bonds](/wiki/treasury-bond/)—[quantitative easing](/wiki/quantitative-easing-framework/).

## Negative rates and the zero [lower bound](/wiki/zero-lower-bound/)

Most central banks have hit the [zero-lower-bound (ZLB)](/wiki/zero-lower-bound/)—an inability to lower [nominal](/wiki/nominal-gdp/) rates below 0% because cash earns 0% and no one will lend at negative rates (they'd lose money). The European Central Bank and Swiss National Bank ventured into [negative interest rates](/wiki/negative-interest-rates/) (−0.5% to −0.75%), taxing bank deposits to force them to lend rather than hoard. This is controversial: negative rates are unpopular with savers and can trigger perverse behavior (e.g., banks charging retail depositors to hold cash). Japan's [Bank of Japan](/wiki/bank-of-japan/) stayed near ZLB for 20+ years, relying on [QE](/wiki/quantitative-easing/) instead.

## Rate expectations and financial markets

Asset prices move on **expected** future rates, not current rates. If markets believe the Fed will cut rates in six months due to recession, bonds rally *today* (prices rise as yields fall). This is why the Fed's [forward guidance](/wiki/forward-guidance-mechanism/)—explicit signals about future rate paths—is so powerful. A surprise hawkish hint from the Fed chair can crash [stock markets](/wiki/stock-market/) because investors suddenly price in higher rates and lower growth. The [yield curve](/wiki/yield-curve/) inverts when short-term rates exceed long-term rates—a harbinger of recession that historically precedes slowdowns by 12–18 months.

## Unconventional tools: [quantitative easing](/wiki/quantitative-easing-framework/) and [quantitative tightening](/wiki/quantitative-tightening/)

When rates hit zero, central banks deploy [balance-sheet expansion](/wiki/balance-sheet-expansion/). The Fed buys long-term bonds, pushing long-rate yields down and flooding the financial system with [liquidity](/wiki/liquidity-risk/). This is [quantitative easing](/wiki/quantitative-easing-intervention/). The [tapering](/wiki/quantitative-easing-taper/) of QE—slowing purchases—itself moves markets dramatically. The 2013 "taper tantrum" occurred when [Fed Chair Bernanke](/wiki/ben-bernanke/) hinted that [QE](/wiki/quantitative-easing/) might end; Treasury yields spiked 100 basis points. Conversely, [quantitative tightening](/wiki/quantitative-tightening/)—shrinking the balance sheet by letting bonds mature without replacement—was controversial in 2017–2019 because it drains liquidity and can crimp markets. During the 2020 pandemic, the Fed cut to zero, launched unlimited QE, and the S&P 500 rebounded from its March lows.

## Divergence across central banks

Central banks move at different paces, creating currency and asset rotation. The Fed hiked rates aggressively in 2022–2023 to fight [inflation](/wiki/inflation-targeting/); the ECB followed but with lag; the Bank of Japan stayed near zero for ultra-loose policy. This divergence strengthened the USD versus the euro and yen. When the Fed *pauses* (stops hiking) while the ECB continues, USD weakens and European assets outperform. Macro investors make large bets on [interest-rate](/wiki/interest-rate/) differentials and central-bank policy divergence. Hedge funds might short USD and long EUR on the thesis that the Fed will cut first, cashing out when the rate gap collapses.

## [Real vs. nominal rates](/wiki/real-interest-rate/)

The Fed's 5% Funds Rate sounds tight until you account for [inflation](/wiki/inflation-hedging/). If inflation is 4%, the *real* rate (nominal minus expected inflation) is only ~1%—loose by historical standards. This distinction matters because companies and savers respond to real rates. A 5% nominal rate with 6% inflation feels loose (lenders lose purchasing power); a 5% nominal rate with 1% inflation feels tight. During the 1970s stagflation, nominal rates were 10%+ but real rates were *negative* because inflation was 9%+—terrible for savers. The Fed's rate-setting must forecast expected inflation, which is inherently uncertain.
