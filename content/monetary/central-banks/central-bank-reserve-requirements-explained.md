*A **reserve requirement** is the minimum percentage of customer deposits a bank must hold in cash or central bank deposits rather than lend out or invest. Central banks use reserve requirements to influence money supply, support financial stability, and enforce prudential rules—though many advanced economies have moved toward zero or near-zero ratios in recent years.*

## The Basic Logic: Fractional Reserve Banking and Control

Banks operate under **fractional reserve banking**—they keep only a fraction of customer deposits on hand and lend the rest. A reserve requirement formalizes this fraction as a regulatory floor. If the Federal Reserve sets a 10% requirement on transaction deposits, a bank holding $100 million in checkable deposits must keep at least $10 million in reserves (either vault cash or deposits at the Fed) and may lend or invest the remaining $90 million.

The purpose is dual: (1) *Prudential*—ensure banks maintain a safety cushion to meet unexpected withdrawal surges—and (2) *Monetary*—give the central bank a tool to expand or contract the money supply. When a central bank lowers reserve requirements, banks can lend more, which increases the money supply and typically stimulates borrowing and economic activity. Raising requirements has the opposite effect.

## How Reserve Requirements Affect the Money Supply

The relationship works through the **money multiplier**. Every dollar a bank lends enters the economy as an asset (a loan) and a liability (a deposit), which another bank can then use to issue more loans. The exact multiplier depends on reserve requirements: under a 10% reserve ratio, one dollar of central bank reserves can theoretically support up to $10 in total deposits and loans.

Lowering reserve requirements increases the potential money supply by freeing capital banks had been holding as a buffer. During the 2008 financial crisis and again in 2020, the Federal Reserve lowered its reserve requirement to 0% specifically to encourage banks to deploy capital into the real economy. Raising requirements does the reverse—it absorbs deposits and constrains lending—though central banks rarely use this tool because it is economically disruptive and politically visible.

## Reserve Requirement Frameworks: Past and Present

### Traditional percentage-based ratios

Historically, reserve requirements varied by deposit type. The U.S. Fed set different ratios for transaction accounts (checking deposits) versus non-transaction accounts (savings and money market deposits), ranging from 3% to 12% on transactions and 0% on savings, depending on bank size and period. These made mechanical sense but became unwieldy as banking and payment systems evolved.

### The shift to zero

Beginning in the 1990s and accelerating after 2008, most advanced economies—including the U.S., Eurozone, UK, and Japan—lowered reserve requirements toward zero. The reasoning: (1) modern payment and settlement systems make the prudential rationale less binding (large banks are now supervised through capital requirements instead), (2) zero-percent requirements don't prevent lending but do simplify compliance, and (3) reserve requirements became redundant once central banks adopted [interest-on-reserves](/central-bank-interest-on-reserves/) as their primary monetary tool.

In March 2020, the U.S. Federal Reserve eliminated reserve requirements entirely (set them to 0%) to unlock bank lending capacity during the pandemic. Many other central banks followed suit. This does not mean banks no longer hold reserves; instead, capital requirements, liquidity ratios, and supervisor oversight now set the meaningful floors.

## Why Central Banks Adjust Reserve Requirements

Central banks change reserve requirements for two main reasons:

**Monetary transmission:** Lowering requirements injects liquidity into the banking system without a traditional [open market operation](/open-market-operations-how-they-work/). Banks become more willing to lend because they need fewer deposits to meet their reserve floor. This expands credit and money supply. The opposite holds for increases.

**Prudential response:** During financial stress, regulators may temporarily lower requirements to reassure banks and encourage lending. Conversely, if credit growth becomes excessive or inflation risks rise, a requirement increase can act as a macro-prudential brake. However, central banks now typically use macroprudential tools (capital buffers, stress tests, leverage limits) rather than reserve requirements for this purpose, because reserve requirements are blunt and affect all banks identically regardless of systemic importance.

## Reserve Requirements Versus Interest on Reserves

Since the 2008 crisis, central banks have increasingly relied on [interest on reserves](/central-bank-interest-on-reserves/) (IOR/IOER) rather than reserve requirements to control short-term rates. IOR is the rate the central bank pays banks on reserves held overnight, which creates a natural floor for market interest rates.

The logic: if the Fed pays 4.5% on reserves, a bank has little reason to lend to another bank at 4%, so the market rate floors near the IOR level. This tool is more flexible than reserve requirements because the central bank can adjust it daily and it does not force banks to hold a specific quantity—instead, the rate makes holding reserves attractive or unattractive at the margin.

## Measuring and Monitoring Compliance

Reserve ratios are typically measured over a maintenance period—historically two weeks in the U.S., though frameworks differ by country. A bank calculates its average daily reserves and average daily reservable liabilities over this window, then checks that average reserves meet the required percentage. Shortfalls incur a penalty; large breaches can trigger enforcement action.

In real time, most central banks publish weekly data on reserves held by the banking system and the total monetary base. These figures show whether banks are hoarding reserves (often a sign of risk aversion or tight credit conditions) or running lean (suggesting confidence and robust lending).

## Why Zero or Near-Zero Ratios?

Ultimately, reserve requirements became a policy anachronism. Capital requirements under [Basel III](/capital-adequacy/) now impose minimum equity levels and stress-test scenarios that force banks to hold far more financial cushion than any simple reserve ratio would demand. Payment systems are fast and deep enough that banks rarely face sudden, massive withdrawals. And the interest rate mechanism (via IOR and the Fed Funds market) proved more nimble for fine-tuning monetary conditions than blunt reserve ratio changes.

The move to zero did not eliminate banking regulation—quite the opposite. Modern banks face tighter liquidity standards (LCR, NSFR), capital buffers, and stress testing than they did when reserve requirements were formally in place. Reserve requirements were replaced by more sophisticated tools, not deregulation.
