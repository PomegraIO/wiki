*Inflation targeting means a [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) publicly commits to keeping [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) at a specific number — almost always 2% — and is accountable if it misses. This framework has become the global standard for how central banks think about their job. Before inflation targeting, central banks had vague mandates and operated more or less in the dark. With a 2% target, the public and markets can judge whether the [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) is succeeding.*

## Why 2% specifically?

The 2% target seems arbitrary, but it reflects a careful compromise. Zero [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) sounds good — stable prices — but in practice zero [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) is dangerous. When the price level stops rising, it often falls into [deflation](/wiki/deflation/) — falling prices, which creates the terrifying expectation that tomorrow's prices will be even lower. This discourages spending and borrowing, which contracts the economy. A small positive [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) rate (2%) gives the economy buffer room: if there is a deflationary shock, the economy can fall to 0% [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) before hitting [deflation](/wiki/deflation/). Also, [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) at 2% is modest enough that it does not erode savings or purchasing power dangerously, but high enough to keep [deflation](/wiki/deflation/) at bay.

## The first inflation targeters: New Zealand and Canada

New Zealand was the first country to formally adopt inflation targeting, in 1990. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand committed to keeping [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) within a 0–2% range (later adjusted to 1–3%). Canada and Sweden followed. The innovation was bold: instead of the [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) operating with secret objectives, it published a specific number. This transparency forced the [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) to explain its policy publicly and allowed markets and voters to judge whether it was succeeding. The result was striking: [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) in these countries fell sharply and stayed low, and inflation expectations became anchored. Investors and workers began to believe [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) would stay near 2%, so they priced contracts accordingly, making the [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/)'s job easier.

## [Bank of England](/wiki/bank-of-england/) and the model spreads

In 1997, the [Bank of England](/wiki/bank-of-england/) adopted a 2% inflation target as part of the newly granted operational independence. The [European Central Bank](/wiki/european-central-bank/), launched in 1999, adopted an explicit 2% target. Even the [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/) — which had long operated without an explicit numerical target — eventually adopted inflation targeting in 2012, though not before facing criticism for being behind the curve. Today, most advanced-economy central banks use a 2% target or something close to it.

## The benefits: transparency and credibility

Inflation targeting disciplines a [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) by giving it a clear, measurable objective. It prevents politicians from pressuring the [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) to abandon price stability in favor of short-term growth. It allows the public to hold the [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) accountable: if [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) is running at 4%, the [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) must explain why and commit to bringing it back to 2%. This transparency builds credibility. When inflation expectations are anchored at 2%, businesses and workers price contracts with 2% [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) in mind, making actual inflation closer to 2%.

## The criticism: does 2% targeting miss the big picture?

Critics argue that inflation targeting, while useful, is too narrow. It ignores [asset prices](/wiki/asset-allocation/), financial stability, and unemployment. A [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) focused only on keeping [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) at 2% might tolerate a massive stock bubble or a credit binge if current [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) is low. This is what many argue happened in the 2000s: the [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/) kept [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) low with [interest-rate](/wiki/interest-rate/) cuts, but inflated a housing bubble and set the stage for the 2008 collapse. Some economists argue for "flexible inflation targeting" or "price-level targeting" instead: allow [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) to run above 2% temporarily if the economy needs stimulus, as long as the average comes out to 2% over time.

## The [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/) dual mandate: a complication

The [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/), unlike most other central banks, has a dual mandate: price stability (2% [inflation](/wiki/inflation/)) *and* maximum employment. This creates a tension. Sometimes low [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) requires high [interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate/), which destroys jobs. The [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/) must balance the two objectives, which is harder than a single-minded inflation target. After the 2008 crisis, the [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/) kept [interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate/) near zero for seven years, well below what a pure inflation-targeting approach would have called for, because unemployment was high.

## Missing the target: 2015–2019 and 2021–2023

The inflation-targeting framework has not always been smooth. From 2015–2019, [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) in many developed economies ran below the 2% target despite [central banks](/wiki/central-bank/) keeping [interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate/) low. Central bankers were baffled: why was [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) so stubborn? Some blamed globalization and technology suppressing prices. Others blamed shifts in inflation expectations. The result was that [central banks](/wiki/central-bank/) grew cautious about raising rates, fearing they would overshoot and cause a recession.

Then, in 2021–2022, [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) surged to 7–10% across developed economies, and [central banks](/wiki/central-bank/) were blamed for being too slow to tighten [monetary policy](/wiki/monetary-policy/). Suddenly, the threat was missing the target on the upside. This episode revealed that the [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) target is not a magic formula — it depends on good judgment, reliable data, and accurate inflation forecasts, all of which can be wrong.

## Flexible inflation targeting and the future

Some economists now argue for "flexible inflation targeting," where the [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) hits 2% over longer periods (say, five years) rather than every quarter, and adjusts temporarily if financial stability or employment is at risk. Others argue for "price-level targeting," where the [central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) allows [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) to run above 2% temporarily to make up for periods when it was below target, smoothing the price level over time. These refinements reflect the real-world learning that a simple 2% number, while useful, cannot be a straitjacket.
