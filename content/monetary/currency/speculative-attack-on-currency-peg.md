*A **speculative attack on a currency peg** is an assault on a fixed exchange rate in which traders bet that a central bank lacks enough foreign reserves to defend it. When speculators sell the pegged currency in volume, they force the central bank to spend down its buffer; if the buffer runs too low, the bank capitulates and lets the currency float, leaving late-exiting speculators with windfall gains.*

## The mechanics of a speculative attack

When a central bank fixes its currency to another currency (or a basket), it promises to exchange domestic for foreign money at that set rate, no matter which way [market forces](/market-cycle/) push. That promise only holds if the bank has enough foreign currency reserves to supply when residents and foreigners want to convert. A speculative attack tests whether the bank's reserves are genuine or illusory.

Here's the sequence. Suppose a peg is unsustainable: the domestic economy runs persistent deficits, inflation erodes competitiveness, or the government's debt story looks shaky. Speculators—hedge funds, banks, even retail traders—notice. They short the pegged currency in [forward](/forward-contract/) or [spot](/spot-exchange-rate/) markets, or they simply buy foreign currency at the official peg rate from the central bank and sell it elsewhere. If thousands do this simultaneously, the central bank's reserve account drains. Each sale forces the bank to part with precious foreign assets to maintain the peg. If reserves fall below a threshold—perhaps three months of imports, or the level needed to cover short-term [debt](/debt-financing/) obligations—traders sense the endgame. Confidence collapses. More sell-offs follow. The central bank raises [interest rates](/interest-rate/) in desperation, hoping to attract inflows and deter flight, but that only signals panic. Eventually, the bank admits defeat and stops defending. The currency tumbles; traders who shorted it or bought it cheap pocket the spread.

## Why reserves matter: the viability test

A central bank defending a peg is not infinitely resourced. Its foreign reserves—gold, [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/), hard-currency deposits—are the ammunition. If a bank claims reserves of $10 billion but faces $15 billion in potential outflows in a crisis, the math is brutal. Speculators know this. They calculate the "reserve adequacy" ratio: reserves divided by the monetary base, or reserves divided by short-term external debt. When that ratio falls below a critical level—often cited as a few months of imports or a multiple of short-term obligations—the peg becomes a target.

The attack accelerates when speculators realize the central bank's reserve figures may lag reality, or that the bank has already diverted reserves to propping up domestic banks or financing the [government deficit](/budget-deficit/). Information asymmetry works in speculators' favor. They act on fears, not certainties, and their collective action makes the fear real.

## Defense mechanisms: short-term buys and costs

When an attack begins, central banks deploy several tools. The most direct is [intervention](/market-risk/): buy domestic currency with foreign reserves, shrinking supply and signaling resolve. But this depletes the reserve buffer faster, creating a paradox—the defense consumes the very asset needed to sustain it.

Central banks also raise [interest rates](/interest-rate/) sharply, making it costly to hold short positions in the domestic currency or borrow it to sell. Higher rates hurt domestic borrowers and can tip a fragile economy into [recession](/recession/), but the political pain of defending a peg often feels more urgent than the economic cost of a crash. Some banks impose [capital controls](/capital-flows/): they ban or tax outflows, preventing speculators from converting domestic currency to foreign. Controls buy time but distort markets and invite evasion.

Verbal intervention—senior officials declaring the peg "non-negotiable"—sometimes steadies nerves, but only if speculators believe the regime has the reserves and political will to back it up. Once doubt takes root, words are cheap.

## Why pegs crack: the unsustainable arithmetic

A peg can fail even if a central bank has adequate reserves, because the defense is not cost-free. If a country runs a large [current account deficit](/balance-sheet/)—it imports more than it exports—reserves drain every quarter simply by paying for the gap. If the government borrows heavily and prints money to cover the deficit, [inflation](/inflation/) rises and the pegged rate becomes overvalued. Exports become uncompetitive; more citizens want to exit the domestic currency. The arithmetic of deficit finance is relentless. A bank with $20 billion in reserves and $5 billion annual deficits has four years, not forever.

Speculators price this in. They ask: will the government cut spending, raise taxes, or devalue? If a government cannot or will not make hard fiscal choices, the peg is doomed. Speculators act on that certainty. Their attack is not irrational; it is an accelerant that brings forward an inevitable reckoning.

## Threshold effects and the crisis moment

Speculative attacks do not unfold smoothly. There is typically a threshold: reserves remain "adequate" for weeks or months, then suddenly drop below a level that panics markets. The crisis moment—when the last big institutional investors flee and retail traders see the news and panic-sell—is often sudden and concentrated. The central bank raises rates to 50%, 100%, or higher per annum; nothing stops the hemorrhage. Within days or weeks, the peg breaks, often with a sharp devaluation of 20%, 30%, or more.

The speed matters because late actors—those still holding domestic currency when the peg breaks—take catastrophic losses. Those who exited early or shorted the currency profit handsomely. This asymmetry is what fuels the initial speculation. Traders calculate that if the peg fails, a 20% move is plausible; the reward justifies the risk and the [upfront cost](/option-premium/) of the trade.

## Aftermath: contagion and institutional lessons

Once a major peg breaks, speculators often turn their sights on neighboring countries with similar vulnerabilities—low reserves, persistent deficits, an overvalued [fixed-rate](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) regime. Regional [financial crises](/credit-cycle/) can metastasize across Asia, Latin America, or emerging-market blocs within weeks. Central banks in the region raise rates in unison, trying to defend, but the herd instinct is powerful.

Policymakers worldwide now watch [reserve levels](/reserve-requirements/) as early warnings. The International Monetary Fund publishes adequacy metrics. Central banks communicate openly about their forward-looking reserves. And many countries have moved away from hard pegs toward managed floats, which allow gradual adjustment and reduce the binary drama of an attack. But the mechanics remain unchanged: any government that borrows excessively, runs persistent deficits, or lets its currency grow overvalued invites speculative pressure. The attack merely forces the issue to resolution sooner.
