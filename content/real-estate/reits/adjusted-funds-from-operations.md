*Adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) is [FFO](/funds-from-operations/) with a hard edge. Where FFO adds back all depreciation and leaves capital spending abstract, AFFO bites down on the cash reality: it subtracts the recurring maintenance capex a REIT must spend annually just to keep properties competitive, and it reverses straight-line rent accounting to match rent received to rent paid. The result is a number closer to what a REIT can genuinely hand to shareholders as a distribution without impairing the asset base. Most large REITs now report AFFO alongside FFO, and many dividend-focused investors consider AFFO the truer picture of dividend sustainability.*

## FFO leaves a capital-spending blind spot

[Funds from operations](/funds-from-operations/) solved the depreciation-distortion puzzle but sidestepped another reality: running a real estate portfolio requires cash. Roofs leak, HVAC systems fail, parking-lot asphalt cracks, kitchens and bathrooms need updating to remain competitive with peer properties. A REIT might report stellar FFO growth, but if it defers maintenance capex to inflate the numbers, the portfolio decays, rents stagnate, and the next REIT's investor presentations will paint a grimmer picture.

Consider a residential REIT owning 500 apartment complexes with an average age of 12 years. Industry benchmarks suggest annual capex of $800 per unit to keep units lease-ready (painting, appliance replacement, plumbing and electrical upgrades). With 50,000 units, that's $40 million annually—a recurring, non-negotiable cash outflow. FFO does not deduct this. If the REIT reports FFO of $150 million but the true recurring capex is $40 million, the distributable cash is only $110 million, not $150 million. Reporting the full FFO as available for distribution invites a dividend cut when the maintenance backlog inevitably surfaces.

This gap between FFO and actual distributable cash is the gap AFFO fills.

## AFFO deducts the capex REIT operators cannot avoid

Adjusted FFO explicitly subtracts "recurring capital expenditures"—the maintenance and minor-improvement spending required to keep properties stabilised and competitive. The exact definition varies slightly by REIT and sector:

- For residential, recurring capex often includes unit renovations, appliance replacement, and grounds maintenance.
- For office, it includes HVAC/electrical system upgrades, interior finishes, and lobby/lobby-adjacent common-area work.
- For retail, it covers roof and structural work, tenant-space reconfiguration, and parking-lot resurfacing.
- For industrial, it's more modest: loading dock repairs, roof work, and basic system upkeep.

The threshold question is: what counts as "recurring" versus "growth" capex? A $5 million renovation of a single unit is routine maintenance. A $50 million campus-wide renovation to attract a new tenant is strategic investment and arguably should be funded from accretive acquisitions or debt, not subtracted from FFO. Most REITs establish historical spending patterns (often as a percentage of revenues or property value) and use that baseline as the recurring-capex figure.

Not all capex is equal in durability. A parking lot resurface lasts 15 years; an apartment kitchen renovation lasts 20. Sophisticated REIT analysis might look at capex intensity relative to property age and market conditions. Young, thriving properties in hot markets might justify higher capex to capture upside; mature, stable properties in slow-growth markets might warrant lower recurring capex and higher AFFO payout ratios.

## Straight-line rent adjustment narrows the timing gap

The second AFFO adjustment addresses an accounting quirk: straight-line rent. Under GAAP, when a REIT signs a long-term lease with scheduled rent escalations (common in commercial leases), the REIT reports the average contractual rent evenly over the lease term, even if the tenant pays nothing upfront and steps into higher rents in years 5 and 10.

Example: A REIT signs a 10-year lease with no rent in year 1, then $1 million annually in years 2–10. The average annual rent is $900,000. GAAP says the REIT recognises $900,000 in rent income each year (straight-line), smoothing the income profile. But actual cash rent is $0 in year 1, $1 million in years 2–10. By year 1, the income statement shows $900,000 in recognised rent, but the cash register shows $0. This mismatch inflates reported income early in the lease.

When preparing AFFO, many REITs adjust for this timing gap by deducting "straight-line rent reclassifications"—undoing the GAAP smoothing to match recognised income to actual cash rent received. If a REIT recognised $10 million in straight-line rent but actually collected $9.5 million, AFFO subtracts $500,000 to restore the true cash picture.

This adjustment is less universally applied than capex deduction, and it varies by lease mix and market. REITs with heavy lease-commencement activity (new properties signing leases with deferred rent) report larger straight-line adjustments. Mature REITs with stable, long-running leases see smaller adjustments.

## AFFO multiples and payout ratios anchor dividend-security analysis

Once AFFO is calculated, investors can evaluate the sustainability of a REIT's [dividend](/dividend/). The [dividend payout ratio](/dividend-payout-ratio/) (dividend per share divided by AFFO per share) is the key metric. A REIT paying out 65% of AFFO has a healthy cushion; one paying 95% leaves little room for error if FFO declines. In downturns, REITs with low payout ratios can maintain dividends without cutting; those at high ratios must choose between reducing distributions or issuing equity.

Some investors also track an AFFO-based "dividend yield"—the annual dividend per share divided by the stock price. A REIT with a 4% dividend yield and AFFO-to-price near 6–7% (meaning AFFO is growing) may offer attractive total-return potential. A REIT with a 5% yield but AFFO declining year-over-year is a dividend-cut candidate.

AFFO multiples (stock price divided by AFFO per share) tend to be lower than FFO multiples, because AFFO is the more conservative metric. A REIT trading at 10× AFFO is typically considered fairly valued or cheap; one at 13× AFFO is in expensive territory.

## Sector variation in capex intensity makes AFFO comparisons tricky

One AFFO caveat: capex intensity varies wildly by sector, making cross-sector comparisons misleading. A data-centre REIT might spend 5–8% of revenues on recurring capex (to upgrade cooling, power infrastructure, and security systems); a triple-net-lease REIT might spend only 1–2% (because tenants often cover maintenance). If you naively compare AFFO-to-FFO spreads, the data-centre REIT looks far worse, even if both are sustainably funding their dividends.

Savvy investors adjust for this by understanding the capex norms in each REIT's sector and benchmarking a REIT's capex ratio against peers, not against REITs in unrelated asset classes.

## AFFO is now the mainstream dividend-security metric

Over the past decade, AFFO adoption has become nearly universal among large REITs. Most now include AFFO guidance in quarterly earnings calls, investor presentations, and annual reports. This shift reflects maturation: as REIT dividend investors became more sophisticated, they demanded a metric that explicitly accounted for the cash cost of maintaining properties. FFO remains the headline earning metric, but AFFO has become the number that serious dividend-growth investors and credit analysts use to assess distribution safety.

For newer or smaller REITs still ramping operations, AFFO may be negative or highly volatile (because growth capex is still elevated). For mature, stabilised REITs, AFFO growth often mirrors FFO growth, with the capex adjustment accounting for only a small percentage of the total. In either case, transparency about capex intensity and straight-line rent effects is now expected, and investors who ignore AFFO are flying blind on dividend sustainability.
