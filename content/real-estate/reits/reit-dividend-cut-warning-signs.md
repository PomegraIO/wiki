*Understanding **REIT dividend cut warning signs** requires tracking concrete financial and operational metrics—funds from operations payout ratio, tenant occupancy, debt covenant stress—that historically precede distribution reductions.* Dividend cuts are not random; they follow measurable distress.

## The FFO payout ratio: the primary alarm

A [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) pays out cash to shareholders in the form of distributions (the REIT equivalent of dividends). The key metric is the **FFO payout ratio**: the distribution divided by [funds from operations](/funds-from-operations/) (FFO).

FFO is net income plus [depreciation](/depreciation/) (added back because real estate depreciates for tax purposes but often appreciates in market value). It's the closest REIT analogue to [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/).

A REIT distributing at a 60–70% FFO payout ratio is sustainable. One at 85–95% is tight. One above 100%—distributing more cash than it generates—is unsustainable. That REIT is either burning reserves or borrowing to pay the dividend. A cut follows within 12–24 months.

Example: A REIT distributes $1.50 per share annually and reports $2.00 FFO per share, for a 75% payout ratio. It's healthy. But if FFO falls to $1.70 (due to lower rents, vacancy, or writedowns) while the distribution stays at $1.50, the payout ratio jumps to 88%. The REIT can sustain it briefly, but if FFO deteriorates further, a cut is coming.

Watch the *trend*, not just the current ratio. A REIT's payout ratio rising steadily from 65% to 85% to 95% is a warning. A sudden jump—say from 70% to 92% in one quarter—signals a sharp earnings miss and urgent pressure.

## Occupancy decline and lease rollover stress

A property REIT's revenue depends on **occupancy rate** (percentage of rentable space leased) and **rent per square foot**. Falling occupancy is the loudest warning signal.

When occupancy drops from 95% to 90%, the market is signaling tenant demand has weakened. A REIT losing tenants is losing cash flow. If occupancy falls 2–3 percentage points, FFO typically falls 5–8%, because the lost rent drops straight to operating profit (most operating costs are fixed).

Worse, falling occupancy often signals that the REIT must offer concessions to renew or attract leases: free months, tenant improvement allowances, or lower rents. These reduce renewal spreads (the rent lift on new leases). A REIT reporting "negative rent growth" or "flat renewal spreads" is one step away from falling FFO.

Occupancy declines often precede cuts by 2–4 quarters. Watch quarterly earnings reports for this metric. A 1–2 point drop per quarter, sustained, is a red flag.

## Debt covenants and refinancing pressure

REITs are leveraged. Most carry debt with **covenants** tying borrowing capacity to financial metrics: debt-to-EBITDA ratios, interest-coverage ratios, or debt-to-gross-asset ratios.

A REIT approaching a covenant breach has limited room to cut the dividend without triggering default. But if FFO falls enough that the REIT risks breaching covenants, it must choose: cut the dividend to preserve cash, or refinance at higher rates.

Listen to earnings call management commentary for phrases like:

- "Our debt-to-EBITDA remains within covenant thresholds."
- "We expect to have $X million of liquidity after the next refinancing."
- "We're focused on maintaining our credit profile."

These are code for "we're watching covenants closely." If a REIT's debt-to-EBITDA is trending toward the covenant ceiling, a cut is likely if earnings worsen.

## Capital expenditure pressure and deferred maintenance

REITs must maintain and upgrade properties to keep them competitive. **Capital expenditures** (capex) are reported in the cash-flow statement. FFO is often adjusted for maintenance capex to create a truer "distributable earnings" figure.

A REIT facing rising capex—new HVAC systems, roof repairs, facade upgrades—must either fund it from operations or borrow. If capex jumps unexpectedly, FFO available for distributions shrinks. Deferred maintenance (the REIT's properties are aging, and upgrades have been put off) often precedes capex surprises.

Management commentary about "accelerating our capital plan" or "investing in repositioning" is euphemism for "we're spending more to keep pace." If capex is rising while occupancy is falling, the pressure to cut distributions is acute.

## Widening bid-ask spreads on the REIT shares

This is a market signal, not a fundamental metric, but it's real. As uncertainty about a REIT's dividend sustainability grows, trading volume often dries up and the bid-ask spread widens. Institutional shareholders quietly reduce positions. Short interest may rise.

A widening spread doesn't *cause* a cut, but it reflects growing concern among informed traders. Retail holders often miss this signal until the cut is announced.

## Same-store NOI growth stalling or negative

**Same-store net operating income** (SS NOI) is the profit from properties the REIT owned throughout both the prior and current periods (excluding new acquisitions or dispositions). It isolates operational performance from portfolio changes.

A REIT reporting negative SS NOI growth—even modest, like -1% year-over-year—is struggling operationally. If this persists for two or more quarters, FFO pressure will follow.

Positive SS NOI growth masks problems. A REIT with negative SS NOI but reported FFO growth is relying on acquisitions or financial engineering (leverage, share buybacks) to prop up per-share metrics. This is unsustainable and often precedes cuts.

## Elevated capitalization rates on property sales

When a REIT sells properties, the cap rate (net operating income divided by sale price) indicates the market's view of property value. Rising cap rates suggest the REIT is receiving lower prices relative to cash flow. This can signal weakening demand for its property type.

If a REIT's recent asset sales show rising caps (say, 5% to 5.5% to 6%), the market is losing confidence in those assets. The REIT's remaining portfolio is valued the same way by the market, so FFO per share is under pressure.

Management may justify sales as "portfolio optimization," but rising caps on exits are a warning.

## Dividend yield spike relative to peers

This is an inverse indicator. If a REIT's dividend yield suddenly jumps to 8% or 9% while peer yields are 5–6%, the market is pricing in a cut. The dividend is unsustainable; the market is telling you so through the yield.

Don't chase high REIT yields without checking the other metrics on this list. A 7% yield is attractive only if FFO is covering it comfortably and occupancy is stable.

## Merger or "strategic initiatives" announced

When a REIT's management announces a merger, restructuring, or strategic shift, they're often signaling trouble. Mergers are sometimes a way to shore up financial metrics, access cheaper capital, or justify a dividend cut in the context of "integration" or "capital allocation."

Watch for vague language: "realigning our portfolio," "strategic capital deployment," "refreshing our business model." These often precede cuts.

## The REIT dividend is not guaranteed

Unlike corporate dividends, REIT distributions are paid from cash flow and are not legally guaranteed. A REIT can cut its dividend at any time if cash flow deteriorates. The metrics above—payout ratios, occupancy, debt covenants, capex pressure—are the measurable symptoms.

Investors in REITs should monitor these quarterly. A REIT reporting two consecutive quarters of rising payout ratios *and* falling occupancy *and* negative SS NOI growth is at high risk of a cut within the next two quarters. That's the time to reassess the position.
