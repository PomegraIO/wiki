*Interest rates shape REIT valuations through multiple channels: higher rates make debt more expensive, widen the spread between property cap rates and bond yields (depressing valuations), and raise the discount rate investors apply to future [dividend](/dividend/) cash flows. Rising rates typically hurt REIT share prices; falling rates lift them.*

## The Two Valuation Channels: Debt and Equity

REITs are hybrids. They borrow against real estate and distribute most earnings as [dividends](/dividend-distribution/). When interest rates rise, both channels hurt:

**The debt channel**: A REIT with $5 billion in debt refinancing at 4% costs $200 million annually; at 6%, it costs $300 million. That extra $100 million must come from rent growth, cost cuts, or [dividend](/dividend/) reductions. REITs with large floating-rate debt or upcoming refinancing maturities feel this pain immediately. Those locked into fixed rates benefit—short-term—because the economic burden is fixed while inflation erodes the real value of fixed payments.

**The equity channel**: Investors compare REIT dividends to Treasury [yields](/yield-curve/). When a 10-year Treasury jumps from 2% to 4%, a REIT yielding 3% looks less attractive. To compete, the REIT's share price must fall so that the dividend yield rises. This is mechanical—no fundamentals changed, but the cost of capital to investors did.

## Cap Rate Spreads: The Property Valuation Mirror

The most intuitive mechanism is the "cap rate spread." A property's [capitalization rate](/reit-capitalization-rate-explained/) (cap rate) is net operating income divided by property value. When rates rise, property values adjust so the cap rate widens.

Imagine an office building generating $10 million annually in NOI. At a 4% cap rate, it's worth $250 million. But if rising interest rates push office cap rates to 5%, the same $10 million building is worth only $200 million. Cap rates widen relative to Treasury yields because investors demand more return for real estate risk when safer bonds pay more.

Since REITs own portfolios of such properties, their [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/) fall in value on paper. Book value per share declines. Market value of equity follows, especially if the REIT is [mark-to-market](/mark-to-market/) sensitive (e.g., trading near book value).

## Floating-Rate Debt: The Immediate Pressure

REITs funding with variable-rate debt (tied to [SOFR](/sofr/) or other benchmarks) feel rate hikes within months. If a REIT has $2 billion in floating debt and rates jump 200 basis points, annual interest expense rises $40 million instantly. This shrinks funds available for dividends or debt paydown, forcing the REIT to cut distributions or sell assets.

Fixed-rate debt is a shield: locked-in costs are unaffected by rising rates. But REITs must refinance eventually. A REIT that borrowed heavily at 3% in 2020–2021 faced a refinancing wall in 2024–2025 as rates climbed. The repricing shock can be severe.

## Discount Rates and Present Value

Analysts value REITs using [discounted cash flow](/discounted-cash-flow-valuation/) models. The discount rate reflects the investor's required return—typically derived from the risk-free rate (Treasury yield) plus a [risk premium](/market-risk/). When Treasury yields rise, the risk-free rate increases, pushing the entire discount rate up.

A REIT expected to pay $4 per share in dividends annually is worth $100 at a 4% discount rate (4 ÷ 0.04). At 5%, it's worth $80 (4 ÷ 0.05). The dividend itself hasn't changed; only the rate used to value it has. A 100 basis-point rate move translates to roughly 15–20% valuation haircut for many REITs.

## Sector Variation in Rate Sensitivity

REITs don't react uniformly to rates:

- **Apartment & industrial**: Moderate sensitivity. Rents typically reset annually, so operators can raise rates with market conditions. Long lease terms (if fixed) buffer them temporarily, but built-in escalators help when inflation rises.
- **Retail**: Higher sensitivity. Long fixed leases mean tenants' rent is locked; operators can't raise rents quickly. Competing with e-commerce also pressures occupancy, making cap rate spreads wider (higher required return).
- **Data centers**: Lower sensitivity. Growing secular demand for cloud, AI, and edge computing supports valuations even as rates rise. Typically longer contract terms with creditworthy tenants.
- **Office**: High sensitivity post-2020. Hybrid work reduced space demand; REITs with office exposure saw cap rates widen sharply and valuations compress as rates climbed.

## The [Interest Coverage Ratio](/interest-coverage-ratio/) Squeeze

As rates rise, a REIT's ability to cover interest payments (earnings before interest and taxes divided by interest expense) deteriorates. When interest costs jump 50%, and earnings don't grow proportionally, coverage ratios fall. Lenders notice. Refinancing becomes harder, [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) widen (raising borrowing costs further), and [bond](/bond/) prices fall.

A REIT with interest coverage of 4.5x (healthy) that sees interest expense surge 30% might drop to 3.5x coverage (tighter). That signals higher risk and can trigger downgrades.

## Duration and Reinvestment Risk

REITs are often compared to bonds in terms of "duration"—sensitivity to interest-rate moves. A REIT with a long-duration portfolio (long-term leases, inflation-protected rents) behaves somewhat like a long-duration bond: high rate sensitivity. One with short lease maturities and spot-market exposure behaves like a short-duration bond: less sensitivity but more flexibility.

Investors reinvesting [dividend](/dividend/) payments also feel reinvestment risk. When rates are low, dividends reinvested buy fewer new shares; when rates rise, investors can reinvest at higher yields, but the REIT's share price has fallen, partly offsetting the benefit.

## Hedging Rate Risk

Sophisticated REITs use interest-rate [hedges](/derivatives-hedging/) to lock in costs. [Interest rate swaps](/interest-rate-swap/) let them convert floating-rate debt to fixed-rate synthetically. A REIT with 80% of debt hedged faces rate rises on only 20%; one with no hedges feels the full blow. Many REITs disclose hedge ratios in earnings releases—higher percentages signal management's confidence (or concern) about future rates.

Property leases themselves hedge rate risk when they include rent escalators tied to inflation. If a lease has a 2% annual bump, rising inflation (and correlated interest rates) push rents up, partially offsetting cap-rate expansion.
