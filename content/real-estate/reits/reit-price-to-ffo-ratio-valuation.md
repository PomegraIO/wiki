*The **price-to-FFO ratio** is how professional investors value REITs, replacing the standard price-to-earnings multiple used in equity analysis. It divides a REIT's stock price by its funds from operations per share, revealing whether the market price is cheap or expensive relative to the cash the property generates.*

## Why FFO instead of earnings

REITs are required by law to distribute 90% of taxable income to shareholders. But taxable income includes depreciation—a non-cash charge that reduces reported earnings while having no effect on the actual cash the REIT collects from tenants. A REIT might show a $10 million accounting loss but distribute $30 million in dividends because depreciation was the culprit.

Enter funds from operations. FFO starts with net income and adds back depreciation and amortization, erasing the accounting distortion. The result is a number far closer to the cash the REIT actually generates. Professional investors use FFO per share as the true earnings proxy for REITs.

## How to calculate and interpret a price-to-FFO ratio

Take a REIT trading at $50 per share with FFO of $5 per share. The price-to-FFO ratio is 10×. A REIT at $50 with FFO of $3 per share has a 16.7× multiple.

A lower multiple signals the market views the REIT as cheaper—either the sector is out of favor or the REIT has execution risk. A higher multiple suggests growth expectations, a strong competitive moat, or lower perceived risk. Context matters: a tech-heavy office REIT might trade at 8× due to structural headwinds in commercial real estate, while an industrial REIT benefiting from e-commerce could trade at 18×.

Compare the multiple to peers in the same property sector. A shopping mall REIT at 9× and an apartment REIT at 14× should not be directly compared—demand dynamics, financing costs, and lease duration differ sharply. Always benchmark within sector.

## Historical ranges and sector variation

Across property types, price-to-FFO has historically ranged from 8× to 22×, with most averaging 12–16×. Sectors with stable, long-term leases—industrial, apartment, essential retail—tend to command higher multiples because cash flow is predictable. Hospitality and office REITs, with shorter leases or tenant-driven risk, often trade lower.

During periods of rising interest rates, FFO multiples compress industry-wide. Investors demand a higher discount to compensate for higher borrowing costs and tighter lending conditions. Conversely, when rates fall, the same FFO might justify a higher multiple.

## FFO versus AFFO

A refinement called adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) further adjusts FFO for maintenance capital expenditures and straight-line rent adjustments. Some investors prefer AFFO because it captures the cash truly available for distribution after keeping properties in working order. A REIT might show strong FFO growth but weak AFFO growth if maintenance costs are rising.

When researching, check which metric a REIT emphasizes in its investor communications. Some focus on FFO, others highlight AFFO. Always use the same metric when comparing peers.

## Using price-to-FFO in practice

A simple buy-and-hold approach: when a high-quality REIT in a growing sector trades below its historical average multiple, it may present an opportunity. But the ratio is a starting point, not a complete investment thesis. You must also examine:

- **Occupancy rates**: If a REIT reports rising FFO but vacancy is climbing, cash flow may not be sustainable.
- **Debt levels**: A REIT with higher leverage will see FFO growth pressure if refinancing costs rise.
- **Lease expiry and renewal risk**: FFO assumes rents are collected as contracted. A wave of expirations at lower rates erodes future FFO.
- **Geographic or tenant concentration**: A mall REIT dependent on one anchor tenant is riskier than a diversified industrial portfolio.

Professional [real-estate-investment-trust](/real-estate-investment-trust/) managers use FFO multiples alongside [cap-rate](/cap-rate/) analysis on individual properties and [debt-to-ebitda-ratio](/debt-to-ebitda-ratio/) monitoring to build conviction on a position.

## Market cycles and multiple expansion

REITs often trade in sync with interest rates and credit cycles. During credit-cycle expansions, multiples expand—investors are willing to pay 18× FFO because debt is cheap and growth feels assured. During contractions, multiples compress sharply. A REIT trading at 14× might fall to 10× in weeks as rates rise and capital markets tighten, even if underlying FFO hasn't changed.

Sophisticated investors track when multiples are unusually wide or narrow versus history, treating extreme compression as a potential entry and expansion as a warning sign to trim.
