*An **estoppel certificate** is a document signed by a tenant confirming the current state of a lease—its terms, rental payments, deposits held, and any disputes or defects known to the tenant. It protects a buyer or lender by preventing the tenant from later claiming different facts about the lease.*

## How it works in a sale

When you're buying commercial or investment property, you want certainty about the tenants already in place. An estoppel certificate gives you that. You or your attorney requests it from each tenant weeks before closing. The tenant must truthfully confirm: the exact lease terms, all rent paid to date, any deposits held by the landlord, any defaults or disputes, and whether the lease remains in full force and effect.

By signing, the tenant becomes legally prevented—"estopped"—from later claiming the lease said something different or that the landlord owes them money for unaddressed repairs. Once you own the property, you step into the landlord's shoes, and the estoppel certificate protects you from surprise claims.

## Why lenders require it

A [mortgage](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) lender also demands estoppels before funding a [commercial real estate](/commercial-real-estate/) loan. Lenders care because a tenant's hidden claim—that the landlord defaulted on maintenance, for instance—could undermine the property's income stream. If a tenant later refuses to pay rent because repairs were never made, the lender's [cash flow](/cash-conversion-cycle/) assumption crumbles. The estoppel certificate binds the tenant to a stated version of facts, reducing lender risk.

## What gets certified

A standard estoppel covers lease dates and duration, base rent and any percentage rent or escalation clauses, any rent abatements or free periods already taken, security deposits and other deposits held, tenant improvements funded by the landlord, any defaults by either party, and whether all side letters or oral modifications exist in writing. Some certificates also address operating expense passes, whether the lease is assignable, and tenant options to renew or expand.

Savvy tenants sometimes note disputes or disagreements on the certificate—perhaps "landlord has not completed promised repairs" or "rent reduction claimed pending negotiation." Those notations become part of the deal: the buyer takes the lease as-is, knowing of the claim, or renegotiates or backs out before closing.

## Teeth: what estoppel actually prevents

The power of estoppel is that it stops the tenant from contradicting the certificate later. If the tenant later claims the lease rate was lower, that the landlord promised repairs, or that a side deal exists, a court will bar that claim because the tenant already certified otherwise. The doctrine of estoppel prevents a party from undoing or denying a prior statement when another party has relied on it.

This is why tenants sometimes negotiate the language. A tenant might insist the certificate say "Subject to discussions with landlord regarding renewal terms" or "Excluding oral promises regarding rent reduction." Such caveats become part of what the new owner buys, but at least they're transparent.

## Getting tenants to sign

Cooperation is usually required. A lease typically says the tenant must execute estoppels upon reasonable request. If a tenant refuses or delays, the buyer or lender may withhold closing or demand an escrow holdback—cash set aside until the estoppel arrives. Tenants occasionally demand a fee for providing one, though most commercial leases already require it at no cost.

A tenant's deliberate falsification of an estoppel can expose them to fraud liability, so most sign honestly. The real friction comes when tenants want to air grievances—disputes that should be resolved before the sale but may delay closing if the buyer and tenant disagree on the truth.

## Duration and updates

An estoppel is a snapshot valid on a specific date. If closing occurs weeks later, the buyer often requests an updated estoppel, confirming that no new defaults or disputes have arisen. Some transactions require "bring-down" certificates the day before closing.

For [refinancing](/refinancing-risk/), a lender may request estoppels from major tenants to confirm stability. If occupancy drops or key tenants leave, the next refinance may be harder or more expensive because the income stream has shrunk.

## Real-world impact

A commercial buyer who skips estoppels runs the risk of assuming surprise liabilities. Perhaps a tenant claims the landlord promised free rent next year, or that the property has a latent code violation the tenant knew about but never disclosed. An estoppel certificate won't prevent every dispute, but it creates a paper trail and sets a mutual understanding at the moment of sale. In contested situations—especially between unsophisticated buyers and experienced tenants—that clarity can be worth significant legal fees later.

For property owners managing a [commercial real estate](/commercial-real-estate/) portfolio, maintaining organized lease files and promptly responding to estoppel requests protects your ability to sell or refinance quickly. Tenants who cause delay or create uncertainty on estoppels can become liabilities in future transactions.
