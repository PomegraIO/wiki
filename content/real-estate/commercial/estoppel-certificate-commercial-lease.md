*An **estoppel certificate** is a signed statement from a tenant confirming the current terms of its lease, the rent amount, any defaults, and whether it has claims against the landlord—a document that lenders and property buyers demand before closing because it protects them from hidden lease disputes or undisclosed conditions.*

## Why Lenders and Buyers Demand Estoppel Certificates

A commercial lease is a binding contract, but the landlord (or new owner) often has only the signed original. A tenant might claim that the lease was verbally modified, that the landlord agreed to rent abatement, or that side deals exist. These disputes are expensive to resolve and can tank a refinance or sale.

An **estoppel certificate** creates a binding point-in-time record of what both parties agree the lease says. Once signed, the tenant is legally "estopped" (barred) from later claiming the lease terms are different. If the tenant later sues and alleges that the rent was supposed to be lower or that there was an oral modification, the estoppel certificate contradicts the claim. Most courts treat a signed estoppel as binding evidence.

**Lenders** demand estoppels because the lease is crucial to loan valuation. If a tenant claims later that rent is lower than stated, the property's income is overstated and the loan is under-secured. The lender's recourse against the landlord (the borrower) is limited once the loan is made. An estoppel locks in the rent and payment status *before* money is advanced.

**Buyers** demand estoppels because they are about to pay for the property and inherit the lease. A tenant could challenge the lease or claim defaults that the buyer is now responsible for. An estoppel certificate signed at closing memorializes that the tenant accepts the new owner and acknowledges no disputes with the original terms.

## Standard Estoppel Certificate Provisions

Most estoppels are short, 1–2 page forms. They typically ask the tenant to confirm:

- **Lease identification:** The full legal names of landlord and tenant, the property address, and the lease date.
- **Lease term:** The commencement date, expiration date, and any renewal options.
- **Current rent and charges:** Monthly or annual rent, common-area maintenance charges, taxes, insurance, utilities—every recurring charge.
- **Rent payment status:** Whether rent is current or in arrears, and by how much.
- **Deposits and prepaid rent:** The amount of the security deposit held by the landlord and any prepaid rent.
- **Defaults:** Whether the tenant is in default and, if so, the nature and status.
- **Modifications:** Whether the lease has been amended or modified (orally or in writing), and if so, a brief description.
- **Disputes or claims:** Whether the tenant has any claims against the landlord (e.g., for breach, indemnification, or specific performance).
- **Subordination and lender consent:** Confirmation that the tenant is aware of any lender's interest in the property and subordinates its lease to the lender's mortgage.
- **Signature and acknowledgment:** The tenant's authorized officer or manager signs, usually before a notary.

## Tenant Leverage and the Refusal Scenario

A tenant may refuse to sign an estoppel for several reasons: to extract rent concessions, to preserve leverage for upcoming renewal negotiations, or because the certificate contains language the tenant disputes.

If a tenant refuses, the landlord or buyer faces a choice:

**Option 1: Negotiate with the tenant.** The parties can agree on revised estoppel language, conditional rent reductions, or lease amendments. This is slow but avoids breaking the deal.

**Option 2: Allow the transaction to close with estoppel risk.** Lenders and buyers are sometimes forced to close without estoppels if the tenant is uncooperative. They must accept the risk that the tenant will later claim lease disputes. Title insurance does not cover lease disputes; the buyer relies on the original lease document and any estoppels they can get. Some buyers demand a price reduction or a holdback (escrow) to offset this risk.

**Option 3: Demand estoppel as a lease default.** Many commercial leases state that the tenant must sign a reasonable estoppel within a specified time (e.g., 10 days) upon landlord request. If the tenant refuses, the landlord can declare a default and potentially terminate the lease. However, this is nuclear and landlords rarely use it except as a threat to force negotiation.

**Option 4: Proceed with an affidavit or title company letter.** If the tenant is uncooperative but not hostile, the landlord or buyer may obtain a sworn affidavit from the landlord confirming the lease terms, coupled with a letter from the title company acknowledging the risk of missing an estoppel. Lenders dislike this but may accept it if the property is otherwise sound.

## Misrepresentation in Estoppels: Tenant Liability

If a tenant signs an estoppel stating that rent is current when it is actually 30 days in arrears, the tenant has misrepresented a material fact. The landlord can sue for fraud or breach of the estoppel obligation. If the tenant's misrepresentation causes the buyer or lender to suffer a loss, the tenant may be liable for damages.

However, liability is not automatic. Courts typically require:

- A material misstatement of fact (not opinion or prediction).
- Reliance by the party harmed (the lender or buyer must have trusted the estoppel).
- Causation and damages (the misstatement must have caused a financial loss).

A tenant who claims default was cured but the landlord has records showing ongoing arrears may face a fraud claim from the new owner. A tenant who claims no side agreements exist but later demands an enforced side deal may be estopped from asserting it.

That said, most estoppel disputes end in negotiation, not litigation. If a tenant has misrepresented facts and the buyer or lender discovers the error, they typically demand a refund or rent reduction from the landlord, who then turns to the tenant.

## Estoppels in Refinances vs. Acquisitions

**Refinance context:** A lender asks all tenants for updated estoppels. The tenant's signature confirms that the lease is as described in the offering memorandum and loan documents, and that there are no hidden defaults or disputes. If the estoppel reveals a default, the lender may reduce the loan amount or demand cure before closing.

**Acquisition context:** The buyer's attorney usually requests estoppels for all leases as part of due diligence. Tenants sometimes resist if they sense the property is being sold and see it as leverage to negotiate lower renewal rent or extra concessions. Sophisticated tenants may refuse to sign until they negotiate directly with the buyer about lease renewal terms.

## The Estoppel and Subordination Connection

Many estoppels include a **subordination clause**: the tenant acknowledges that the landlord's lender has a mortgage on the property superior to the lease, and that if the landlord defaults on the mortgage, the lender can foreclose and wipe out the lease.

Tenants often resist this if the subordination is unconditional. They may negotiate for a "SNDA" (subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreement), which subordinates the lease to the lender but protects the tenant from being evicted if the lender forecloses, provided the tenant is not in default.

An estoppel signed without a SNDA leaves the tenant vulnerable; subordination without protection is a real risk that could be triggered if the landlord faces financial troubles.

## Estoppel Timing and Escrow

In a property sale, the buyer typically requests estoppels before closing and makes closing contingent on receiving them. Brokers and closing attorneys coordinate with tenants to obtain signatures. If a tenant is slow, the transaction may be delayed.

Some deals close into escrow: the buyer's funds are held pending receipt of estoppels. Once the estoppel is signed and delivered, the funds are released to the seller. This gives the buyer a short window (typically 10–20 days after closing) to confirm that estoppels match expectations and that no new defaults emerge.
