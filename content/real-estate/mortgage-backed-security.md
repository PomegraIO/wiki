*A **mortgage-backed security** (MBS) is a fixed-income security backed by a pool of mortgages. MBS investors receive monthly principal and interest payments from borrowers; securities are issued by government agencies ([Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae), [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac), Ginnie Mae) or private institutions.*

## How MBS work

A mortgage originator (bank, mortgage company) makes mortgages to borrowers. Rather than hold the mortgages, the originator sells them to an institution (Fannie Mae, investment bank) that pools them together and issues securities backed by the pool.

Example:
1. **Bank originates**: 1,000 mortgages, averaging $300,000, averaging 5% rate.
2. **Bank sells mortgages**: To Fannie Mae for $300M.
3. **Fannie Mae pools**: The 1,000 mortgages and issues MBS worth $300M.
4. **Investors buy MBS**: Investors purchase the MBS, which pay monthly principal and interest.
5. **Borrowers pay mortgages**: Borrowers make monthly payments; Fannie Mae collects and passes payments (minus fees) to investors.

Investors receive monthly cash flow (principal + interest) from borrowers, pro-rata to their share of the MBS.

## Agency MBS versus non-agency MBS

**Agency MBS** (agency-mbs): Issued by or backed by Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or Ginnie Mae. The government agency guarantees principal and interest, eliminating credit risk. Yield is lower (closer to Treasury rates) because risk is lower.

**Non-agency MBS** (non-agency-mbs): Issued by private institutions (banks, investment firms), backed by mortgages but without government guarantee. Credit risk exists: if borrowers default, investors lose principal. Yield is higher (2–4% above Treasuries) to compensate for credit risk.

Most MBS investors hold agency MBS because the government guarantee eliminates credit concerns.

## Prepayment risk

The unique challenge of MBS is prepayment risk: when mortgage rates fall, borrowers refinance (pay off old mortgages, take out new ones at lower rates). When they do, MBS investors' principal is returned early.

This is bad for investors: they lose a high-yielding asset and must reinvest the returned principal at lower rates.

Example:
- Investor buys agency MBS yielding 3.5% at par ($100,000 principal).
- Interest rates fall; borrowers refinance; investor gets principal back (loss of future interest income).
- Investor must reinvest at 2.5% yield (lower rate). Annual income drops from $3,500 to $2,500.

Conversely, when rates rise, prepayments slow (refinancing is less attractive). Investors keep the higher-yielding investment longer, which is good. But they face principal decline in value (rising rates push bond prices down).

This asymmetry — investors benefit when rates stay high, suffer when rates fall — makes MBS negatively convex compared to Treasury bonds.

## MBS valuation and duration

MBS prices are driven by:

**Interest rates**: Like all bonds, MBS prices fall when rates rise and rise when rates fall. A 1% rate increase might cause a 2–3% MBS price decline.

**Prepayment expectations**: If investors expect heavy refinancing (rates are falling), MBS duration is shorter (principal returns sooner). If investors expect low prepayment, duration is longer.

**Credit quality** (non-agency MBS): The credit quality of the underlying mortgages affects yield.

MBS yields are typically 0.5–1.5% above comparable Treasury bonds (agency MBS) or 2–4% above Treasuries (non-agency MBS) to compensate for prepayment and credit risk.

## Types of MBS

**Fixed-rate MBS**: Backed by fixed-rate mortgages. Most common.

**ARM MBS**: Backed by adjustable-rate mortgages. Less common; more complex because rates adjust.

**IO MBS** (interest-only): Investors receive interest payments only; principal goes to other investors. Used in CMO structures.

**PO MBS** (principal-only): Investors receive principal payments only; interest goes elsewhere. Zero-coupon characteristics.

## MBS in the portfolio

MBS are held by:

**Mortgage REITs**: Specialize in MBS; leverage them for higher returns.

**Banks and insurance companies**: Hold for yield and to match liabilities.

**Federal Reserve**: Holds for monetary policy; MBS purchases inject liquidity into mortgage markets.

**Individual investors**: Via MBS ETFs or mutual funds (less common to hold directly).

For a conservative investor, agency MBS ETFs offer yield slightly above Treasury bonds with less interest rate risk than stocks.

## MBS during the 2008 crisis

Non-agency MBS were central to the 2008 financial crisis. MBS backed by subprime mortgages (high-default-risk loans) were issued, rated AAA (incorrectly), and sold to banks, pension funds, and insurance companies.

When subprime borrowers defaulted en masse, MBS holders took catastrophic losses. Credit rating agencies were criticized for failed ratings; lenders were criticized for loose underwriting; rating agencies reassessed risk across all MBS.

The crisis highlighted the credit risk in non-agency MBS and the importance of the government guarantee in agency MBS.
