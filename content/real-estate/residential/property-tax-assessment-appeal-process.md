*Homeowners can challenge a property **tax assessment** by filing a formal appeal within a deadline (often 30–60 days), gathering comparable sales and evidence of overvaluation, and presenting their case at a county assessor hearing or appellate board—a process that can reduce assessed value and lower annual [property tax bills](/real-estate-investment-trust/).*

## Why Assessments Become Overvalued

Property tax assessments are created by county assessors or assessment agencies using publicly available sales data, automated valuation models (AVMs), and periodic inspections. Most assessments are defensible, but errors happen:

**Market lag.** Assessments may be based on stale comparable sales. If your neighborhood declined in value (local job losses, school rating drops), but your assessment was based on peak-market comps from two years ago, your assessed value is inflated.

**Data errors.** The assessor's record might state your home has four bedrooms when it has three, or list 2,500 square feet when you have 2,100. These transcription errors directly inflate the assessed value.

**Condition misclassification.** Assessors rate properties on condition: excellent, good, average, fair, poor. If your home was inspected after deferred maintenance or a temporary problem (water damage, roof leak), it might have been downrated unfairly. A "fair" condition rating can reduce assessed value by 10–20%.

**Comparable sale issues.** The assessment model uses arm's-length sales of comparable properties. If the comps selected are much larger, in better condition, or on better lots than your home, the assessed value inflates.

**Automated model errors.** Many jurisdictions now use mass appraisal models or machine-learning AVMs. These can systematically overvalue homes in up-and-coming neighborhoods or undervalue niche properties that the model underweights.

Most homeowners discover overvaluation by accident—after receiving an assessment notice, they check it against recent sales of similar homes in their area and notice a gap.

## Step 1: Gather Comparable Sales

Before filing an appeal, homeowners must assemble evidence that the assessed value exceeds [fair market value](/fair-value/). The strongest evidence is **comparable sales**—recent arm's-length transactions of properties similar to yours.

**Sources for comps:**

- **County assessor's public database**: Most counties publish sales data online, including sale price, property address, sale date, and property characteristics.
- **Online real estate platforms**: Zillow, Redfin, Trulia, and MLS databases show recent sales, including some off-market transfers (though not all; auctions and corporate transfers may not appear).
- **Your local appraiser or real estate agent**: If you hire a professional, they have MLS access and can pull comps faster and with more detail.
- **FOIA requests**: Public records requests to the county can yield detailed sales data, though they may take weeks.

**Selecting comps:** Look for properties that are:

- Within 0.25 miles of your home (or same neighborhood).
- Sold within the last 6–12 months (more recent is better; beyond 12 months weakens the argument).
- Similar in age, square footage (within 10%), bedroom/bathroom count, and condition.
- Arm's-length sales (not foreclosures, fire-sales, or sales to family members, which distort value).

**Typical comp analysis:** If your home is assessed at $500,000 but three recent comparable sales in your area averaged $450,000, you have a prima facie case of overvaluation. The larger the gap and the more recent and relevant the comps, the stronger your appeal.

## Step 2: Obtain Professional Appraisal (Optional but Helpful)

While not required, a professional appraisal can strengthen an appeal significantly. An appraiser hired by you (independent of the lender) will conduct a full valuation using the sales comparison, cost, and income approaches.

**Cost:** $300–$800 for a residential appraisal.

**Benefit:** An appraisal carries professional credibility and shows the assessor that you've done your homework. If the appraisal value is substantially below the assessed value, it becomes hard for the assessor to defend the assessment.

**Limitation:** Appraisals are opinions, not fact. An appraiser's conclusion is defensible but not binding on the assessor, especially if the two sides used different comps.

Many homeowners skip the appraisal and rely on comps alone, saving $300–$500. The appeal will still succeed if the comps are strong.

## Step 3: Document Your Property's Condition

If your home has defects or deferred maintenance, document them thoroughly:

- **Photographs**: Interior and exterior shots showing damage, wear, or needed repairs.
- **Repair estimates**: Get written quotes from contractors for major work (roof repair, foundation issues, HVAC replacement).
- **Inspection reports**: If you've had a home inspection, the report detailing defects is admissible evidence.
- **Condition comparison**: Show how the assessor's condition rating differs from your home's actual state, perhaps by comparing to comps rated in better condition.

## Step 4: File the Appeal Within the Deadline

Every jurisdiction has a **filing deadline**—typically 30–90 days after the assessment notice is mailed. Missing the deadline usually forfeits the right to appeal, so mark it on your calendar.

**Filing location and process:**

- Contact your county **assessor's office** or **board of assessment appeals** (name varies by state).
- Request the appeal form; many jurisdictions now accept online filing.
- Include your property ID (parcel number), a brief explanation of why the assessment is wrong, and copies of your evidence (comps, appraisal, photographs, repair estimates).
- Submit before the deadline, with a copy retained for your records.

**Filing fee:** Most assessor-level appeals are free. If you move to a county board of review or appeal board, there may be a small fee ($100–$300).

## Step 5: Prepare for the Hearing

If the assessor doesn't agree with your appeal, the next step is a formal hearing before a county or state appeals board. You'll have a chance to present your evidence and arguments in person (or, in many jurisdictions, in writing or remotely).

**Preparation:**

- Organize your comps and evidence into a clear narrative. Example: "My home is 2,100 sf, built 1995, in average condition. The three comps below are all similar properties sold recently for an average of $450,000. My assessed value of $500,000 is 11% above the market average."
- Bring originals of your appraisal, comps, and photographs.
- Practice explaining your argument concisely. Board members hear dozens of appeals; clarity matters.
- If you're not comfortable speaking publicly, hire a local tax appeal professional ($500–$2,000) to represent you. Many real estate attorneys and appraisers offer this service.

**What to expect:**

- The assessor will present its case, citing the valuation methodology and comps used.
- You present your case and evidence.
- The board asks questions and deliberates.
- A decision is issued within weeks to months.

## Step 6: Final Appeal (Optional)

If the board denies the appeal, you can usually escalate to state court, but this is expensive and rarely worthwhile. Court appeals cost $1,000–$5,000 in legal fees and require proving the assessor's valuation is not just high but **clearly erroneous** (a high bar). Most homeowners stop at the board level.

## Success Rates and Practical Expectations

Nationwide, about **10–20% of formal appeals** result in a reduction. The success rate is higher if you have:

- Multiple relevant comps showing clear overvaluation (>10% gap).
- A professional appraisal.
- Documentation of defects the assessor missed.
- A simple, compelling story (e.g., "Your database shows 2,200 sf; I have 1,900 sf").

Success rates are lower if:

- Your comps are weak (old, dissimilar, or showing the assessed value is fair).
- The assessor recently updated valuations and used strong data.
- You're appealing a market increase in a hot neighborhood where recent sales support high prices.

**Tax savings:** If your appeal succeeds and assessed value drops 10%, the annual tax savings equal the reduction times your jurisdiction's [tax rate](/tax-bracket-investor/). In a high-tax state (2% effective rate), a $50,000 assessed reduction saves $1,000/year—a meaningful win for a one-time effort.
