*A **hybrid REIT** combines elements of [equity REITs](/equity-reit/) and [mortgage REITs](/mortgage-reit/), holding both physical real estate properties and mortgages or mortgage-backed securities. This strategy aims to capture stability from property ownership while capturing higher yield from mortgage interest.*

## The hybrid strategy

A hybrid REIT takes a portfolio approach, allocating capital across both real estate ownership and mortgage lending. It might, for example, own apartment buildings (generating stable rent) while holding a portfolio of mortgage-backed securities (generating higher current yield and spread income).

The logic is diversification: equity REIT returns depend on property values, rents, and occupancy; mortgage REIT returns depend on interest rate spreads and credit quality. By mixing both, a hybrid REIT aims to reduce dependence on any single return driver.

This also offers flexibility. In a rising-rate environment where mortgage spreads are shrinking, the REIT can lean more heavily on its equity properties. When property prices are peaking and rental growth is slowing, it can increase its mortgage portfolio to capture the higher yields.

## Trade-offs of the hybrid approach

The hybrid structure offers potential benefits but at a cost.

**Advantage**: Reduced concentration risk. A pure equity REIT is vulnerable to a single asset class (office, retail, apartments). A pure mortgage REIT is vulnerable to interest rate volatility. A hybrid REIT hedges across both.

**Disadvantage**: Management complexity and potential for misalignment. Running both an equity and mortgage portfolio requires different expertise. A REIT that excels at buying and managing properties might be mediocre at mortgage investing, or vice versa. Investors also lose clarity on what they are actually owning.

**Disadvantage**: Scale and focus. With capital divided between two distinct businesses, a hybrid REIT may be smaller in each domain than a specialist. Specialist REITs achieve scale, better vendor terms, and operational leverage.

## Allocation decisions

The percentage of assets dedicated to equity versus mortgage is a critical management choice. A REIT might start 70% equity and 30% mortgage, then shift to 50/50 as market conditions change.

In a low-rate environment, property values are high (good for equity holdings) but mortgage spreads are thin (bad for mortgage holdings). A prudent hybrid REIT might reduce its mortgage portfolio and redeploy capital to equities.

Conversely, in a high-rate environment, property values may be depressed but mortgage spreads are attractive. The REIT might buy mortgages.

This dynamic allocation is theoretically powerful but requires disciplined execution and alignment with shareholder interests.

## Regulatory requirements

As with all REITs, a hybrid REIT must meet the standard tests: at least 75% of assets in real estate or mortgages, at least 75% of income from real estate sources, and distribution of 90% of taxable income.

The "real estate" category includes both equity (actual properties) and debt (mortgages and MBS). This regulatory flexibility is what makes the hybrid structure possible.

## Hybrid REITs in practice

Hybrid REITs are relatively rare. Most successful REITs have chosen to be specialists. The largest REITs tend to be large equity REITs (holding thousands of apartment units or warehouses) or pure mortgage REITs (holding billions in MBS).

The hybrid approach requires exceptional management and a clear strategic thesis. When it works — as in the case of a few large hybrid REITs — it can offer diversification and resilience. When it does not, the REIT can get caught between two stools, excelling at neither.
