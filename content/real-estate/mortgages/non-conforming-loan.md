*A **non-conforming loan** is a [mortgage](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) that falls outside the purchase criteria of [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/) and [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/)—the government-sponsored enterprises that buy the majority of mortgages in the secondary market. Without [GSE](/federal-deposit-insurance-corporation/) backing, the lender must hold the loan on its own balance sheet or sell to alternative investors, typically at higher [interest rates](/interest-rate/).*

## What makes a loan non-conforming

A [mortgage](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) is **conforming** if it meets [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/) and [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/) purchase criteria: a loan amount below the annual conforming limit (roughly $766,000 in 2024), a [credit score](/credit-risk/) of at least 620, a maximum [debt-to-income ratio](/debt-to-equity-ratio/) of 43%, a [loan-to-value](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) ratio no higher than 97%, and a primary [residential](/residential-real-estate/) property. Any deviation pushes you into non-conforming territory.

The most common type is a **jumbo mortgage**: a loan for $1 million or more, or in high-cost markets, anything above the conforming threshold. A borrower buying a $900,000 home in rural Montana might get a conforming loan; the same borrower in San Francisco almost certainly needs a jumbo. Because jumbo loans are by definition large and riskier, lenders typically demand a 20% down payment, a 740+ credit score, and ample liquid assets.

Other non-conforming categories include low-credit-score mortgages (550–619 FICO), [investment property](/residential-real-estate/) loans (not primary residences), [self-employed](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) borrowers with volatile income, [ARM loans](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) with longer fixed-rate periods or wider margin bands, and [bank statement mortgages](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) for borrowers who cannot produce standard W-2s but can document income through bank deposits.

## Why GSE standards matter

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were created to standardise mortgages, making them tradeable securities in the [secondary market](/secondary-market/). By adhering to their guidelines, a lender can originate a $400,000 loan and immediately sell it on the [secondary market](/secondary-market/) to investors worldwide, recovering capital to lend to the next borrower. This [securitization](/securitization/) model fuels the conforming mortgage market and keeps rates competitive.

Non-conforming loans, by definition, cannot be sold to [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/) or [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/). The lender either holds the loan in portfolio (keeping it on its balance sheet for 30 years) or sells it to a private investor, hedge fund, or insurance company that buys non-conforming mortgages. These alternative investors do not have a government backstop and charge higher [interest rates](/interest-rate/) to compensate for [credit risk](/credit-risk/). The rate premium is often 0.5–2% depending on the risk profile.

## Portfolio mortgages and bank retention

Some banks—particularly community banks, credit unions, and mortgage banks—keep conforming loans in portfolio instead of selling them. Banks that retain mortgages are willing to hold longer-term [credit risk](/credit-risk/) and can sometimes offer slightly better rates because they do not immediately need to recover origination capital. A large bank with billions in assets might hold billions in mortgages, creating a diversified portfolio.

These portfolio lenders sometimes have looser underwriting standards than [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/) because they own the risk. You might qualify for a portfolio mortgage with a 580 credit score or a 45% [debt-to-income ratio](/debt-to-equity-ratio/) when you would be rejected for a conforming loan. The tradeoff is that portfolio mortgages usually carry a rate premium, and the lender may require a larger down payment or more documentation to offset the added risk.

## The secondary market for non-conforming mortgages

Private investors increasingly buy non-conforming mortgages, either as whole loans or as bundled mortgage-backed securities. Some investors specialise in jumbo mortgages from creditworthy borrowers; others hunt for yield in riskier segments: borrowers with credit scores below 620, high [debt-to-income ratios](/debt-to-equity-ratio/), or [investment properties](/residential-real-estate/). These investors typically demand higher [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and stricter underwriting, but they are willing to fund loans that traditional lenders will not touch.

Hedge funds, private equity firms, and debt funds have entered the non-conforming market, especially after the 2008 financial crisis taught banks to be cautious with [credit risk](/credit-risk/). This private capital has made non-conforming mortgages more accessible, though at a cost. A [self-employed](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) borrower might borrow at 8% on a non-conforming [bank statement mortgage](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) when a conforming borrower pays 6%. That 2% spread reflects the lender's perception of risk.

## Underwriting and documentation

Non-conforming underwriting is more stringent than conforming, not less. A jumbo lender typically demands a 740+ credit score (versus 620 conforming minimum), a 20–25% down payment (versus 3–5% for [FHA](/federal-deposit-insurance-corporation/) borrowers), and documented liquid assets equal to 6–12 months of payments. A [bank statement mortgage](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) lender may ask for 24 months of bank statements and a CPA letter certifying self-employment income.

The underwriting timeline for non-conforming loans is also longer. Conforming loans can often be approved in 3–5 days via automated underwriting; non-conforming loans often require manual review, property appraisal, title insurance, and sometimes an environmental survey. Approval can take 2–3 weeks.

## Strategic considerations

Borrowers sometimes have a choice between a jumbo mortgage and a [piggyback loan](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/): a conforming first [mortgage](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) plus a smaller second [mortgage](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) to cover the gap. If you are buying a $900,000 home, you could take a $720,000 conforming loan (80% LTV) and a $180,000 second mortgage (20% LTV). The first loan gets a conforming rate; the second is often a [HELOC](/fixed-rate-mortgage-personal/) or portfolio loan at a higher rate. The blended cost may be lower than a single jumbo, though the two payments are separate.

Non-conforming loans are also more sensitive to credit cycles. When credit tightens after a recession, jumbo lenders often raise rates sharply or tighten underwriting. Conforming mortgages, backed implicitly by the government, are more resilient. A jumbo borrower in a weak market may face a 3% rate increase; a conforming borrower might see only 0.5%.
