*Real estate **due diligence** is the systematic investigation of a property's physical condition, financial performance, legal standing, and market position before closing. Skipping or shortcutting due diligence is the leading cause of post-purchase regret, cost overruns, and investment underperformance.*

## The Four Domains of Due Diligence

### Physical and Structural Inspection

Before committing capital, hire a professional inspector to examine every major system: roof, foundation, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, windows, doors, structural integrity, water intrusion, mold, pest damage, and accessibility.

Get a detailed written report. If the property is multi-unit, inspect a sample of units if not all. Ask about age of roof and systems, expected replacement costs, and maintenance backlog. A 25-year-old roof in a 30-year climate zone may need replacement within 2–5 years—a six-figure cost.

For income properties, request the maintenance log from the current owner. Does it show deferred maintenance? Are repairs piecemeal or proactive? A property with minimal documented maintenance history is a red flag; it suggests ignored problems or poor record-keeping.

**Common surprises found in inspection:**
- Outdated or failing electrical systems (knob-and-tube wiring, insufficient capacity)
- Plumbing issues (lead pipes, corroded cast iron, inadequate water pressure)
- Roof leaks, missing flashing, structural rot
- Foundation cracks, settling, moisture intrusion
- Asbestos, lead paint (in older properties)
- HVAC systems at end of life

Budget for inspection costs. A thorough commercial or multi-unit inspection costs $500–$2,000 depending on property size and complexity. It's the cheapest insurance you'll buy.

### Environmental and Health Assessment

For commercial or industrial properties, environmental contamination is a catastrophic risk. Prior uses matter: gas stations, dry cleaners, manufacturing, and auto repair shops all leave lingering toxins. Hire a Phase 1 Environmental Site Assessment (ESA). It involves document review and a site walkover to identify potential contamination. If Phase 1 flags concerns, a Phase 2 (soil and groundwater testing) follows.

Environmental liability can exceed the property's value. A contaminated site may require $100,000–$1,000,000+ to remediate, depending on severity. Lenders will not finance a contaminated property until it's cleaned up. Make environmental due diligence non-negotiable for any commercial property or property with industrial history.

Also check for lead paint (pre-1978 residential), radon, mold, and any health department violations or complaints against the property.

### Financial Performance Review

Request the last 3 years of **actual financial statements**: rent rolls, lease agreements, expense bills (utilities, insurance, maintenance, property taxes, HOA fees), and any reports filed with a lender or accountant.

Don't trust the seller's pro forma projections. Look only at historical data. Ask:

- Are rents current? (Compare to market.)
- Is the rent roll accurate? (Verify tenants are real, leases are valid, and occupancy is as stated.)
- What is the **actual occupancy rate**? (50% occupancy vs. 95% massively changes cash flow.)
- Are there below-market leases that won't reset soon?
- What are **gross operating expenses**? (Property taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities, trash, landscaping, management.)
- Is there a capital improvement backlog?
- Are there below-market rent leases or long-term tenants?

Calculate **Net Operating Income (NOI):** Gross Rental Income − Operating Expenses = NOI.

If the property generates $100,000 per year in rent and has $30,000 in annual expenses, NOI is $70,000.

From NOI, you can calculate the **cap rate:** NOI ÷ Purchase Price. If you buy for $1,000,000 and NOI is $70,000, the cap rate is 7%. (Higher cap rate = higher initial yield, but often reflects higher risk or lower growth potential.)

Watch for red flags:
- Owner-occupied expense allocations (the seller's mortgage interest is not an actual operating expense).
- One tenant paying most of the rent (tenant concentration risk).
- Rents significantly below market (you won't raise them immediately).
- Large, undocumented cash payments (unreported income, or worse, fictitious revenue).
- Deferred maintenance disguised as low expenses (a property kept cheap by neglect).

### Legal and Title Review

Hire a real estate attorney to perform a thorough title search. You want to confirm:

- **Clear title:** No liens, judgments, or claims against the property.
- **Deed restrictions:** Covenants that limit use, setbacks, or improvements. Some restrict commercial use, pets, or property alterations.
- **Easements:** Rights granted to neighbors or utilities (e.g., power line easements, water access rights). They may affect property value or future development.
- **Zoning compliance:** Is the property legally zoned for its current use? If it's a commercial rental in a residential zone, you may face nuisance complaints or zoning violations.
- **Encroachments:** Does the property encroach on neighbors' land, or vice versa? Does a neighbor's fence actually sit on your lot?
- **Liens and judgments:** Tax liens, contractor liens, or judgments against the owner can cloud the title.
- **Association restrictions:** For condos or properties in HOAs, review governing documents, rules, assessments, and any special assessments planned.

A title insurance policy protects you against historical title defects (e.g., a forged deed from 1985 suddenly resurfaces). It does not protect against current zoning violations or easements already in the record.

### Market and Exit Analysis

Real estate is illiquid. Before closing, understand the market: Can you sell or refinance when you want to? What are exit costs?

- **Comparable sales (comps):** What have similar properties sold for in the last 90 days? A property appraising $800,000 but offering 8% cap rate in a market where comps show 6% cap rate may be overpriced or over-leveraged.
- **Rent comps:** What are comparable units renting for? If market rents are $1,200/month and you're locked into 5% below that, your revenue is capped until the lease resets.
- **Vacancy trends:** What is average vacancy in the neighborhood? If it's 25%, assume you'll face extended vacancies. Budget accordingly.
- **Neighborhood trends:** Is the area growing or declining? Are major employers moving in or out? Is crime rising or falling?
- **Interest rate environment:** If rates are rising and you need to refinance in 3 years, your new rate could be 1–2% higher, materially reducing cash flow.
- **Supply pipeline:** Are new competing properties being built? Will supply flood the market and suppress rents?

Use comps and rent data to stress-test your projections. If your investment relies on 5% annual rent growth but the market is flat, that's a bet, not a fact.

### Tenant Quality and Lease Review

For multi-tenant properties, review **every lease**. Don't sample—read all of them. Check:

- **Lease terms:** Rent amount, renewal date, escalation clauses (automatic rent increases), tenant responsibilities.
- **Renewals pending:** If 50% of rent revenue renews in the next 6 months, you have significant rent-reset risk. Plan for tenant turnover costs.
- **Tenant creditworthiness:** Are tenants creditworthy? Have they paid consistently? Credit reports and payment history matter.
- **Lease guarantees:** Are personal guarantees in place? (They help if a tenant defaults.)
- **Lease strength:** Are you bound into low rents for years, or do you have flexibility to market up at renewal?

One tenant paying 40% of rent is a concentration risk. If that tenant vacates, your cash flow drops 40% immediately. Understand the risk.

### Appraisal and Valuation Alignment

Your lender will order an independent appraisal. The appraiser's estimate should be close to your purchase price (within 5%). If the appraisal comes in significantly lower than your offer, your lender will not loan the full amount. You must either renegotiate the price down or bring more cash to closing.

If your analysis suggests the property is worth $800,000 but the appraisal says $750,000, that's a warning. Don't ignore it. The market may be telling you the property is overpriced.

### Environmental, Zoning, and Code Compliance

Beyond title, investigate:

- **Building code violations:** Any previous violations? Have they been remediated? Open violations can force you to fund corrections.
- **Zoning compliance:** Is the property legally operating in its zone? A short-term rental in a residential zone may violate local ordinances.
- **Permits:** Were major improvements (roof, HVAC, electrical upgrades) permitted and inspected? Unpermitted work can be a lender concern and may require remediation.
- **ADA compliance:** If the property is commercial, is it accessible? Failing accessibility standards can trigger lawsuits.
- **Insurance availability:** Can you obtain hazard insurance at a reasonable cost? Flood-prone or high-crime areas may face premium surcharges or denial.

### The Due Diligence Timeline

Most purchase contracts include a "contingency period," typically 10–45 days, during which you inspect, review, and investigate. Use that time fully. Don't waive contingencies early just to look serious. The contingency period is your protection.

- **Days 1–3:** Order inspection and appraisal.
- **Days 5–10:** Physical inspection occurs; preliminary title search ordered.
- **Days 10–15:** Review financial documents, rent rolls, leases, tax returns.
- **Days 15–25:** Final appraisal results; cap rate and financial analysis finalized.
- **Days 25–30:** Attorney review of title and legal documents; environmental ESA (if commercial).
- **Days 30–45:** Final walkthrough, address any last-minute concerns, prepare closing.

If anything material fails inspection or appraisal, your contingencies allow you to renegotiate, request repairs, or cancel without losing earnest money.

### Red Flags That Should Stop a Deal

- Deferred maintenance exceeding 10% of property value.
- Cap rate materially lower than market comps (overpriced).
- Tenant concentration (one tenant over 30% of rent).
- Significant vacant units without clear reason.
- Environmental contamination or unresolved liens.
- Zoning violations or code citations.
- Lease terms locking you into below-market rents for years.
- Appraisal significantly below your offer (suggests overvaluation).
- Lender's valuation and your valuation diverge sharply.

These don't automatically kill a deal, but they demand explanation and price adjustment.
