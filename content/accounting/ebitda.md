*[EBITDA](/ebitda) stands for **Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization**. It is a widely used metric that starts with net income and adds back the four items to isolate operating profitability. [EBITDA](/ebitda) strips out the effects of capital structure (interest), tax jurisdiction (taxes), and accounting choices ([depreciation](/depreciation) and [amortization](/amortization)). This makes it useful for comparing companies with different leverage, tax positions, or asset bases. However, [EBITDA](/ebitda) is not a [GAAP](/generally-accepted-accounting-principles) measure, and it can obscure real economic differences. Companies often disclose [EBITDA](/ebitda) as a non-gaap-measure to support valuations.*

## Calculation of EBITDA

[EBITDA](/ebitda) starts with net income and adds back four items:

Net Income
+ Interest expense (and interest income, net)
+ Income tax expense
+ Depreciation
+ Amortization
= EBITDA

Example: A company reports:
- Net income: $100 million
- Interest expense: $20 million
- Income tax: $30 million
- Depreciation: $50 million
- Amortization: $10 million
- EBITDA = $100 + $20 + $30 + $50 + $10 = $210 million

## Why EBITDA is useful

**Comparability across leverage**: Two identical companies differ only in financing. One is all-equity (no interest); one is leveraged (high interest). Net income differs due to financing, not operations. [EBITDA](/ebitda) strips out the interest difference, making them comparable.

**Comparability across tax jurisdictions**: A company in a high-tax country appears less profitable than one in a low-tax country, even if operations are identical. [EBITDA](/ebitda) eliminates the tax difference.

**Comparability across accounting methods**: One company uses [straight-line-depreciation](/straight-line-depreciation) over 10 years; another over 20 years. [EBITDA](/ebitda) removes the depreciation difference.

**Proxy for cash generation**: [EBITDA](/ebitda) is roughly a proxy for operating cash flow. While not identical (accrual adjustments, capex needs), [EBITDA](/ebitda) is often used as a first pass at estimating cash.

## EBITDA multiples and valuation

[EBITDA](/ebitda) is central to valuation multiples. Companies are often valued as:

Enterprise Value = [EBITDA](/ebitda) × Multiple

Example: If a company has [EBITDA](/ebitda) of $100 million and the industry [EBITDA](/ebitda) multiple is 10×, the company is worth $1 billion.

[EBITDA](/ebitda) multiples vary by industry (mature industries trade at lower multiples; high-growth at higher). This is why [EBITDA](/ebitda) is central to M&A pricing.

## Limitations of EBITDA

**Ignores capex**: [EBITDA](/ebitda) does not account for capital expenditures. A capital-intensive company with high [EBITDA](/ebitda) may have low [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow) after capex. [Free-cash-flow](/free-cash-flow) is a better metric for cash generation.

**Ignores working capital**: [EBITDA](/ebitda) does not account for changes in working capital. A growing company may have negative operating cash flow despite high [EBITDA](/ebitda).

**Can be manipulated**: Companies adjust [EBITDA](/ebitda) for various items to make it look better. Some add back stock-based compensation, restructuring costs, and other "one-time" items, creating "adjusted EBITDA" that may bear little resemblance to reality.

**Not a measure of profitability**: [EBITDA](/ebitda) ignores interest and taxes, which are real costs. A company with high [EBITDA](/ebitda) but high interest expense may not be profitable.

## EBITDA vs. Operating Income

[EBITDA](/ebitda) is different from operating-income (EBIT):

EBIT = Net income + Interest + Taxes (no D&A adjustment)

EBIT includes the effect of [depreciation](/depreciation) and [amortization](/amortization), which are real, non-cash costs related to operations.

[EBITDA](/ebitda) excludes them, making [EBITDA](/ebitda) more aggressive (higher) than EBIT in most cases.

## Non-GAAP disclosure

Because [EBITDA](/ebitda) is not a [GAAP](/generally-accepted-accounting-principles) measure, companies must reconcile it to net income in footnotes. The SEC requires companies to disclose adjustments and explain why [EBITDA](/ebitda) is relevant.

Companies often disclose adjusted-EBITDA, which includes further adjustments (stock-based comp, restructuring, etc.). Investors must scrutinize these adjustments.
