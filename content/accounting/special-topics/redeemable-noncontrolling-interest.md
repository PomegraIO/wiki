*A **redeemable noncontrolling interest** is a minority ownership stake held by a non-parent investor that carries a put or call option—typically allowing that investor to force the parent to buy the stake, or giving the parent the right to buy it. Because redemption is probable and at the investor's option, the stake sits between permanent [preferred stock](/preferred-stock/) and liabilities, classified as temporary equity rather than as part of [stockholders' equity](/common-stock/).*

## Why noncontrolling interests need special treatment

When a parent company owns (say) 80% of a subsidiary, the remaining 20% is a noncontrolling interest—usually a straightforward equity line item on the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/). The parent consolidates the entire subsidiary; the noncontrolling interest reflects outsiders' claim on the subsidiary's net assets.

But that 20% stake often comes with a twist: a put option (the minority investor can force a buyout) or a call option (the parent can force a sale to itself). These options fundamentally change the nature of the investment. A put option is economically closer to a short-term payable than to permanent equity. It represents a probable cash outflow, contingent only on an event that the minority holder controls. GAAP reflects this economic reality by reclassifying the stake as temporary equity—also called mezzanine equity—a halfway house between liabilities and stockholders' equity.

## The mechanics: put options and forced redemption

The most common structure is a minority stake with an embedded put. An investor owns 15% of a mid-sized insurance broker, and the founder-owners hold the other 85%. The minority investor's purchase agreement includes a put option: anytime after year three, the investor can force the founders to buy out the stake at a formula price (typically based on [EBITDA](/ebitda/) multiples or book value).

From the issuer's (parent's) perspective, this is a contingent obligation. The parent has not paid out cash yet, but the probability of eventual payment is high—the minority holder retains the option indefinitely, or until a specified deadline. If redemption is probable and within a defined window (or at the holder's discretion at any time), the stake must be classified outside permanent stockholders' equity.

Similarly, a call option—where the parent can force the minority to sell—also triggers redeemable classification if the parent intends or is obligated to repurchase. The criterion is whether **the event triggering redemption is within the parent's control, the minority holder's control, or neither**. If the minority holder can force redemption, it is redeemable. If only the parent can, the standard is higher—intent and obligation must be clear. If neither party has unilateral control (e.g., redemption only upon the subsidiary's IPO), classification depends on probability assessment.

## Balance sheet location: temporary equity

Redeemable noncontrolling interests appear in a separate section of the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/), between liabilities and permanent stockholders' equity. This layout communicates three truths:
- The claim exists and is economically real (unlike pure contingent items).
- It is likely to be settled by cash payment, not by ongoing equity participation.
- It is not part of permanent ownership structure.

When presenting consolidated statements, the redeemable noncontrolling interest line shows the amount the parent might have to pay. Often there is also a settlement date or range (e.g., "Redeemable within three years: $5.2 million"). Any changes in fair value of the put or call—for example, if the underlying subsidiary's profitability rises and the formula price increases—are marked through either equity or [earnings](/earnings-per-share/), depending on whether the instrument is remeasured.

## Measurement and remeasurement

Redeemable noncontrolling interests are initially recorded at the fair value of the consideration paid by the minority investor (or at the fair value of the underlying equity, if the option was embedded in an earlier deal). After initial recognition, the treatment splits:

- **Instruments remeasured each period** (most common): The carrying amount is adjusted to the higher of its initial amount or the current redemption amount. If the underlying subsidiary becomes more profitable, the put strike might increase, and the balance sheet reflects this upward adjustment. Changes flow through equity in some cases, through retained earnings in others, depending on whether the redemption is probable and imminent.

- **Instruments not remeasured**: Some redeemable interests are carried at historical cost, with disclosure of the redemption range. This is less common but acceptable if fair value cannot be reliably measured.

The key risk: if the subsidiary's value declines sharply, the minority investor might demand redemption at a formula price that exceeds current [intrinsic value](/intrinsic-value/). The parent faces a loss on the buyback. Conversely, if value rises, the parent accrues an obligation that may exceed the original investment—a drag on its own equity.

## Interaction with [consolidation](/merger/) and [earnings quality](/earnings-quality/)

Redeemable noncontrolling interests complicate [earnings](/earnings-per-share/) presentation. When a parent consolidates a subsidiary with a redeemable noncontrolling interest, should the minority's share of earnings be subtracted from consolidated net income to arrive at earnings attributable to parent shareholders?

The answer hinges on whether the minority stake is treated as equity or as a quasi-liability. If redeemable and probable, the trend is to exclude the minority's earnings from attributable earnings and to record any loss on the obligation as a charge to parent earnings. This ensures [earnings per share](/earnings-per-share/) reflects only the parent's true claim. Investors and analysts scrutinize this calculation—a parent with a large redeemable noncontrolling interest in a high-growth subsidiary might show artificially depressed earnings if the subsidiary performs well.

## Common examples and triggers

Redeemable noncontrolling interests are prevalent in:

- **Private equity buyouts**: The seller of a business (or a management team) retains a minority stake with a put option, allowing exit at a later date.
- **Venture capital rounds**: A later-stage investor receives a preferred [stock](/stock/) class that includes redemption rights if the company does not achieve milestones or have an [IPO](/initial-public-offering/) by a deadline.
- **Joint ventures**: When a joint venture partner can unilaterally exit and force a buyout by the other partner.
- **Employee stock plans**: If retiring executives have the right to put their shares back to the company at a formula price.

In each case, the economic substance is an obligation, not permanent equity. A [public company](/public-company/) acquiring a private target may leave founders with a 10% earnout stake that is redeemable after three years—the balance sheet must reflect this deferred payment obligation separately.

## Disclosure requirements

[Public companies](/public-company/) must disclose:
- The terms of the put and call options (strike prices, exercise windows, conditions).
- The range or fair value of the redemption obligation.
- The settlement date or range.
- Any recent changes in fair value or probability of redemption.
- The impact on [earnings per share](/earnings-per-share/) calculations.

These disclosures let [shareholders](/common-stock/) understand the magnitude of the obligation and assess whether the parent is likely to dilute them in order to fund a redemption.
