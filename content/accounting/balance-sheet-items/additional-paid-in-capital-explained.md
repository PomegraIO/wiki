## Par Value and the Issue Premium

Every share of stock carries a **par value**—a nominal amount set by the corporation at incorporation, typically $0.01 to $1 per share. Par value has little to do with market price; it is purely an arbitrary number filed in the corporate charter. When a company [issues stock](/initial-public-offering/), it sells shares to investors at whatever price the market bears. The difference between what investors pay and the par value per share is the premium.

If a company issues 1 million shares with a par value of $0.10, but investors pay $15 per share, the company raises $15 million. Of that, $100,000 (1 million × $0.10) flows to the [common stock](/common-stock/) line item. The remaining $14.9 million flows to additional paid-in capital.

## Balance-Sheet Presentation

Additional paid-in capital sits squarely in the [shareholders' equity](/return-on-equity/) section, immediately below or alongside the common stock line. The two together—common stock at par plus APIC—equal the total amount shareholders have paid in for their ownership stake. This distinction matters for accounting and legal purposes, though investors typically care about the combined figure.

A simplified equity section might look like:

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Common stock (1M shares, $0.10 par) | $100,000 |
| Additional paid-in capital | $14,900,000 |
| Retained earnings | $8,500,000 |
| **Total shareholders' equity** | **$23,500,000** |

Par value is almost always tiny relative to APIC, especially for established, profitable companies. This is why many people loosely refer to the combined figure as "paid-in capital."

## How APIC Grows

APIC increases whenever a company issues new shares above par value—whether through a public offering, private placement, employee stock plans, or conversion of other securities. [Stock buybacks](/share-buyback/) and other treasury-stock transactions can affect APIC in nuanced ways (e.g., a treasury-stock purchase at a loss may reduce APIC).

The account reflects the company's historical ability to sell shares at a premium. A high APIC relative to common stock par suggests investors have consistently valued the company above its nominal per-share amount. Conversely, a company forced to issue stock at or below par would contribute little to APIC and might even be seen as a warning sign of financial distress.

## APIC vs. Par Value: The Legal Distinction

The separation of common stock (par) from APIC serves legal and accounting purposes:

- **Par value** is a permanent amount per share, set in the charter and difficult to change. Many jurisdictions have mandatory minimum par values or restrictions on issuing stock below par.
- **Additional paid-in capital** is flexible and has no per-share minimum; it accumulates based on actual transaction prices.

Some accountants and investors treat the two as a single "contributed capital" figure, since both represent shareholder investment at the purchase price. However, regulators and financial statements keep them separate for clarity and to preserve the legal identity of par value.

## Tax and Dividend Implications

APIC is not taxable income to the corporation when it arises from stock issuance. The company does not deduct it or recognize a gain; the journal entry simply splits the cash received into two equity components.

For shareholders, APIC affects [cost basis](/cost-basis/) in the stock but does not by itself trigger dividend distributions. The company may declare a dividend from [retained earnings](/retained-earnings/) or other available sources, and the amount has no direct link to APIC levels. Nor is APIC "paid out"—it is a permanent part of equity unless the company liquidates or conducts a [share buyback](/share-buyback/) that reduces it.

## When to Scrutinize APIC

Analysts sometimes examine APIC as a proxy for past capital raises. A young growth company might have substantial APIC (representing venture funding at escalating valuations) with minimal retained earnings. A mature, profitable firm might show the reverse: large retained earnings, modest recent APIC growth, and reduced share issuance.

A sharp decline in APIC can signal aggressive buybacks or a loss on treasury stock transactions. An unusual increase might indicate a major secondary offering or a significant employee exercise of stock options. Neither is necessarily good or bad—they simply reflect management's capital-allocation decisions.
