*[Debt financing](/wiki/debt-financing/) is the practice of raising capital by borrowing funds—through [bank loans](/wiki/loan-origination-fees/), [corporate bonds](/wiki/corporate-bond/), or lines of credit—that must be repaid with [interest](/wiki/interest-rate/) over a fixed term. Unlike [equity financing](/wiki/equity-financing/), debt obligates the company to service payments regardless of profitability, but preserves ownership for existing shareholders and creates a tax-deductible [interest expense](/wiki/interest-coverage-ratio/).*

## Debt versus equity trade-off in capital structure

A company balances debt and equity to minimize [weighted average cost of capital (WACC)](/wiki/weighted-average-cost-of-capital/). Debt is cheaper—[interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate/) often sit below the return investors demand on [equity](/wiki/return-on-equity/)—but raises [financial risk](/wiki/leverage-ratio-forex/). At low debt levels, adding leverage is profitable: the [after-tax cost of debt](/wiki/after-tax-cost-of-debt/) is less than the return on invested capital, boosting returns to shareholders. At high debt levels, lenders demand higher rates ([credit spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) widen) and firms risk [default](/wiki/sovereign-default/) if earnings slip. The optimal capital structure balances these forces.

## Debt maturity and refinancing risk

Companies issue [short-term debt](/wiki/debt-maturity-structure/)—lines of credit, [commercial paper](/wiki/commercial-paper/)—for operational needs, and [long-term bonds](/wiki/bond-maturity-corporate/) for major projects or acquisitions. Short-term debt is cheaper but creates [refinancing risk](/wiki/refinancing-risk/)—if credit markets freeze, renewal becomes expensive or impossible. Long-term debt locks in rates but is more expensive upfront. During the [2008 financial crisis](/wiki/subprime-mortgage-crisis/), firms dependent on [short-term rolling borrowing](/wiki/credit-creation-mechanism/) faced acute crisis when markets refused to refinance.

## Covenants and debt restrictions

[Corporate bonds](/wiki/bond-covenants/) and [bank loans](/wiki/debt-covenant-type/) often include restrictive covenants: minimum [interest coverage ratios](/wiki/interest-coverage-ratio/), maximum [debt-to-equity](/wiki/debt-to-equity-ratio/) thresholds, or restrictions on additional borrowing. These protect lenders but constrain management flexibility. Violating a covenant can trigger [cross-default](/wiki/cross-default-clause/) provisions, allowing creditors to demand immediate repayment. Sophisticated [leverage buyouts](/wiki/leveraged-buyout/) manage covenants closely, sometimes renegotiating if business softens.

## Tax advantage of debt

The primary advantage of debt over equity is the tax deduction for [interest expense](/wiki/interest-coverage-ratio/). A firm borrowing $100 million at 5% pays $5 million in interest, which reduces [taxable income](/wiki/marginal-tax-rate-investor/) by $5 million. At a 21% corporate tax rate, this shields $1.05 million from taxes annually. This tax benefit explains why profitable firms carry debt—it is cheaper after-tax than raising the same capital through equity. However, [tax-loss carryforwards](/wiki/deferred-tax-asset/) limit the value of interest deductions when firms face losses.

## Debt issuance and market conditions

When [interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate-risk/) are low (or declining), firms rush to issue [long-term bonds](/wiki/callable-bond/) to lock in cheap funding. During rate hikes, issuance slows as companies balk at higher [coupons](/wiki/coupon-rate/). Market [liquidity](/wiki/liquidity-risk/) also matters: in crises, even investment-grade firms pay [widened spreads](/wiki/credit-spread-corporate/) or cannot issue at all. [Credit rating](/wiki/credit-rating/) agencies assess [default probability](/wiki/credit-loss-model/), determining whether a firm qualifies for [investment-grade](/wiki/investment-grade-bond/) or [high-yield](/wiki/high-yield-bond/) markets.

## Leverage metrics and financial health assessment

Investors track [debt-to-equity](/wiki/debt-to-equity-ratio/), [debt-to-EBITDA](/wiki/debt-to-ebitda-ratio/), and [interest coverage](/wiki/interest-coverage-ratio/) ratios to gauge leverage. Debt-to-EBITDA above 4–5x signals elevated [default risk](/wiki/default-rate/). [Fixed-charge coverage](/wiki/fixed-charge-coverage-ratio/) assesses the ability to service debt from operating cash flow. Highly leveraged companies—e.g., real estate firms with 70% debt-to-cap—are sensitive to earnings declines or [recessions](/wiki/recession/), making them volatile investments.

## Restructuring and debt forgiveness

If a company cannot service debt, [restructuring](/wiki/debt-restructuring/) becomes necessary. This may involve [debt-equity swaps](/wiki/debt-equity-swap/) (creditors become shareholders), [maturity extensions](/wiki/bond-maturity-corporate/), or haircuts (creditors accept cents on the dollar). [Junk bond](/wiki/junk-bond/) holders often face significant losses in distressed [restructurings](/wiki/distressed-value-opportunity/), while [senior](/wiki/bond-seniority/) secured lenders fare better. Bankruptcy courts allocate proceeds according to [seniority](/wiki/bond-seniority/); [subordinated debt](/wiki/subordinated-bond/) holders recover little.
