*Minority interest (also called noncontrolling interest) on the income statement represents the portion of a subsidiary's earnings that belongs to shareholders other than the parent company.* When a company consolidates a subsidiary it does not fully own—say it owns 75% and minority shareholders own 25%—the [income statement](/income-statement/) includes 100% of the subsidiary's revenues and expenses, then subtracts the minority shareholders' share of net income. This line isolates the parent's true economic claim on the subsidiary and answers a critical question: how much of reported consolidated earnings actually belong to the parent's shareholders?

## Why Consolidation Creates Minority Interest

Consolidation is the accounting rule that applies when a parent company owns more than 50% of a subsidiary's voting shares and can control the subsidiary's operations, strategy, and assets.

Under consolidation, the parent combines 100% of the subsidiary's revenues, expenses, assets, and liabilities with its own figures. This gives a true picture of all the economic resources the parent controls, even if it does not own them outright.

However, consolidating all of the subsidiary's net income on the parent's [income statement](/income-statement/) would overstate the parent's actual earnings. If the parent owns 75% of the subsidiary, it has a claim to only 75% of that subsidiary's profit. The other 25% belongs to the minority (noncontrolling) shareholders. Recording all of it as the parent's would be misleading.

**Minority interest** solves this problem. It is a line item that deducts the noncontrolling shareholders' share of subsidiary earnings from consolidated net income, leaving only the parent's claim.

## The Calculation

The calculation is straightforward:

**Minority Interest = Subsidiary Net Income × Minority Ownership %**

**Example:**

- Parent Company owns 80% of Subsidiary ABC; minority shareholders own 20%.
- Subsidiary ABC's net income: $10,000,000.
- Minority interest deduction: $10,000,000 × 20% = $2,000,000.
- Amount attributable to parent: $10,000,000 × 80% = $8,000,000.

On the consolidated income statement:

```
Consolidated Net Income (before minority interest)     $125,000,000
Less: Minority Interest in Subsidiary ABC              ($2,000,000)
―――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――――
Net Income Attributable to Parent                      $123,000,000
```

The parent's [earnings per share](/earnings-per-share/) is calculated using the $123,000,000 figure (net income attributable to parent), not the pre-minority $125,000,000. Failing to adjust for minority interest would inflate EPS.

## Position on the Income Statement

Minority interest typically appears in one of two locations, depending on the [accounting standard](/generally-accepted-accounting-principles/) applied:

**US GAAP and IFRS (modern treatment):** Minority interest is a **deduction in the bottom section** of the income statement, after operating income and [income tax](/corporate-income-tax/), just before or just after the final net income line. It is presented as a subtraction, moving from "consolidated net income" to "net income attributable to parent."

**Some older formats:** Minority interest may appear as a deduction under "other expenses" or in a separate section labeled "noncontrolling interests," but the net effect is identical.

The key principle is that minority interest is a **reduction of net income**, not an operating expense. It does not affect [operating margin](/operating-margin/) or [EBITDA](/ebitda/); it is strictly an allocation of the bottom-line profit.

## An Earnings Waterfall

Think of minority interest as an earnings waterfall:

1. **Consolidated net income** includes all revenues minus all operating expenses, taxes, and financing costs, as if the parent owned 100% of every subsidiary.
2. **Minority interest deduction** diverts a fraction of that consolidated income to the noncontrolling shareholders.
3. **Net income attributable to parent** is what remains—the parent's true economic claim on profits.

This waterfall approach is especially important for comparing companies with different ownership structures. Two companies might report identical consolidated net income, but if one has significant minority interests and the other does not, the parent's net income will differ materially.

## Interaction with the Balance Sheet

Minority interest also appears on the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/), in the **equity section**. This balance-sheet line represents the accumulated claim of noncontrolling shareholders on the subsidiary's net assets (assets minus liabilities).

The relationship between the income statement and balance sheet minority interest lines is:

```
Beginning Minority Interest Balance
+ Minority's share of subsidiary earnings (from income statement)
- Dividends paid to minority shareholders
= Ending Minority Interest Balance
```

Like all equity accounts, minority interest grows (or shrinks) based on retained earnings and transactions.

## Multiple Subsidiaries and Tiered Ownership

If a parent owns multiple subsidiaries at different ownership levels, each generates its own minority interest calculation.

**Example:**

- Parent owns 90% of Subsidiary A (net income $10M) and 70% of Subsidiary B (net income $5M).
- Minority interest in A: $10M × 10% = $1M.
- Minority interest in B: $5M × 30% = $1.5M.
- Total minority interest deduction: $2.5M.

Each subsidiary's noncontrolling shareholders have a separate economic claim, calculated independently.

**Tiered ownership** (the parent owns 80% of a holding company, which owns 90% of an operating subsidiary) requires a cascade calculation. The parent's effective ownership of the operating subsidiary is 80% × 90% = 72%. The minority interest at each level is calculated separately, and the consolidated income statement will show minority interest for each tier if applicable.

## When Ownership Changes

If the parent increases or decreases its ownership percentage during a year—buying out some minority shareholders or issuing new shares that dilute the parent's stake—minority interest must be recalculated for each period.

If the parent's ownership drops below 50% (losing control), the subsidiary is no longer consolidated. The parent switches to equity method accounting, and no minority interest line appears. Instead, the parent's share of subsidiary earnings is shown as investment income.

Conversely, if the parent acquires control of a previously unconsolidated investee, the subsidiary is consolidated going forward, and a new minority interest line emerges.

## Interpreting Minority Interest for Investors

For an investor analyzing a company with significant minority interests, the key insight is:

**Reported consolidated net income overstates the parent company's earnings.** The true earnings attributable to the parent's shareholders are lower by the minority interest amount.

A company reporting $100M consolidated net income but $20M minority interest has only $80M of earnings that belong to parent shareholders. This is crucial for [valuation](/relative-valuation/) and [price-to-earnings ratio](/price-to-earnings-ratio/) calculations.

High minority interest may indicate:

- The parent owns a large number of partly-owned subsidiaries (common in conglomerates and private equity firms).
- The parent has made acquisitions but did not acquire 100% of the target.
- The parent intentionally maintains minority co-investors to spread risk or share returns.

Low or zero minority interest suggests the parent owns most or all of its subsidiaries outright, a more typical structure for focused operating companies.
