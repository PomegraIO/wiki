*A **common-size financial statement** rescales every line item as a percentage of a single base figure—on the [income statement](/income-statement/), the base is [revenue](/revenue-recognition/); on the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/), it is total assets. The result reveals the internal structure of a business without the distraction of absolute scale. A $1 billion company and a $100 million company can be compared directly. Margin trends over a decade become visible at a glance. Analysts rely on common-size statements to spot operational efficiency gains, cost creep, asset bloat, and deviation from industry norms.*

## Why scale is noise

Two companies—one with $5 billion in annual revenue, one with $100 million—look radically different when stacked side by side on a traditional [income statement](/income-statement/). But if the big one keeps 15% of revenue as [operating profit](/operating-margin/) and the small one keeps 20%, the small one is actually more efficient. Traditional statements obscure this. They reward readers for mental arithmetic. Common-size statements do the math and display the answer.

The principle is simple: ratios matter more than absolute numbers for understanding business health. An analyst cares whether a company's [operating costs](/operating-margin/) are shrinking as a percentage of sales (a sign of efficiency or pricing power) or growing (a sign of cost pressure or lost pricing leverage). Are [receivables](/accounts-receivable/) ballooning as a share of assets (a sign of slower collection, maybe weaker sales)? Is the company loading up with debt—[liabilities](/debt-financing/) growing as a percentage of total assets—to finance growth or mask profit weakness? These questions are answered instantly by a common-size statement.

## Building a common-size income statement

Start with a standard [income statement](/income-statement/): revenues, cost of goods sold, operating expenses, interest, taxes, net income. Now divide every line by revenue (set to 100%). The result:

| Line Item | Dollar Amount | Common-Size % |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $500 million | 100% |
| Cost of Goods Sold | $300 million | 60% |
| **Gross Profit** | $200 million | 40% |
| Operating Expenses | $120 million | 24% |
| **Operating Profit (EBIT)** | $80 million | 16% |
| Interest Expense | $10 million | 2% |
| **Pre-Tax Income** | $70 million | 14% |
| Income Tax | $17.5 million | 3.5% |
| **Net Income** | $52.5 million | 10.5% |

The percentages tell a complete story. This business retains 10.5 cents of profit for every dollar of revenue. Its [gross margin](/gross-profit-margin/) is 40%, meaning 60% of revenue goes to the cost of the product itself. Operating expenses are 24% of revenue—a reasonable range for many industries. Interest is 2%, suggesting moderate debt. From this view, an analyst can quickly ask: How does this compare to competitors? How has it trended over the past five years?

## Building a common-size balance sheet

On the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/), set total assets to 100% and express every line as a percentage of that. If a company has $1 billion in assets, a common-size balance sheet might look like:

| Asset | Dollar Amount | Common-Size % |
|---|---|---|
| Cash and Equivalents | $150 million | 15% |
| [Accounts Receivable](/accounts-receivable/) | $200 million | 20% |
| Inventory | $250 million | 25% |
| Current Assets | $600 million | 60% |
| Property, Plant & Equipment | $350 million | 35% |
| Goodwill and Intangibles | $50 million | 5% |
| **Total Assets** | $1,000 million | 100% |

On the liability side:

| Liability & Equity | Dollar Amount | Common-Size % |
|---|---|---|
| [Accounts Payable](/accounts-payable/) | $100 million | 10% |
| Short-term Debt | $50 million | 5% |
| Current Liabilities | $150 million | 15% |
| Long-term Debt | $250 million | 25% |
| Total Liabilities | $400 million | 40% |
| Shareholders' Equity | $600 million | 60% |
| **Total Liabilities & Equity** | $1,000 million | 100% |

This reveals the capital structure plainly: 40% [debt](/debt-financing/)-financed, 60% equity-financed. A decade ago, this company might have been 50% debt, 50% equity. The shift signals either a decision to deleverage or a sharp increase in [retained earnings](/retained-earnings/). An analyst spots the transition without doing a calculation.

## Horizontal vs. vertical analysis

Common-size statements are sometimes called "vertical analysis" because you divide down the column (each item is a percentage of the column total). Vertical analysis reveals the internal mix of a single statement. But analysts often pair this with "horizontal analysis"—tracking the same percentage across multiple years to see trends.

For example:

| Metric | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross Margin % | 42% | 41% | 39% |
| Operating Margin % | 18% | 17% | 15% |
| Net Margin % | 11% | 10% | 8% |

This shows cost creep over three years. Even though the company's absolute revenue may have grown, its profitability as a percentage of sales is falling. This could signal rising input costs, competitive pressure, or deteriorating operational efficiency. The common-size format makes the trend impossible to miss.

## Spotting outliers and industry comparison

One of the most powerful uses of common-size analysis is benchmarking against the industry. If the industry average [gross margin](/gross-profit-margin/) is 45% but your company sits at 50%, you are outperforming. If competitors average 24% for operating expenses and yours are 28%, you are cost-burdened. A single common-size statement can trigger a deep-dive investigation.

Similarly, common-size statements reveal structural anomalies. A retail company with 50% of assets in cash suggests either excess liquidity (a sign of capital discipline or pending deployment) or a struggling business that has slowed reinvestment. A tech company with 30% [goodwill](/goodwill/) might be acquisition-dependent or might have vastly overpaid for past deals (see [accumulated depreciation](/accumulated-depreciation/)). Again, the percentages flag questions worth investigating.

## When common-size can mislead

The main limitation: common-size statements normalize for scale but not for business model differences. A profitable business and a failing one both show expenses as percentages of revenue. If you compare a high-margin luxury brand (perhaps 40% net profit on revenue) to a high-volume low-margin retailer (perhaps 3% net profit), the percentages look wildly different, even though both are viable. The common-size statement reveals the difference but does not judge it. Context—the industry, competitive position, [return on equity](/return-on-equity/), growth—matters.

Also, a common-size statement cannot be used in isolation to rank companies. One firm with a 20% operating margin and another with a 12% both might trade profitably. The margin difference could reflect [scale economics](/market-capitalization/), capital intensity, or brand power—factors that are visible in the common-size statement but not evaluated by it. Analysis must still apply judgment.

## Practical workflow

Analysts typically start with a common-size income statement to check [margins](/gross-profit-margin/). If they see deterioration, they dig into the [cost of goods](/cost-basis/) or operating expenses to isolate where the problem lies. Then they examine the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) in common-size form to see if [asset efficiency](/return-on-assets/) is deteriorating (more assets needed to generate the same revenue) or if the capital structure is shifting (more debt, less equity). Finally, they plot the percentages over time to confirm the trend and project forward.

The common-size approach is especially useful for investors evaluating businesses in different sectors or geographies, where absolute scale differences would otherwise swamp the comparison. A 5% operating margin improvement sounds modest until you see that the industry average is flat: suddenly the business looks operationally robust.
