*[Historical cost](/historical-cost) is the original amount paid to acquire an asset. For example, if a company buys a building for $5 million, the [historical cost](/historical-cost) is $5 million. This is the standard basis for recording most assets under [GAAP](/generally-accepted-accounting-principles) and [IFRS](/international-financial-reporting-standards). [Historical cost](/historical-cost) is adjusted for [depreciation](/depreciation) and [amortization](/amortization) as the asset is used, and may be tested for impairment if its value declines unexpectedly. [Historical cost](/historical-cost) contrasts with [fair value](/fair-value), which is the current market price. [Historical cost](/historical-cost) is less volatile but may be outdated; [fair value](/fair-value) is current but can be uncertain.*

## Historical cost principle

The [historical cost](/historical-cost) principle is fundamental to [accrual-accounting](/accrual-accounting). Assets are recorded at what the company actually paid for them, adjusted for allocation ([depreciation](/depreciation)) and any indication of decline in value (impairment).

Example: A company buys land for $1 million. Under [historical cost](/historical-cost), the land remains on the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet) at $1 million forever (adjusted if impaired). Even if the land is worth $10 million five years later, the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet) shows $1 million.

This is conservative: it avoids inflating asset values based on speculation about future market prices.

## Historical cost vs. current market value

The advantage of [historical cost](/historical-cost) is objectivity. What was paid is a historical fact; current market value requires estimation. However, the disadvantage is that [historical cost](/historical-cost) becomes less relevant over time.

Consider two companies with identical property:
- Company A bought in 1980 for $1 million. [Historical cost](/historical-cost): $1 million.
- Company B bought in 2020 for $10 million. [Historical cost](/historical-cost): $10 million.

If both properties are now worth $12 million, the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet) shows very different values, even though the economic reality is the same.

## Depreciation and amortization under historical cost

[Historical cost](/historical-cost) assets are reduced by [depreciation](/depreciation) or [amortization](/amortization):

- Building cost: $10,000,000
- Accumulated [depreciation](/depreciation): ($4,000,000)
- Net book value: $6,000,000

The net book value is what appears on the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet). The net book value is objective (based on original cost and mechanical [depreciation](/depreciation) schedules) but likely differs from fair value.

## Impairment testing under historical cost

If the value of a [historical cost](/historical-cost) asset declines below its net book value, the asset must be tested for impairment. If fair value falls substantially below book value (e.g., a building is damaged, or equipment becomes technologically obsolete), the asset must be written down.

This represents the main exception to the pure [historical cost](/historical-cost) principle: assets are not written up for appreciation, but they are written down for decline.

## When fair value is required instead

Some assets must be measured at [fair value](/fair-value), not [historical cost](/historical-cost):

- **Trading securities**: [fair value](/fair-value) with changes in [income statement](/income-statement).
- **Derivatives**: [fair value](/fair-value).
- **Certain debt**: [fair value](/fair-value) option available.
- **Investment property** (under [IFRS](/international-financial-reporting-standards)): [fair value](/fair-value) option.

The choice of measurement basis is specified in the relevant standard and disclosed in footnotes.

## Historical cost and conservatism

[Historical cost](/historical-cost) is viewed as a conservative measurement basis. It avoids overstating assets and profits. A [balance sheet](/balance-sheet) with assets at [historical cost](/historical-cost) is more conservative than one with assets at [fair value](/fair-value) (assuming prices have risen).

Conversely, [historical cost](/historical-cost) can understate assets if prices have risen significantly, which is why many companies voluntarily disclose fair values of major assets in footnotes.

## International perspectives

[IFRS](/international-financial-reporting-standards) permits revaluation of property, plant, and equipment to [fair value](/fair-value) on a class-by-class basis. Companies can choose to measure buildings at [fair value](/fair-value) and update them annually. [GAAP](/generally-accepted-accounting-principles) does not permit this (with minor exceptions).

This means US companies' [balance sheets](/balance-sheet) tend to be more conservative (assets at [historical cost](/historical-cost)) than international companies' (which may include [fair value](/fair-value) revaluations).

## Historical cost as an assumption in valuation

When investors value a company, they often adjust for the gap between book value (based on [historical cost](/historical-cost)) and fair value. A company with old, long-depreciated assets may have book values far below current market value. Adjustments for these hidden values are common in investment analysis.
