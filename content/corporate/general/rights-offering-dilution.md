*A **rights offering** lets existing shareholders buy newly issued shares at a fixed discount price before the general public can. Those who do not participate face dilution—their ownership stake shrinks as a percentage of the company, though the mechanism and severity depend on whether they can transfer or sell their rights. Companies typically use rights offerings when raising capital quickly, when distressed, or when bylaws require protecting shareholder ownership.*

## The Mechanics of a Rights Offering

A **rights offering dilution** scenario unfolds when a company issues new shares and grants existing shareholders the first opportunity to buy them at below-market terms. The company selects a subscription price (say 75% of the current stock price) and a ratio—typically "one new share for every 5 held" or "one for every 10." The shareholder receives a notice of rights, along with subscription documents, and a deadline to act.

When a shareholder exercises their rights, they pay the subscription price and receive new shares. When they do not, the rights either expire worthless or are offered for sale on the open market. Here lies the dilution question: if only some shareholders participate, the participating shareholders' cost basis is lower (they bought at discount), but the non-participating shareholders' ownership percentage falls without them having had the option to maintain their stake proportionally.

The fair-value math works like this: assume a company has 100 shares outstanding, trading at $100 per share, for a total market cap of $10,000. It issues 20 new shares via rights offering at $75 each, raising $1,500. The new total is 120 shares and $11,500 in value. A shareholder who originally held 1 share (1%) and does not exercise will now own 1 share of 120, or roughly 0.83%. Their percentage stake has fallen from 1% to 0.83%, even though the economic value of that single share may have improved or declined based on how the capital is deployed.

## Why Existing Shareholders Face Dilution

The dilution effect arises because new shares are created and introduced to the cap table. If a shareholder holds a fixed number of shares and the total share count grows, the percentage of the company owned by that shareholder shrinks. This is **mechanical dilution**, not value dilution—the shareholder's voting power and earnings-per-share weight fall, regardless of what the company does with the raised capital.

However, the severity is not inevitable. If a shareholder can purchase enough new shares to maintain their original percentage, they incur no dilution. For example, in a "one-for-five" rights offering, a shareholder with 100 shares can exercise all rights, buy 20 new shares, and own 120 of 600 total—still 20%. The cost is the capital outlay, not the loss of ownership percentage.

The catch: most shareholders cannot or will not deploy the cash needed to participate fully. Constraints include lack of liquidity, unwillingness to add capital at uncertain terms, or inability to act in time. This is why rights offerings can leave passive or cash-strapped shareholders visibly poorer on the share register.

## The Ex-Rights Date and Rights Trading

The company sets an **ex-rights date**, after which new buyers of the stock do not receive rights. This creates an important window: rights themselves often trade separately from the common stock, allowing shareholders who do not want to exercise to recover some value by selling their rights to investors who do.

If rights trade actively, a shareholder can sell their rights at a fair price—perhaps recovering 70–90% of the theoretical benefit of the discount. If rights trade poorly or there is no secondary market, a non-exercising shareholder may lose the entire benefit. This is especially common in distressed situations, where the perceived risk of the company makes participation unattractive and rights prices collapse.

The spread between the rights price and the theoretical exercise benefit reflects market sentiment: if the company's prospects are improving, rights commands higher prices; if distress deepens, they approach zero.

## When and Why Companies Use Rights Offerings

Rights offerings appear most often in three scenarios:

**Distressed capital raises.** A company in financial difficulty needs cash quickly. Rights offerings are faster to close than a standard [secondary offering](/secondary-offering/), require less regulatory back-and-forth, and avoid the negative signal of an immediate [share buyback](/share-buyback/) or equity raise that dips sharply below market price.

**Shareholder protection bylaws.** Many jurisdictions (especially in Europe) and some company charters include preemptive rights, which obligate the company to offer new shares to existing shareholders first. A rights offering fulfills this legal obligation and can be required even if the company would prefer a direct institutional placement.

**Rapid dilution with fairness.** If a company needs to raise capital but cannot stomach the optics of a non-offering dilution (such as options exercise without a capital raise), a rights offering at least signals that all shareholders had an equal opportunity to participate.

## The Accounting and Tax Treatment

From an accounting perspective, a rights offering is a straightforward equity transaction. The company records the cash received and increases shareholder equity. There is no income-statement impact—it is purely a balance-sheet shift.

For shareholders, a [cost basis](/cost-basis/) question arises. If you exercise rights, your new shares carry a cost basis equal to the subscription price paid. If you sell rights, you realize a gain or loss if the rights price differs from zero. Tax treatment varies by jurisdiction: in the U.S., rights typically inherit the cost basis of the underlying shares (calculated on a weighted-average basis), and gain or loss on the sale of rights is capital gain or loss.

## Dilution as a Warning Signal

A rights offering at a steep discount can signal underlying stress. If the company could tap debt or private equity at reasonable rates, a rights offering with a 20% discount might not be necessary. A 30% or larger discount, or repeated rights offerings, often suggests the company's creditworthiness or growth prospects have deteriorated, making traditional capital raises difficult.

Conversely, a modest rights offering (5–10% discount) by a stable company can be a signal of financial discipline—the company prefers to raise capital from those most committed (existing shareholders) and is confident enough not to discount heavily.
