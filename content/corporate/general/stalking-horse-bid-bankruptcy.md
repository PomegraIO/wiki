*A **stalking-horse bid** in bankruptcy is a preliminary offer to purchase substantially all of a debtor's assets under Section 363 of the Bankruptcy Code. The stalking-horse bidder sets a price floor for a court-supervised auction, and because it bears the burden of due diligence and commits to a specific offer, other bidders can compete from a clear starting point.*

## What Is a Stalking-Horse Bid?

In a typical Chapter 11 bankruptcy liquidation, the company's assets must be sold to pay creditors. Rather than conduct an open market sale (which is slow and uncertain), or sell to a single buyer without any competitive check, the debtor may request court approval of a **363 sale**—a process governed by Section 363 of the Bankruptcy Code.

A stalking-horse bid is the opening bid in this structured auction. A potential buyer (often a competitor, private equity firm, or secured lender) agrees in principle to buy all or substantially all assets at a specified price. The buyer and debtor negotiate deal terms, representations, and break-up fees. The debtor then asks the bankruptcy court to approve a bidding procedure that uses this stalking-horse offer as the baseline.

The purpose is elegantly practical: rather than start from zero and negotiate a deal in the open, the stalking-horse gives the court and creditors a concrete reference point and gives competing bidders a clear floor they must exceed.

## The Bidding Procedure and Auction Timeline

Once the court approves a bidding procedure, a typical sequence unfolds:

**Stalking-horse submits offer**: The initial buyer files a detailed purchase agreement and term sheet with the bankruptcy court.

**Court approves procedure**: The judge signs an order allowing the debtor to conduct a 363 auction, setting the stalking-horse bid as the opening offer and establishing bid increments (usually 2–5% above the stalking price).

**Qualified bidders register**: Other potential buyers sign confidentiality agreements, receive due diligence data, and confirm they are credible and able to close.

**Auction window opens**: A date (perhaps 30–45 days out) is set for the "go-shop" period during which other bidders can submit competing offers.

**Auction or sealed bids**: On the designated date, competing bids are compared. If there are higher bids, an auction (live or sealed) may take place.

**Court approves winning bid**: The judge approves the highest and best offer in a final hearing, sometimes called the "363 motion" or "sale order" hearing.

**Closing**: The winning bidder takes control of the assets.

## Why Creditors and the Court Favor Stalking-Horse Sales

Stalking-horse bids serve several stakeholder interests:

**Certainty for creditors**: A named buyer with a specific offer gives creditors visibility into the likely recovery. They know they will receive at minimum the stalking price, minus transaction costs.

**Competitive pressure**: Because the stalking-horse establishes a floor, it invites competing bids. Other buyers know the baseline and can make a rational choice to top it or walk away. This competition often drives a higher final price than a negotiated one-off deal.

**Speed**: A 363 sale with a stalking-horse typically closes in 30–90 days, versus 6–12 months for a traditional market sale. Faster closure means lower carrying costs and less value deterioration.

**Reduced fraud risk**: The debtor and court have scrutinized the stalking-horse's terms and financial ability to close. Competing bidders can see the deal in full and judge whether to beat it. This transparency reduces the risk of a buyer later backing out or suing over undisclosed liabilities.

## The Break-Up Fee and Bid Protections

Stalking-horse bids usually include a **break-up fee** (or "reverse topping fee"), typically 2–4% of the purchase price. This fee compensates the stalking-horse if another bidder wins the auction. It exists because the stalking-horse has invested in due diligence, negotiated detailed terms, and taken legal and commercial risk.

Without a break-up fee, a stalking-horse bidder might back away after investing months in analysis, knowing that competing bids could emerge at the last minute. The fee incentivizes the stalking-horse to bid seriously while still allowing other bidders to compete.

The court scrutinizes these fees to ensure they are not so high that they discourage competition. Typically, fees of 2–4% are approved; fees above 5% face skepticism.

## Who Becomes the Stalking Horse?

Stalking-horse bidders are usually:

- **Secured lenders**: A company's bank or senior creditor may bid to recover as much as possible of its claim.
- **Competitors**: A rival firm in the same industry may see assets as valuable and worth acquiring at a bankruptcy discount.
- **Private equity investors**: PE firms often have cash and appetite for distressed asset purchases.
- **Strategic buyers**: Companies seeking bolt-on acquisitions or market consolidation.

The stalking-horse is rarely a brand-new buyer with no prior relationship. It is typically someone who has been watching the troubled company or has an existing stake (secured debt, trade vendor relationship, or competitor status).

## How Competing Bidders Benefit

Competing bidders gain several advantages from the stalking-horse structure:

**Clear information**: They receive a detailed due diligence package because the debtor has already compiled it for the stalking-horse.

**Defined rules**: The bidding procedure sets out exactly what they must do to qualify as a "stalking-horse" or competing bidder, how bids are ranked, and the timeline.

**Reduced legal risk**: The bankruptcy process limits litigation over the sale. Once the court approves the winning bid, creditors and shareholders cannot easily challenge it (with narrow exceptions for fraud or unfair procedure).

**Known floor**: They know they must beat a specific price, not negotiate from the debtor's opening anchor. This lets them do true competitive analysis.
