*A **reverse break-up fee** (or reverse termination fee) is a penalty the acquirer pays to the target company if the buyer walks away from a [merger](/merger/) or [acquisition](/acquisition/) before closing. Unlike a standard [break-up fee](/break-up-fee/), which protects the buyer, the reverse version shields the seller from abandonment—most often when the acquirer's [debt financing](/debt-financing/) evaporates.*

## Why sellers demand reverse break fees

When a target company agrees to be acquired, management and shareholders surrender autonomy and access to other buyers. The moment a deal is announced, competitors may vanish, share price may become pinned to the offer, and the target's board assumes risk that the buyer will fail to deliver.

The reverse break-up fee is the buyer's insurance policy that protects the seller. If the acquirer loses [financing](/debt-financing/) or encounters unexpected regulatory walls, the seller receives cash compensation for lost time, opportunity cost, and any share-price depression that may follow a deal collapse.

This is especially critical when the buyer is relying on leverage. A [leveraged buyout](/leveraged-buyout/) or equity-financed mega-deal with tight credit markets can unravel if the markets move. The target, which has sat idle marketing itself to competitors, needs assurance that the buyer is serious.

## When reverse fees get triggered

The most common trigger is **financing failure**. An acquirer secures a financing commitment, announces the deal, and then—perhaps due to credit market tightening, a ratings downgrade, or covenant violations—the lender withdraws the commitment or tightens terms to unreasonable levels. The buyer declines to close on the new terms and walks away. The reverse break fee is due.

Other triggers include:

- **Regulatory rejection** by competition authorities (though sometimes both parties waive the fee jointly if neither wanted it).
- **Material adverse change** clauses, where the buyer claims the target's business deteriorated materially. Most deals define this narrowly to avoid buyer gaming.
- **Buyer breach** of explicit closing conditions (failing to sell required assets, for example).

A few deals tie the fee to share-price movements or market dislocations, though these are rarer and more legally contentious.

## Negotiating size and structure

The reverse fee is almost always smaller than the [standard break-up fee](/break-up-fee/) the target would pay if *it* walked away. This reflects market convention: buyers have fewer incentives to abandon deals they've announced, whereas targets sometimes flee to higher bidders.

A typical reverse fee ranges from 2 to 4 per cent of the deal value. Large, strategic acquisitions may see lower percentages (1–2%) because the buyer's reputational cost of walking is already severe. Contested takeovers or deals relying heavily on external financing may exceed 4%.

Sellers negotiate aggressively on the trigger definitions. A well-drafted reverse fee will:

- Exclude financing failures caused by general market conditions beyond the buyer's control (vs. buyer-specific issues).
- Require the buyer to demonstrate it made good-faith efforts to arrange alternative financing.
- Define material adverse change so narrowly that the buyer cannot exploit it.

## The tension with financing certainty

Reverse break fees create a dilemma: they incentivise the buyer to secure reliable financing *before* announcing the deal. Yet markets reward speed, and many acquirers announce before lenders have committed. This is where **committed financing** and **financing certainty letters** become essential—they impose penalties on the lender if it fails to deliver, shifting risk away from the target and onto the financial institutions.

Some large deals pair a reverse break fee with a **specific performance** clause, where the target can compel the buyer to close if financing is available. This is rare but powerful: it converts the deal from optionality into obligation.

## Strategic use and pitfalls

Reverse break fees can be used strategically by targets. A high reverse fee may discourage last-minute offers from hostile bidders (since they'd have to beat not only the current price but also pay the penalty if they fail). Conversely, a low or nonexistent reverse fee signals weakness and may invite competing bids.

For buyers, accepting a substantial reverse fee is a credibility signal—it demonstrates to shareholders and lenders that management is committed. However, it also increases the cost of walking away from a deal that deteriorates during diligence, which is why buyers push back hard on trigger definitions.
