*A **club deal** is an acquisition in which two or more private equity firms jointly purchase and own a company, splitting both the capital commitment and the post-exit returns. Club deals became the standard way to execute large buyouts because no single PE firm holds enough dry powder to go it alone—pooling capital, risk, and operational expertise lets sponsors tackle multi-billion-dollar targets that would be unwieldy or capital-straining for one firm to hold alone.*

## Why PE Firms Club Together

The mathematics of modern large acquisitions force the issue. A mega-cap [leveraged buyout](/leveraged-buyout/) of a $10 billion enterprise might require $3–4 billion of equity from sponsors, plus debt. No single PE firm, even the largest, wants to tie up that much capital in one deal and hold 100% of the execution risk. Teaming up lets each sponsor contribute $500 million to $1.5 billion—large enough to meaningfully invest, small enough to preserve capital for other bets, follow-on add-on acquisitions, and opportunistic deployment.

Beyond balance-sheet relief, club deals pull in different operational playbooks. One sponsor might excel at margin expansion; another at revenue growth or bolt-on strategy. Placing management fees, monitoring rights, and board seats across multiple firms also diffuses fiduciary pressure and reduces the concentration of decision-making in a single org.

## The Lead-Sponsor Model

In most club deals, one sponsor leads. This lead firm typically sources the deal, shepherds due diligence and negotiations, and earns a larger carried interest stake than co-investors. Co-sponsors participate in board seats and operating discussions but may cede primary control and exit timing to the lead, or negotiate side-car vehicles for different exit scenarios.

The lead also typically manages [debt](/debt-financing/) relationships and refinancing, controls the management team and budget allocation during the hold, and coordinates the sale process or exit event. Co-sponsors have governance rights—unanimous or supermajority thresholds for major decisions—but the operational responsibility concentrates on the lead.

## Capital Sourcing and Investor Base

Club deals complicate fundraising narratives. A large LP (like a pension fund or endowment) may commit capital to Sponsor A's flagship fund expecting to co-invest alongside Sponsor B's separate capital for a specific club transaction. Secondary terms—entry valuations, expense allocations, fee splits—must be negotiated upfront and clearly stated in the investment agreement.

This transparency requirement has made club-deal terms increasingly standardized. LPs demand clarity on how monitoring fees, transaction fees, and carry are split. A common structure: the lead sponsor takes a higher carry (say, 30% of net profits) and the co-sponsors take a lower carry (10–15% each), with the remainder going to LPs. Alternatively, sponsors may split carry equally but differentiate by fee-paying responsibility or operational ownership percentages.

## Antitrust and Regulatory Scrutiny

Club deals have drawn heightened antitrust attention in recent years, especially when competitors in the same sector team up to buy a rival. The U.S. Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission have signaled concern that multiple PE sponsors acquiring the same company and sitting on the board together might dampen post-acquisition competition or create illegal agreements on pricing or strategy.

In concentrated industries—healthcare, manufacturing, retail—regulators now routinely question whether a club structure is designed to foreclose competition. The 2024–2025 period has seen several large deals delayed or conditioned on divestitures specifically because the sponsors' combined holdings elsewhere in the sector raised competitive concerns. This has pushed some club deals toward "active" co-management arrangements with separate operational teams post-close to credibly maintain competitive independence.

## The Side-Car and Preferred Return Structure

For very large deals or when co-sponsors have different return expectations, clubs sometimes create side-car funds or separate tranches. One co-sponsor might take a preferred return (say, 10% annually on their contribution) with co-sponsors taking a catch-up on the residual. This is common when a private equity generalist firm co-invests alongside an infrastructure or debt specialist with lower return hurdles.

Side-cars also allow one sponsor to have a different exit timeline or liquidity preference than others. This flexibility comes at the cost of complexity in the legal documents and ongoing accounting, but it has become market-standard in mega-deals.

## Exit Timing and Tension

A common friction point in club deals emerges at the exit. If the company is acquired by a strategic buyer or taken public, proceeds are distributed according to the pre-negotiated waterfall. But if one sponsor believes the company should be held longer (to refinance and take another dividend) while another wants to exit, tensions surface. The operating agreement typically specifies supermajority or unanimous consent thresholds for major decisions, which can lead to deadlock.

To avoid this, many modern club deals include explicit exit-timing windows, drag-along rights (allowing a supermajority to force an exit over a minority's objection), and appraisal mechanisms so dissenters can be bought out at a formula price.

## Economic Impact and Market Concentration

The rise of club deals has coincided with a consolidation of PE ownership—fewer, larger sponsors with more capital each backing fewer, larger targets. This has flattened the number of independently held mid-market companies, which some argue reduces competition in their operating industries. Others contend that club deals actually enable more competition for large assets because no single firm could match the bid of a coordinated consortium.

The regulatory framing is still evolving, but it is clear that regulators view club-deal control structures as more scrutinizable than single-sponsor buyouts. Sponsors increasingly document their post-close operational independence and competitive divergence proactively to withstand FTC challenge.
