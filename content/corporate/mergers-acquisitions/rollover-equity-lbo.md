*In a **rollover equity** structure within a leveraged buyout, selling shareholders—most commonly the founding or incumbent management team—commit to reinvest a meaningful portion of their sale proceeds into new equity in the acquisition entity, creating continuity of ownership and aligning incentives between the exiting seller and the incoming sponsor.*

The rollover is one of private equity's most elegant tools for bridging the gap between a seller who wants cash and a buyer who needs alignment. It solves a credibility problem: a sponsor buying a company needs management to stay engaged and deliver the promised operational improvements. If the seller cashes out entirely, that motivation evaporates. A rollover ties the seller's remaining wealth to the company's future performance, signaling confidence to the buyer and the lender.

## Why Sponsors Push for Rollovers

Private equity sponsors typically ask management to roll 10–30% of their sale proceeds back into the company for three strategic reasons.

First, **incentive alignment**. If a founder or CEO walks away with a check and a clean exit, they have no financial reason to help the sponsor fix operational issues, grow revenue, or prepare for a future refinancing or sale. A rollover ties management's upside to the sponsor's exit, creating a shared interest in maximizing value over a 4–7 year hold period.

Second, **sponsor capital efficiency**. A typical LBO is financed with debt, preferential equity (such as preferred stock held by the sponsor), and common equity. By asking management to roll proceeds into common equity rather than taking all cash, the sponsor preserves its own capital for other deals or reduces the check it must write from its fund. This improves the sponsor's internal rate of return (IRR) on the total fund.

Third, **lender comfort**. Banks and debt investors are more willing to lend at aggressive leverage ratios when they see management reinvesting in the company. It signals skin-in-the-game and reduces the moral hazard risk that management will extract cash and let the company deteriorate.

## The Mechanics of a Rollover

In a typical LBO rollover structure, the sale closes in a single event. The seller receives cash from two sources: the sponsor's purchase price and the proceeds of the debt facility. Out of that cash, the seller pays any debt it currently carries and the seller's shareholders (or the owner) are distributed the net proceeds. Rather than taking all of that in cash, management or other selling shareholders are asked to reinvest a portion—say 20% of their would-be cash proceeds—into new common equity in the acquisition entity.

The reinvested capital purchases shares of the new company at the same valuation the sponsor paid. For example, if a company is valued at $100 million and the sponsor writes a check for $40 million (40% equity), management agrees to roll $10 million, and debt provides $50 million, then management owns 10%, the sponsor owns 40%, and debt holds the remaining 50% of the cap table (in nominal terms; preferred equity structures often reserve sponsor control).

Crucially, the sponsor and the lender each receive *pro-rata* documents from the rollover. Management's common shares carry the same governance and economic rights as any other common shareholder, though management may also be granted additional common equity as a "management incentive plan" (MIP) to reward performance over the hold period.

## Tax Implications of Rollovers

One of the rollover's primary appeals is its tax efficiency. If management is structured as a C corporation shareholder (or holds equity through a vehicle), the sale typically triggers long-term capital gains. However, under Section 1033 of the Internal Revenue Code, a shareholder can avoid immediate tax on gains if they roll proceeds into a "like-kind" asset—in this case, equity in the acquired company (or its parent). This is a deferral, not a permanent exclusion; tax is due when the management shareholders eventually sell their new shares.

For individual shareholders operating through S-corps or pass-through vehicles, the treatment is similar: deferral of gain on the rollover portion, with tax due on eventual exit.

The tax benefit is not unlimited. The rollover asset must be held for a minimum period (generally one year) to qualify for like-kind treatment in some jurisdictions, and the IRS monitors that the rollover is genuine and not a disguised distribution. Nonetheless, for a management seller facing a large capital gains bill, the tax deferral on a 20% rollover is often worth $2–5 million in cash flow, making the rollover economically palatable even if the equity upside is uncertain.

## Common Rollover Structures

**Full-cash-equivalent rollovers** occur when management agrees to roll proceeds but the sponsor simultaneously issues management a seller note or deferred payment equal to that amount. This creates a "synthetic" cash exit for management—they nominally roll equity but economically receive cash, though with some deferral risk if the company underperforms. These are less common in pure private equity deals but appear in situations where management is skeptical about the sponsor's ability to exit profitably.

**Founder or key executive rollovers** are the most common. If a founder is selling to a sponsor, the founder may roll 20–50% of proceeds into new equity, often coupled with a seat on the board or operational role. This preserves founder incentives and gives the founder upside if the sponsor's operational strategy succeeds.

**Partial management rollovers** occur when some members of the management team roll while others do not. A CFO or head of operations might roll, while the CEO takes a clean exit, or vice versa. The rollover portion often reflects that manager's expected stay and confidence in the sponsor's plan.

## Exit and Cascade on Rollover Equity

When the sponsor exits the investment—typically through a sale to a strategic buyer or another private equity sponsor, or an IPO—the rollover equity is subject to the same capital structure waterfall as the initial investment.

If the company is sold for $400 million (a 4× return from the $100 million valuation), the cascading proceeds go: first to debt, then to any preferred equity held by the sponsor, then to common equity. Management's rollover common equity shares in the residual upside along with the sponsor's common equity. If the sponsor invested $40 million common equity, management $10 million, and the common equity pool receives $100 million on exit, management nets approximately $20 million (20% of $100 million), while the sponsor nets $80 million.

However, if the exit returns are weak—say $150 million total, with $100 million consumed by debt repayment and preferred equity—then common equity holders (both sponsor and management) may receive a nominal return or nothing. This is the key risk in a rollover: management is now economically aligned but also exposed to downside if the sponsor's plan fails.

## Negotiating Rollover Percentages

Sponsors typically target management rollovers of 15–30% of proceeds, though this varies by deal maturity and management confidence. In a founder-led company with strong historical returns, the founder may push back and accept only a 10% rollover. In a distressed or turnaround situation where the sponsor's operational plan is unproven, the target management may demand a higher total purchase price to offset the rollover risk.

The effective cash management receives is therefore the gross purchase price *minus* the rollover, *minus* any taxes on the sale. A management team selling a $100 million company for $40 million in equity and $60 million in net proceeds (after debt payoff) might net only $48 million in cash if they roll $12 million and owe $10 million in taxes—even though the headline price was $100 million.
