*A **scrip dividend** is a payout in which shareholders receive new shares of the company instead of cash. When a scrip dividend is offered, shareholders can usually elect whether to take shares or cash, while the company gets to conserve cash at the cost of diluting the ownership stake of non-participating shareholders.*

## The mechanics of a scrip dividend offer

When a company announces a scrip dividend, it typically specifies an election period during which each shareholder can choose to receive either cash or new shares for their dividend. The company sets a conversion price—commonly the volume-weighted average price (VWAP) over a defined period before the election deadline, or a discount to market price to incentivize participation.

The mechanics are straightforward. A shareholder holding 100 shares receives a notice stating that they can elect to receive $1 per share in cash or 0.95 new shares for each share held (for example). If they elect shares, they receive 95 new shares instead of $100 in cash. Non-electing shareholders may receive cash by default, or the company may have a fallback arrangement.

The company benefits immediately on the balance sheet: instead of paying out cash, which depletes working capital, it issues equity. For capital-intensive businesses or those facing temporary cash constraints, this preserves liquidity. However, the trade-off is real—the share base grows, and the ownership percentage of every shareholder who does not participate shrinks proportionally.

## Why companies offer scrip dividends

The primary motivation is cash preservation during periods when the company wants to maintain or grow its [dividend-payout-ratio](/dividend-payout-ratio/) without draining cash reserves. This is common in utilities, REITs, and commodity-linked sectors where earnings may be volatile or where capital expenditure requirements are high.

A secondary motive is accounting simplicity and legal flexibility. In jurisdictions where scrip issues avoid certain regulatory filings or tax withholding burdens that cash dividends trigger, the administrative cost drops. Some companies also use scrip as a way to lock in shareholder capital retention: by allowing shareholders to compound returns in shares rather than extract cash, they reduce the need to attract new investors elsewhere.

Acquisition and integration activity can also drive scrip use. If a company has taken on debt to fund an acquisition, it may not have ample cash for large cash dividends but still wants to signal financial health to shareholders. A scrip option lets existing shareholders reinvest without asking them to deploy new cash.

## Dilution implications for existing shareholders

The arithmetic of dilution is unavoidable. If 40% of shareholders elect the scrip and 60% take cash, the company issues new shares but still pays out the same total dollar amount—or less, if the conversion price is below the cash alternative. The share count rises, and **the ownership percentage of every shareholder falls**.

Consider a simplified example. A company with 1,000 shares outstanding announces a $1-per-share dividend, elective in either cash or 0.95 new shares. Assume all shareholders elect scrip. The company issues 950 new shares (0.95 × 1,000). The share count becomes 1,950. Each original shareholder now owns 51.3% of their previous percentage stake (1,000 / 1,950).

The impact on earnings per share (EPS) is immediate and material. If the company's earnings remain flat while the share count rises, EPS falls. This is one reason some investors view scrip unfavorably—it mechanically reduces the earnings attributed to each share, even if the company's underlying profitability has not changed. Over time, if growth exceeds dilution, this impact can be offset; but in low-growth or mature sectors, scrip is sometimes seen as value-destructive by income-focused investors.

## Election mechanics and the shareholder choice

Most scrip dividend offers are elective, meaning shareholders can opt for cash, shares, or in some cases a mix. The company sets a defined election period—often 2 to 4 weeks—during which shareholders must declare their preference. Election is usually done through the company's transfer agent or brokerage portal.

The conversion price is critical. If set too high (above current market price), few will elect shares, and the company saves little cash. If set too low, nearly every shareholder elects shares, maximizing dilution. Companies often calibrate by offering a discount of 2–5% below the recent average price, making the scrip modestly attractive while preserving the real value proposition of the underlying company.

Non-electing shareholders pose a tricky question. Some plans specify that non-electors automatically receive cash (the default). Others default to scrip. This distinction matters for investor behavior: a default-to-cash approach nudges retention of cash; a default-to-scrip approach accelerates dilution if many shareholders are passive.

## Tax and accounting treatments

Tax treatment of scrip dividends varies markedly by jurisdiction. In the United States, a scrip dividend is typically taxable as ordinary income at its fair value on the date of issuance, just like a cash dividend. The shareholder must report dividend income even though they received no cash. This creates a tax liability without a cash inflow—a risk if the shareholder then sells the shares and realizes a loss.

In the UK and some Commonwealth jurisdictions, the treatment is more favorable: scrip dividends may defer tax recognition until the shares are sold, or the taxable event aligns with realization of gain, reducing the mismatch between tax and cash flow.

Accounting-wise, the company records the scrip as an ordinary dividend expense (reducing retained earnings) and credits equity. From an [accrual-accounting](/accrual-accounting/) standpoint, the dividend has been declared and issued; the company's equity base has expanded while assets (cash) remain intact.

## When scrip makes strategic sense

Scrip dividends fit best in scenarios where a company has adequate earnings and free cash flow growth but faces temporary or cyclical cash pressures. Utilities expanding grids, REITs refinancing debt, and renewable energy companies managing high capex often use scrip windows.

They are less suitable for companies in genuine financial distress or with stagnant earnings. Shareholders in such firms may view scrip as a sign of weakening health. If a company cannot sustain even modest cash dividends, offering shares instead can feel like an attempt to hide deteriorating fundamentals.

A well-structured scrip can align long-term investor interests with the company's capital deployment strategy. If the management team is deploying retained capital at returns above the cost of equity, compounding shares may ultimately deliver better total returns than extracting the cash. But this requires genuine credibility—and it shifts risk to the shareholder, who now has exposure to both the existing business and its future capital allocation choices.
