*When a company issues new shares, the ownership stake of each existing shareholder is proportionally reduced—a phenomenon called **equity issuance dilution**. If you owned 1% of a company and it doubled its share count, you now own 0.5%. Beyond ownership percentage, dilution also reduces [earnings-per-share](/earnings-per-share/) (the same profit divided among more shares) and [book value per share](/book-value-per-share/) (the same equity divided among more shares), both of which can depress the stock price even if the company's total value is unchanged.*

## The Mechanics of Ownership Dilution

Ownership dilution is the simplest form to visualize. Suppose a company has 100 million shares outstanding, and you own 10 million (a 10% stake). The company needs $500 million in cash to fund an expansion, so it issues 50 million new shares at $10 per share, raising the full $500 million. Now the company has 150 million shares outstanding, and you still own 10 million—but that is now 10 / 150 = 6.67% of the company. Your ownership percentage has been cut by one-third, even though you bought or sold no shares.

Ownership dilution is permanent. Once the new shares are issued, they remain part of the capital structure. If you want to restore your 10% stake, you would have to buy an additional 5 million new shares at market prices, deploying capital at a time when the market may not be favorable for you.

This form of dilution matters most to founders, controlling shareholders, and strategic investors who care about their voice in governance. A founder with 30% of a startup who watches equity dilute to 20% has lost relative power in the boardroom and in voting on major decisions.

## EPS Dilution: The Same Pie, More Slices

Equity dilution also reduces earnings-per-share, often called **EPS dilution**. Here is a concrete example.

A company earned $100 million in net income last year on 100 million shares outstanding, yielding an EPS of $1.00. The stock is trading at $20 per share, a [price-to-earnings-ratio](/price-to-earnings-ratio/) of 20x. The company issues 25 million new shares at $20 each, raising $500 million. Absent any change in earnings, the company now has 125 million shares and the same $100 million in net income, so EPS falls to $100 / 125 = **$0.80**.

If the stock's valuation multiple remains at 20x PE, the stock price falls from $20 to $16. Existing shareholders lose 20% of their equity value, purely from the dilution of shares.

The loss is real, even though the company's total market capitalization has changed only by the amount of capital raised minus any cash drag. Before the issuance, market cap was $2 billion (100M shares × $20). After issuance at $20, total market cap is nominally $2.5 billion (125M shares × $20), but existing shareholders' slice has shrunk to $1.6 billion ($16 × 100M shares).

The $500 million raised went to the company, not to existing shareholders. Shareholders suffered because the company issued new shares at the then-current market price, diluting the value attributable to existing shares.

## Book Value Per Share Dilution

**Book value per share** is the accounting equity (assets minus liabilities) divided by share count. Dilution of book value happens when new shares are issued for cash raised at less than the pre-existing book value per share.

Suppose a company has:
- $1 billion in equity
- 100 million shares
- Book value per share = $10

The company issues 50 million new shares at $8 each, raising $400 million. Now the balance sheet shows:
- $1.4 billion in equity ($1 billion + $400 million)
- 150 million shares
- Book value per share = $1.4B / 150M = **$9.33**

Even though the company has more cash and more equity, the book value per share has fallen from $10 to $9.33. This is because the new shares were issued at a price ($8) below the pre-existing book value per share ($10).

Book value per share matters to value investors and for banks, insurance companies, and other financial firms where tangible book value is a key metric. Dilution of book value can signal that management is raising capital inefficiently.

## When Dilution Does NOT Harm Shareholders

The key insight is that dilution of ownership percentage and per-share metrics is not inherently destructive if the capital raised is deployed at a high return.

Suppose the company that raised $500 million deploys it to build a new factory that generates $75 million in additional annual EBITDA. If the company trades at 10x EBITDA, the new factory adds $750 million to total company value. The company's market capitalization rises from $2 billion to $2.75 billion.

Existing shareholders owned 100 million shares out of 100 million originally, so they owned 100% of $2 billion = $2 billion in value. After dilution, they own 100 million shares out of 125 million, which is 80%, but now they own 80% of $2.75 billion = **$2.2 billion** in value. They are better off, even though they own a smaller percentage.

The rule of thumb is: **dilution is acceptable if the return on the new capital (75M EBITDA / 500M raised = 15% gross return) exceeds the cost of equity.** If shareholders have a 10% required return, a 15% return on new capital creates value. If the return is only 5%, the capital was wasted and shareholders are worse off.

## Dilution from Employee Stock Options

Many companies grant stock options to employees as incentive compensation. When options are exercised (an employee buys shares at a strike price), new shares are issued and dilution occurs. Companies must disclose "diluted" earnings-per-share, which assume all in-the-money options are exercised.

A company might report basic EPS of $5 on 100 million shares, but diluted EPS might be $4.80 if outstanding options (assumed exercised) would add 5 million shares. The difference is the dilution from the option grant.

Over time, option dilution can be significant. A technology company that grants 2% of its outstanding shares in options each year will see cumulative dilution of roughly 20% per decade (ignoring buybacks). This is why many companies use [share buyback](/share-buyback/) programs to offset the dilution from options—buying back stock at market prices to match the dilution from option exercises, keeping share count relatively stable.

## Secondary Offerings and Market Reactions

When a company announces a secondary (new) equity offering, the stock often falls immediately, even before the shares are issued. This reflects several concerns:

1. **Dilution expectations**: investors anticipate the reduction in EPS and ownership percentage.
2. **Signal of weakness**: a company raising equity at depressed valuations signals that management believes the stock is fairly valued or overvalued. If management thought the stock was cheap, they would buy back shares, not issue them.
3. **Capital deployment risk**: investors may worry the proceeds will be wasted on an acquisition or project with low returns.

A company with a strong balance sheet, high profitability, and credible capital deployment plans (e.g., a specific acquisition or buyback plan) may see a smaller stock decline. A company in distress, signaling via equity issuance that it is conserving cash, often sees a sharp decline.

## Dilution vs. Stock Splits

A **stock split** (e.g., 2-for-1) creates the same dilution of ownership percentage as an equity issuance, but without raising capital. If a company splits 2-for-1, each shareholder now owns twice as many shares, but each worth half as much. Market cap is unchanged, and earnings-per-share is cut in half to reflect the doubled share count.

The difference is that stock splits do not change the capital structure or raise cash. They are typically done to lower the nominal share price and improve liquidity. An equity issuance, by contrast, raises capital and expands the balance sheet.

## Assessing Dilution in Practice

When evaluating a company's equity issuance, investors should ask:

1. **At what price relative to intrinsic value?** If the company raises capital at a discount to fundamental value, existing shareholders lose. If the company raises capital at a premium, existing shareholders may benefit (though the opposite might be true if the buyer overpays and the stock falls later).
2. **What is the capital used for?** An acquisition of a high-quality strategic asset may justify dilution. A general debt paydown or cash hoarding does not.
3. **How much is dilution offset by buybacks or growth?** If the company commits to buybacks, dilution may be temporary. If new capital drives revenue growth and margin expansion, the dilution may be offset.
4. **Is the company in a capital-constrained growth phase?** A young, fast-growing company raising capital to fund 50% annual revenue growth is in a different category from a mature company raising capital for an unclear purpose.
