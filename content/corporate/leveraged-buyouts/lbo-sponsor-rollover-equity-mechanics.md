*In a **rollover equity mechanics** scenario, the selling shareholder or founder doesn't cash out entirely. Instead, they exchange some of their old shares for equity in the new [leveraged-buyout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leveraged_buyout) structure, creating a personal and financial link to the deal's future performance. This mechanic shapes how the transaction is priced, taxed, and how incentives align.*

## The Basic Mechanic: Equity for Equity, Not Cash for Equity

A traditional buyout is simple: sponsor buys 100% of the company for a price payable in cash (funded by debt and sponsor equity). The seller walks away, and the sponsor owns everything.

A rollover flips this. The selling shareholder—often a founder or controlling family—converts some equity stake into new equity in the post-acquisition entity. If a founder owns 100% of a $100 million company and rolls 30% of her stake, she receives stock representing 30% of the combined entity valued at acquisition. In exchange, the sponsor pays her only 70% of the enterprise value in cash.

From the sponsor's standpoint, the rollover reduces cash required at close. If the company needs $70 million in cash proceeds and a $30 million equity roll is arranged, the sponsor (through the new holding company) supplies only $40 million in cash equity; the seller contributes $30 million in value as rolled equity. The gap is bridged by [debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt).

## Tax Treatment and Section 368 Reorganization

The tax incentive for rollover equity is powerful. In the US, if the transaction qualifies as a reorganization under Section 368 of the Internal Revenue Code, the seller can defer [capital gains tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_gains_tax) on the rolled portion. The rolled shares carry a carryover basis—the seller's original cost basis—so tax is pushed into a future event: a liquidity event (exit, secondary sale), a public offering, or a deemed liquidation.

This deferred-tax feature makes rollover equity attractive to sellers facing large [capital gains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_gains) liability. A founder with a $50 million gain on a $100 million company sale would owe roughly $10 million in federal tax (assuming a 20% long-term rate, plus state taxes) if she took all cash. By rolling 30% of equity, she defers tax on $30 million of gain, saving ~$6 million in immediate tax and preserving that cash for reinvestment.

The structure must satisfy reorganization tests: continuity of business purpose, continuity of interest (the seller maintains an equity interest post-close), and common control. Most sponsor-led LBOs meet these tests. However, tax law varies by jurisdiction, and the structure must be documented carefully. A seller's tax counsel will verify that the rollover qualifies before signing.

## Structuring the Rollover: Carve-Outs and Tiers

Sponsors and sellers typically negotiate the rollover percentage as part of deal price. The conversation often sounds like: "We value the company at $100 million, but we're tapping debt for $60 million and putting in $10 million of sponsor equity. We need another $30 million. We can pay you $70 million in cash today or $50 million in cash plus a $20 million equity roll."

The seller's choice depends on confidence in the business, the sponsor's track record, and tax circumstances.

In larger deals, rollover equity may be carved into tranches:

- **Common rollover shares:** The seller receives new common stock, sharing pro-rata in all future cash flows and gains. If the company doubles in value before exit, the seller's equity stake also doubles.

- **Preferred rollover shares:** Some deals issue preferred stock to the seller with protective provisions (board seats, liquidation preferences, anti-dilution) to enhance downside protection or governance rights.

- **Earnout-linked equity:** The seller's full equity stake may vest or become liquid only upon hitting EBITDA or revenue targets. This aligns the seller's interest with sponsor value creation but introduces execution risk.

## Alignment and Conflict Implications

Rollover equity creates a unique alignment: the seller (often the founding CEO or long-tenured operator) now has direct financial exposure to post-close performance. If the sponsor cuts costs too aggressively, damages the brand, or fails to grow the business, the seller's equity stake suffers.

This alignment can be positive. Sellers with rollover stakes tend to stay engaged post-close, mentoring management or sitting on advisory boards. They have skin in the game and will escalate concerns if the sponsor is mismanaging.

But conflicts also emerge. If the sponsor wants to load additional debt to fund a distribution, the seller (as equity holder) bears the risk. If the sponsor pursues an aggressive cost-cutting program that undermines long-term growth, the seller's stake is diluted. The sponsor, by contrast, typically has a fee-driven structure and a time-bounded hold (5–7 years); it may optimize for a near-term exit rather than decades of sustainable growth.

Documentation must address this through [voting rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_rights), consent mechanics, and board representation. Sellers with meaningful rollover equity often negotiate a board seat or observer rights.

## Tax Basis Carryover and Post-Exit Taxation

When the seller ultimately liquidates (via secondary sale, sponsor exit, or IPO), tax becomes due on the full gain—original basis plus appreciation post-rollover.

Example: A founder with a $10 million basis in her original stake rolls $30 million of value (basis carryover: $10 million × 30/100 = $3 million). The new equity has a $3 million carryover basis. If the company exits five years later at $150 million total value and her rolled equity is now worth $45 million, her taxable gain is $45M − $3M = $42 million. Tax is deferred, but not forgiven.

Some sellers use a rollover as a planning tool: by rolling into a private equity-backed entity, they secure tax deferral, and they can time a subsequent liquidity event (secondary sale) to a tax year with lower income or move their residence to a lower-tax jurisdiction before exit (though state restrictions vary).

## Dilution and Follow-On Rounds

If the sponsor raises additional growth capital or arranges secondary transactions (bringing in a co-investor), the seller's rolled equity may be diluted. Seller-friendly deals include anti-dilution language or carve-outs protecting the founder's percentage stake or board representation.

Conversely, if new equity is issued to managers or employees as incentive compensation, the seller's ownership percent declines even if per-share value rises. This is standard post-LBO; the seller must accept dilution or negotiate a carve-out.

## Holdbacks and Escrows

Sponsors typically require the seller to place a portion of the rollover equity (or even the cash proceeds) into escrow, subject to indemnification claims or earn-back schedules. A standard escrow might hold 10–15% of consideration for 12–24 months, releasing funds if no material breaches occur.

For rollover equity specifically, the seller's shares may be held in a voting trust or subject to restricted-stock agreements that vest over time or upon hitting operational milestones. This protects the sponsor from a seller who received equity and immediately departed.
