*A **vendor financing** arrangement is a loan extended by a seller to a buyer as part of a [leveraged buyout](/leveraged-buyout/), allowing the buyer to fund a portion of the purchase price through deferred payments rather than requiring upfront cash or third-party borrowing. The seller effectively becomes a creditor, accepting a note secured by the company's assets in lieu of immediate cash at closing.*

## How vendor financing structures the deal

In a [leveraged buyout](/leveraged-buyout/), the buyer typically assembles a capital stack: equity from investors, debt from banks or bond markets, and subordinated financing. Vendor financing fills a gap in this stack—it substitutes for or supplements third-party [debt financing](/debt-financing/), allowing the buyer to complete the purchase with less equity capital or traditional leverage.

The seller agrees to finance a portion of the [purchase price](/merger/), often 10–30% of the total consideration, payable over 3–7 years. The buyer signs a promissory note; the seller holds security interests in company assets or equity. This arrangement transfers credit risk from a bank (which might decline the deal as too leveraged) to the original owner, who has superior visibility into the business's ability to perform.

## Why sellers accept vendor notes

A seller might accept financing for several reasons. First, it can bridge valuation gaps: if a buyer cannot secure enough third-party capital to meet the seller's price, a vendor note sweetens the deal without the seller cutting price. Second, it defers tax liabilities. If the seller finances a portion of the sale, gains are recognized over the payment period under the installment-sale method, sometimes reducing the immediate tax hit. Third, it demonstrates confidence in the business—implicitly, the seller believes management will generate sufficient [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) to service the debt.

Sellers also use vendor notes to maintain a retained stake in upside. Some arrangements include [contingent payments](/earnout/) or equity kickers, letting the seller participate if the business performs better than projected.

## Risk and subordination

Vendor financing sits low in the capital stack. Banks typically demand that seller notes be subordinated—meaning third-party lenders are repaid first if the company hits financial trouble. This subordination makes vendor notes riskier than traditional bank debt, so sellers demand higher [coupon rates](/coupon-rate/)—typically 8–12%—to compensate.

If the buyout proceeds poorly and the company cannot service all debt, the vendor-financed portion faces write-down risk. The seller, once the business owner, becomes an unsecured or lightly secured creditor with limited recourse, recovering only what remains after senior lenders are satisfied. This transforms the seller's economic interest from equity ownership to a hybrid debt-equity position.

## Vendor financing vs. traditional leverage

Traditional bank [leverage](/leverage-ratio-forex/) has several advantages: it is fungible, scalable, and impersonal. Banks do not interfere with operations. Vendor notes, by contrast, introduce ongoing creditor relationships. Sellers may insist on board representation, covenant compliance (minimum [EBITDA](/ebitda/) levels, [debt-to-equity](/debt-to-equity-ratio/) caps), or veto rights over major decisions.

Yet for buyers facing tight capital markets, vendor financing often unlocks deals that banks alone cannot fund. A moderately leveraged private-equity buyout might layer 40% senior debt, 20% mezzanine debt, 10% vendor financing, and 30% equity, creating a capital structure that feels balanced to all parties.

## Accounting and tax treatment

From an [accounting](/generally-accepted-accounting-principles/) standpoint, vendor financing is a liability. The buyer records the note payable and accrues [interest expense](/interest-coverage-ratio/); the seller records a receivable, recognizing gain as installments are collected. If the note is contingent (tied to earnout metrics), the buyer may initially record the liability at a discounted amount and true it up as payment obligations crystallize.

For tax purposes, both buyer and seller have incentives to allocate the purchase price carefully. The buyer wants to maximize the portion allocated to [depreciable assets](/depreciation/) (equipment, intangibles) and minimize goodwill, to increase future [deductions](/depreciation-recapture-investor/). The seller wants clarity on whether the transaction qualifies for installment-sale treatment. The note's interest rate must be above the IRS-mandated applicable federal rate (AFR) to avoid imputed-interest recharacterization.

## Common pitfalls

Vendor notes can complicate future refinancing. Buyers often seek to repay vendor debt early if they refinance with cheaper bank debt; sellers naturally resist unless the new terms are favorable. Disputes arise over whether the note is truly subordinated (if other lenders later join the capital stack) and whether the buyer is entitled to prepay without penalty.

A vendor note also locks the seller into a long relationship with the buyer. If the business falters, the seller may have to restructure the debt, accept reduced payments, or negotiate a haircut—a familiar headache for former owners reluctant to play creditor.

## When vendor financing alters outcomes

Vendor financing can change incentive alignment. A seller who retains debt exposure has reason to ensure the new owner succeeds; conversely, a buyer who has engineered a highly leveraged structure using vendor debt may be tempted to harvest cash from the business to service the note, starving growth investments. These tensions are resolved through covenants and board oversight.

For institutional buyers managing dozens of add-ons in a large buyout, vendor notes on acquired businesses can aggregate into meaningful portfolio-level debt. Tracking collateral positions, payment schedules, and covenant compliance across many seller-financed subsidiaries demands operational discipline.
