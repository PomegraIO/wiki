*For decades, directors were elected under **plurality voting**: whoever received the most votes won a seat, even if they got only 20% of ballots cast. The shift to **majority voting** — where a director needs over 50% of votes to win — has quietly become the most powerful tool activists wield. It allows them to unseat underperforming directors without mounting a costly proxy contest, and it locks in accountability that management cannot easily sidestep.*

## The Voting Rule Shift and Why It Matters

Under **plurality voting**, a director only needed to receive more votes than any other candidate, regardless of the total. In an uncontested election where shareholders simply voted "yes" on a slate, every director passed automatically with near-unanimous affirmation — even if 30% of voters withheld support. The withholding was a signal but not a veto.

**Majority voting** raises the bar: a director must receive votes from over 50% of shares voted (excluding abstentions and broker non-votes). This is a high threshold in uncontested elections, where withhold campaigns by activists are the primary tool. A director who fails to achieve 50% cannot take office, and the board must address the rejection or face market criticism.

The shift accelerated after the 2008 financial crisis, when shareholders demanded stronger accountability at underperforming companies. By 2015, virtually all S&P 500 companies had adopted majority voting in director elections. Today it is standard practice, even among smaller public companies.

## Mechanics: How Vote Withholding Becomes a Veto

In an uncontested director election under majority voting, there is no formal [proxy fight](/proxy-fight/): the board nominates candidates, shareholders vote, and the tally determines winners.

**Activist mechanics:**
1. Activist investor identifies underperforming or entrenched director(s).
2. Activist launches public [withhold campaign](/proxy-statement/) via public statements, investor meetings, and sometimes media.
3. Retail and institutional shareholders withhold votes for the targeted director(s).
4. If withholding reaches >50%, director(s) fail to achieve a majority and cannot be elected.
5. Board must address the rejection: replace the director before the next meeting, negotiate with the activist, or face ongoing vacancy and credibility damage.

Unlike a formal proxy contest — which requires the activist to nominate a replacement, solicit proxies, and win a head-to-head election — a withhold campaign is cheaper and faster. The activist is not making a nomination; they are simply saying "this person lacks majority support."

## Why Majority Voting Empowers Activists

Plurality voting was activist-proof in practice. Even a director with 25% withholding would be elected, provided no other candidate received more votes. A board could thus weather significant shareholder dissatisfaction as long as alternatives did not emerge.

Majority voting removes that cushion. Sustained withholding below 50% is a warning; withholding above 50% is a removal. This makes director accountability immediate and binary.

**Practical implications:**
- A director with poor attendance, obvious conflicts of interest, or ties to failed management decisions is now vulnerable.
- Boards must periodically assess director performance and market perception, not just assume re-election.
- Activists can target one or two problematic directors without mounting a slate and full proxy contest.
- Executives and boards must maintain dialogue with major shareholders or risk surprise withhold campaigns.

## Plurality Voting: Where It Persists

A small minority of companies still use plurality voting, usually in closed or controlled environments:
- **Controlled companies** where founders or insiders own >50% and withhold campaigns have no practical effect.
- **Real estate investment trusts** ([REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/)) and business development companies, where voting coalitions differ.
- **International companies** listed in the U.S. but incorporated elsewhere, where home-country law may mandate plurality.

Even these exceptions are shrinking. Investor pressure and index-fund policies now favor majority voting across the board, and most companies adopting corporate governance reforms move to majority voting voluntarily.

## Vote-Withholding Strategy and Outcomes

Successful withhold campaigns typically require 25–40% of votes to gain momentum, depending on voter turnout and how widely the campaign is publicized. Once withholding exceeds 40%, institutional investors often join to avoid being on the wrong side of a governance issue.

**Historical examples:**
- Directors at major banks post-2008 faced withholding campaigns after poor performance or overstated risk management.
- Executives at tech companies with aggressive [share buyback](/share-buyback/) programs have been targeted for "poor capital allocation."
- Board members lacking diversity expertise faced withholding when institutional investors (and the public) demanded better representation.

The outcome is not always removal. Sometimes the withholding campaign causes the director to resign voluntarily before the vote, the board preemptively replaces the director, or negotiations result in governance changes (new board committees, term limits, regular succession planning).

## Board Response and Governance Evolution

Most major boards now embed majority voting directly into bylaws, treating it as permanent governance architecture. The board also typically adopts:

- **Regular director assessments** to identify underperformance early.
- **Succession planning committees** tasked with identifying and preparing replacement candidates.
- **"Plurality fallback" bylaws** that state the rule if majority voting is later repealed or contested legally.
- **Investor engagement practices** where directors and the [board of directors](/board-of-directors/) meet regularly with shareholders to understand concerns.

A [board of directors](/board-of-directors/) operating under majority voting is leaner and more responsive than under plurality, because underperforming directors cannot hide behind low withholding numbers.

## Majority Voting and Say-on-Pay

Majority voting in director elections often runs parallel to non-binding [shareholder say-on-pay votes](/say-on-pay/). A director who fails to win election under majority voting often simultaneously faces a failed say-on-pay vote, signaling broad shareholder dissatisfaction with both the person and compensation philosophy.

Some boards have loosened executive compensation in response, while others have replaced compensation committees or hired new advisors after majority voting defeats. The two mechanisms reinforce each other.

## Legal Enforceability and Proxy Access

Majority voting bylaws are binding and legally enforceable once adopted. A board cannot sidestep the result by appointing a director who failed majority voting; the director simply does not take office. This differs from [say-on-pay](/say-on-pay/), which is advisory only.

**Proxy access** (the right for shareholders to nominate directors using the company's proxy) amplifies majority voting by allowing activists not just to withhold votes but to field a replacement. When paired with majority voting, proxy access creates genuine board contestability.
