*A **poison pill trigger threshold** is the ownership percentage—usually between 10% and 20%—that activates a shareholder rights plan and unleashes its dilutive consequences on an acquirer. The choice of threshold is a balancing act: too low, and legitimate large investors cannot build passive stakes; too high, and the deterrent weakens. Most public companies cluster around 15%, a level courts have upheld as reasonable.*

## Why a Threshold Exists at All

A **shareholder rights plan** (the technical name for a poison pill) is a board resolution granting shareholders a special right: if an investor crosses a defined ownership percentage without board approval, existing shareholders can purchase additional shares at a steep discount, massively diluting the acquirer's stake.

But why not trigger at any ownership increase? The answer is practical: passive investors—mutual funds, index funds, institutional holders—accumulate stakes slowly and legitimately. If a pill triggered at 5% or any tiny percentage, it would constantly flip on and off as passive capital naturally adjusted. Courts and investors would see this as pure entrenchment with no legitimate purpose.

The threshold answers: "Below this percentage, we assume you're not attempting a hostile takeover; above it, we assume you are." That boundary is the trigger threshold.

## The Range and Logic

Most public companies set triggers between 10% and 20%, with 15% being the mode:

**10% or below:** Rare today. This was common in the 1980s and 1990s during the hostile takeover wave, but courts increasingly viewed very low thresholds as over-defensive. A 10% threshold might block legitimate activist accumulation. Few modern boards hold the line here; those that do often justify it by citing a volatile sector or a specific bidder concern.

**15% (the standard):** The median and modal threshold across S&P 500 companies. Courts have repeatedly upheld 15% as proportionate and reasonable. At 15%, a large activist investor or a determined strategic buyer can still accumulate a meaningful position without immediately triggering dilution, but the message is clear: cross this line, and the pill activates. The threshold sends a negotiation signal: "You want more than 15%? Talk to us first."

**20% or higher:** Less common but used by companies expecting activist pressure or in sectors prone to strategic consolidation. A 20% threshold gives the board more breathing room before triggering dilution. However, very high thresholds (25%+) courts view skeptically—they look less like a reasonable deterrent and more like a rubber stamp for large stakes.

Some companies use a tiered approach: different thresholds for different scenarios. For example, a lower threshold (10%) if acquisition triggers without board negotiation, and a higher threshold (18%) for a passive accumulation by a non-bidder. This is rare but shows how flexible the trigger can be.

## Historical Evolution of Thresholds

In the 1980s takeover wave, boards set very aggressive thresholds—sometimes 5%—trying to stop any large accumulation. Acquirers quickly learned they could circumvent this by using derivatives, options, or indirect ownership structures, so thresholds rose to keep pace. By the 2000s, courts also began to hold boards accountable: Delaware courts ruled that poison pills must have a legitimate, non-entrenchment purpose, and thresholds that were clearly excessive or anti-shareholder would not survive scrutiny.

The result: thresholds stabilized around 15%. This became the market standard because it is low enough to genuinely deter hostile bidders (crossing 15% openly is a public declaration of intent), while high enough that passive investors and activists can accumulate meaningful stakes without triggering the pill immediately.

## Passive Investors and the Threshold Problem

Major index funds (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street) hold large stakes in most public companies—sometimes 5–15% collectively. If a poison pill's threshold is too low, passive ownership itself becomes a problem.

Most pills exempt passive investors explicitly: if you cross the threshold *but* do not engage in hostile acquisition activity or oppose the board, the pill may not apply to you, or the board may grant a waiver. This is contractual and discretionary—the board decides. In practice, index funds are virtually never targeted by a pill because they are passive and not seeking control.

However, the existence of passive exemptions (often called "grandfathering" clauses) creates room for selective enforcement: a board could theoretically grant a waiver to a friendly accumulator but not a hostile one. For this reason, pills with explicit passive exemptions are generally viewed more favorably by institutional investors than pills without them—they demonstrate the board is willing to distinguish between passive and hostile intent.

## The Trigger Versus the Flip-in

It is important to separate the trigger threshold from the flip-in mechanism. The trigger is the ownership percentage that activates the rights. The flip-in is the *consequence*—usually, existing shareholders (excluding the acquirer) can buy shares at 50% of market value, diluting the acquirer's stake from (say) 25% to 15% instantly.

A company might have a 15% trigger but a flip-in that multiplies existing shareholders' rights by 5 or 10x (depending on when the flip is triggered relative to the acquisition announcement). The higher the multiplier, the more severe the dilution. Most modern pills use a flip-in ratio that cuts the acquirer's stake roughly in half, making continuation of the acquisition economically irrational.

## How Courts Review Triggers

Under Delaware law (which governs most US public companies), poison pills must pass two judicial tests:

**Proportionality.** Is the threshold reasonable relative to the threat? A 15% threshold for a company in a stable industry is proportionate; a 5% threshold might not be. Courts consider the company's history, sector, and specific bidder pressure when reviewing thresholds.

**Legitimacy of purpose.** Is the threshold designed to protect shareholders from coercion or extraction, or is it pure entrenchment? A pill triggered if an acquirer crosses 15% *without board negotiation* is legitimate; a pill that *prevents* board negotiation entirely is not. Boards must show the threshold serves a legitimate corporate purpose, not just insulate management.

The result: most 15% triggers survive court challenge. Much lower or much higher thresholds invite scrutiny.

## Activist Pushback on Thresholds

Institutional investors and activists increasingly scrutinize poison pills and their thresholds. Some large funds vote against pills with thresholds below 15%, arguing they are too stringent. Others argue that in the absence of an active buyback or material capital needs, even a 20% threshold is acceptable—because it still protects the company from creeping accumulation leading to a surprise bid.

The debate has not settled on a single "right" threshold. Instead, the market has converged on 15% as a reasonable middle ground, with variation permitted based on company-specific factors.

## Modern Context and Variations

Some companies have experimented with dynamic thresholds: the trigger percentage adjusts based on market conditions or board circumstances. For example, if a company is already in active negotiation with a potential acquirer, the board might lower the threshold temporarily to prevent a competing bidder from accumulating a stake above the lower bar.

However, courts and shareholders scrutinize dynamic thresholds carefully—they can look like the board is gaming the system. A more transparent approach is for the board to explicitly declare: "We are in talks with Bidder X. Until those talks conclude, we will lower the pill trigger from 15% to 12% to prevent Bidder Y from building a competing position." This is legitimate negotiation leverage; a secret dynamic adjustment is not.
