*A **poison pill ownership threshold** is the percentage of shares an acquirer can buy before a shareholder rights plan is triggered, forcing either a price negotiation or massive dilution of the buyer's stake. Boards typically set this at either 15% or 20%, a choice that reflects competing concerns: lower thresholds activate defenses earlier, but risk seeming too restrictive to ordinary shareholders; higher thresholds give buyers more room, but delay the board's legal leverage.*

## How the Trigger Works

When a shareholder crosses a poison pill's ownership threshold—say, 15%—the board issues new shares to all other shareholders, diluting the acquirer's stake and making the deal uneconomic. The acquirer is *not* entitled to these new shares. So if a buyer reaches 15% and the pill springs, their 15% ownership might instantly fall to 8% or 10%, depending on the total dilution. The buyer now faces a choice: abandon the bid, or negotiate a [deal](/merger/) the board will agree to accept (which typically requires the board to waive the pill).

This dilution is not automatic for all plans; some pills instead allow shareholders other than the acquirer to buy shares at a steep discount. Either way, the threshold is the trip wire.

## 15% vs 20%: The Threshold Debate

### The Case for 15%

A 15% trigger activates the defense earlier, giving the board maximum time and leverage to either:

1. **Negotiate from strength.** Once the pill springs and the acquirer sees their investment diluted, they typically must come to the board with an improved offer to win the right to waive the pill.
2. **Block creeping accumulation.** Buyers sometimes accumulate shares quietly, hoping to avoid triggering defenses by staying just below the threshold. A 15% limit makes this strategy less viable.
3. **Signal vigilance.** Boards that set 15% send a message: "We are serious about negotiating from a position of power."

The downside is perception. Institutional shareholders sometimes view a 15% threshold as overly aggressive, framing it as a board entrenching itself against even modest investor pressure. This can earn a poison pill a reputation as a "king-sized" defense.

### The Case for 20%

A 20% threshold offers a different trade-off:

1. **Appear reasonable.** 20% feels like a natural red line—just below a blocking minority in many voting structures. Boards can argue they are preventing *hostile* control, not squashing legitimate ownership stakes.
2. **Attract long-term investors.** Passive indexers and long-term holders who might hit 5–10% are less spooked; they know they have room to grow without accidentally triggering the pill.
3. **Reduce activist grief.** Activist shareholders and proxy contests sometimes push back against pills with ultra-low thresholds; a 20% threshold can defuse that criticism.

The downside is weaker early-stage leverage. A determined buyer can accumulate up to 19% without the board's pill kicking in, potentially building a visible stake before negotiations must begin.

## Why Not 10% or 25%?

Some companies use 10% or 25%, but they are outliers:

- **10%** is rare in practice. It feels hair-trigger and provokes shareholder outrage at annual meetings. Investors worry even a legitimate 8–9% stake puts them at risk of accidental dilution.
- **25%** was more common in the 1990s but has fallen out of favor. It allows the acquirer so much room that the pill loses its deterrent effect; a 20–24% stake can be accumulated without triggering the defense.

## Setting the Threshold: Board Considerations

In practice, boards balance four factors:

**1. Industry norms.** In sectors where hostile bids are rare (utilities, consumer staples), a 20% or higher threshold is standard and expected. In tech and retail, where takeouts are more frequent, boards tend toward 15% or lower.

**2. Share structure.** If the company has a high outstanding share count or dispersed ownership, the board may favor 20% to make clear that ordinary long-term investors are safe. Conversely, if a few large shareholders already hold 10–15% each, a 15% threshold avoids accidentally triggering the pill for existing friendly investors.

**3. Credit rating and leverage appetite.** A company targeting an [investment-grade bond](/investment-grade-bond/) rating may prefer the optics of a 20% threshold, signaling to rating agencies and debt holders that the firm is open to shareholder engagement.

**4. Activist history.** If the company has weathered proxy contests or activist campaigns, the board's lawyer will advise on the threshold that minimizes future legal challenges while still providing defense.

## Thresholds and Actual Takeover Dynamics

The threshold affects how bidders behave:

- **At 15%.** A buyer facing a 15% pill knows they have limited runway. They often go public with an unsolicited bid immediately or attempt a negotiation once they approach 10–12%, while still below the trigger.
- **At 20%.** A buyer might accumulate to 18–19% before the board takes notice. By then, the investor's visibility can create shareholder pressure on the board to negotiate, even without the pill kicking in.

In practice, most unsolicited bids never reach the trigger percentage. Instead, boards and buyers typically negotiate a [merger agreement](/merger/) well before the acquirer needs to test the pill's mechanics.

## Scope and Time Limits

Most poison pills last 10 years before expiring unless the board actively renews them. Some boards set pills with sunset provisions, or allow the board to redeem the pill if the triggering event (the stock purchase) does not occur within a specified window. The 15% vs 20% choice is independent of duration; both can exist on the same 10-year or longer timeline.

## Shareholder and Investor Implications

For a shareholder considering investment in a company with a poison pill:

- **15% threshold**: You cannot accumulate beyond 14.99% without board discussion. If you want to become a meaningful stakeholder, you must negotiate with management or launch a [proxy fight](/proxy-fight/).
- **20% threshold**: You have more latitude but should still plan to engage with the board if you approach 18–19%.

Importantly, neither threshold prevents a shareholder from voting their shares or attending meetings; a pill only affects the economics of *buying* additional shares past the trigger.
