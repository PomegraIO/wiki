*The **Pay-As-You-Go (PAYGO) rule** is a statutory fiscal constraint requiring that any new [mandatory spending](/mandatory-spending/) or [tax](/corporate-income-tax/) cut be offset dollar-for-dollar by [spending reductions](/discretionary-spending/) or revenue increases elsewhere, preventing legislation from expanding the [federal deficit](/budget-deficit/) without explicit compensating action.*

## The Fiscal Constraint Design

PAYGO is elegantly simple: it treats the [federal budget](/budget-deficit/) like a family [budget](/budgeting-methods/). If you increase spending on one thing, you must cut another or raise income. If you cut [taxes](/corporate-income-tax/), you must cut [spending](/mandatory-spending/) elsewhere or raise other taxes. The rule prohibits legislation from making [deficits](/budget-deficit/) larger without explicit tradeoffs.

The genius lies in its teeth. If lawmakers violate PAYGO, the consequence is not a fine or a vote of no-confidence; it is automatic [sequestration](/appropriations-bill/)—across-the-board cuts to [mandatory spending](/mandatory-spending/) programs, apportioned equally unless [Congress](/appropriations-bill/) has carved out exemptions. This automatic [sequestration](/appropriations-bill/) is politically painful enough that PAYGO acts as a powerful deterrent.

The rule covers only [mandatory spending](/mandatory-spending/) and [tax](/corporate-income-tax/) policy, not [discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending/) (which is governed by annual [appropriations bills](/appropriations-bill/)). [Discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending/) decisions—the defense [budget](/budgeting-methods/), education, infrastructure—are made through separate [appropriations](/appropriations-bill/) with their own rules and constraints.

## How PAYGO Constrains Legislation

Imagine [Congress](/appropriations-bill/) proposes a new paid-leave benefit, costing $100B over 10 years in [mandatory spending](/mandatory-spending/). Under PAYGO, that legislation must identify $100B in offsets. Options include:

- Raising the [payroll tax](/corporate-income-tax/) by X basis points.
- Cutting [Medicare](/mandatory-spending/) reimbursement rates by Y percent.
- Reducing [Social Security](/mandatory-spending/) benefits for higher-income retirees.
- Extending means-testing for existing programs.
- Extending or creating new tax provisions that raise revenue.

Without identified offsets, the bill violates PAYGO and faces automatic [sequestration](/appropriations-bill/) if enacted. In practice, this means the bill cannot pass unless either (a) offsets are added, or (b) [Congress](/appropriations-bill/) explicitly votes to exempt it from PAYGO, which requires a supermajority in some legislative contexts and is politically difficult.

The rule applies to the 5-year and 10-year [budget](/budgeting-methods/) windows. If a tax cut is scored as costing $50B over 5 years but $200B over 10 years, the offsets must cover the 10-year cost.

## Historical Implementation and Lapses

**1990–2002**: PAYGO was enacted as part of the Budget Enforcement Act and is credited with helping reduce [budget deficits](/budget-deficit/) in the 1990s. [Mandatory spending](/mandatory-spending/) growth was constrained, and legislation that expanded [entitlements](/mandatory-spending/) or cut [taxes](/corporate-income-tax/) required hard choices and visible tradeoffs. When PAYGO expired in 2002, [deficits](/budget-deficit/) widened substantially.

**2003–2010**: Without PAYGO, [tax cuts](/corporate-income-tax/) passed without offsets (the [2003 Bush tax cuts](/corporate-income-tax/)), and [Medicare](/mandatory-spending/) expanded (the prescription drug benefit in 2003) without corresponding [spending](/mandatory-spending/) reductions. The [federal deficit](/budget-deficit/) grew, and [debt-to-GDP](/debt-to-gdp-ratio/) ratios rose.

**2010–present**: PAYGO was reinstated in 2010 as part of broader deficit-reduction efforts. However, it has been repeatedly subject to exemptions and carve-outs. Emergency [spending](/mandatory-spending/) measures—stimulus bills, COVID relief, natural-disaster aid—are often exempted from PAYGO. Tax extenders (temporary [tax provisions](/corporate-income-tax/) that are periodically renewed) are sometimes treated as baseline rather than new [spending](/mandatory-spending/), weakening the rule's bite.

## Exemptions and Loopholes

[Congress](/appropriations-bill/) has carved exceptions into PAYGO:

**Emergency designations**: [Spending](/mandatory-spending/) labeled "emergency" can bypass PAYGO entirely. The definition is flexible; during COVID-19, [Congress](/appropriations-bill/) designated trillions in relief [spending](/mandatory-spending/) as emergencies, exempting them from offset requirements.

**Baseline gimmicks**: If a [tax provision](/corporate-income-tax/) is set to expire and [Congress](/appropriations-bill/) extends it, the extension might be scored relative to a hypothetical baseline (in which the provision expires) rather than current law. This creates a "savings" claim even though no new revenue is actually raised. These baseline manipulations weaken PAYGO without formally violating it.

**Supermajority exemptions**: Some proposals require a supermajority vote to override PAYGO. In practice, with narrow [Congressional](/appropriations-bill/) margins, even 60-vote supermajorities are hard to achieve, but they are not insurmountable for high-priority [legislation](/appropriations-bill/).

**Sequestration caps**: [Congress](/appropriations-bill/) has periodically raised the cap on [mandatory spending](/mandatory-spending/) that is exempt from [sequestration](/appropriations-bill/), further weakening the constraint.

## Interaction with Discretionary Spending and Budget Caps

PAYGO operates independently of [discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending/) caps (which are set in [appropriations bills](/appropriations-bill/)). A bill that increases [Medicare](/mandatory-spending/) reimbursement must find offsets under PAYGO, but a bill that increases the defense [appropriations](/appropriations-bill/) must navigate [discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending/) caps instead. This split creates complexity: a major [fiscal policy](/fiscal-consolidation/) change may require compliance with both systems simultaneously.

In some [budget](/budgeting-methods/) years, [discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending/) caps are binding (limiting new defense or domestic [appropriations](/appropriations-bill/)), while PAYGO is loose. In others, the reverse holds. The interaction affects which [policy](/fiscal-consolidation/) lever is available to lawmakers.

## Fiscal Multiplier and Economic Effects

The economic effects of PAYGO are contested. Proponents argue that by constraining [deficit spending](/budget-deficit/), PAYGO reduces [crowding out](/capital-flows/) (private investment displaced by [government debt](/national-debt/)) and keeps [interest rates](/interest-rate/) lower. If the rule prevents wasteful [spending](/mandatory-spending/), it improves [fiscal efficiency](/fiscal-consolidation/).

Critics contend that PAYGO is pro-cyclical. During a [recession](/recession/), when new [spending](/mandatory-spending/) or tax cuts could stimulate [demand](/gross-domestic-product/), PAYGO's offset requirement makes such relief legislatively harder. Lawmakers must choose between stimulating the [economy](/gross-domestic-product/) and complying with PAYGO. In response, PAYGO is typically suspended during crises (hence the emergency exemptions), but the delay between recession onset and [Congressional](/appropriations-bill/) action can be costly.

## PAYGO vs. Structural Deficits

PAYGO is a constraint on new [legislation](/appropriations-bill/), not a tool for closing existing [budget deficits](/budget-deficit/). If the government already runs a [deficit](/budget-deficit/) and [mandatory spending](/mandatory-spending/) is growing faster than [revenue](/budget-deficit/), PAYGO alone cannot fix the imbalance. It only prevents new [legislation](/appropriations-bill/) from making it worse.

Closing a structural [deficit](/budget-deficit/) requires either new [revenue](/budget-deficit/), permanent [spending](/mandatory-spending/) reductions, or both. PAYGO makes it illegal to do either in isolation—you must choose one [policy](/fiscal-consolidation/) path and pay for it—but it does not compel [Congress](/appropriations-bill/) to choose any [policy](/fiscal-consolidation/) at all. If [Congress](/appropriations-bill/) is deadlocked, nothing happens, and [deficits](/budget-deficit/) remain.

## Comparison to Other Fiscal Rules

**Debt brakes**: Some countries (e.g., Germany) use debt-brake rules that limit [government debt](/national-debt/) as a percentage of [GDP](/gross-domestic-product/) and require structural [budget](/budgeting-methods/) balance over the [business cycle](/business-cycle/). These are more stringent than PAYGO but also more rigid.

**[Appropriations](/appropriations-bill/) limits**: The [discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending/) limits enacted in 2011 (the Budget Control Act) set hard caps on [discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending/) for a decade, triggering [sequestration](/appropriations-bill/) if exceeded. This is an operational enforcement mechanism similar to PAYGO but applied to [discretionary](/discretionary-spending/) rather than [mandatory spending](/mandatory-spending/).

**Supermajority requirements**: Requiring a supermajority (60 or 67 votes) to raise [taxes](/corporate-income-tax/) or cut [benefits](/mandatory-spending/) is another constraint approach, though not formally enforceable and subject to parliamentary manipulation.

## The Political Debate

PAYGO remains politically contested. Conservative economists and lawmakers view it as a critical firewall against [entitlement](/mandatory-spending/) expansion and [tax cuts](/corporate-income-tax/) they oppose. Liberal [policymakers](/fiscal-consolidation/) sometimes see it as a constraint on necessary [spending](/mandatory-spending/) and argue that during downturns, automatic exemptions are justified.

The practical effect of PAYGO is to make new [spending](/mandatory-spending/) or [tax cuts](/corporate-income-tax/) politically expensive: you cannot pass one without explicitly choosing what to cut. This is, in principle, the intent. Whether this constraint improves [fiscal outcomes](/budget-deficit/) or merely slows needed [policy](/fiscal-consolidation/) change is an empirical question with no consensus answer.
