*A **crowding out** effect occurs when government borrowing raises [interest rates](/interest-rate), making it more expensive for private business and households to borrow for investment and consumption. The rise in government demand for credit "crowds out" private borrowing, reducing private [investment](/stock) and partially offsetting the stimulus from government spending.*

## How crowding out works

When the government runs a large [budget deficit](/budget-deficit), it must borrow money by issuing Treasury bonds. This increases demand for credit. If the supply of credit is limited, [interest rates](/interest-rate) rise.

Higher [interest rates](/interest-rate) make borrowing more expensive for:

- **Businesses:** Higher [interest rates](/interest-rate) reduce the return on [investment](/stock) projects, so fewer projects are undertaken.
- **Households:** Higher [interest rates](/interest-rate) increase mortgage costs, car loan costs, and other borrowing costs, reducing consumption and home purchases.

The result: Government [deficit](/budget-deficit) spending is partially offset by reduced private spending, reducing the net stimulus to the economy. The [fiscal multiplier](/fiscal-multiplier) is smaller than it would be without crowding out.

## When crowding out is strongest

Crowding out is strongest when:

**[Interest rates](/interest-rate) are flexible:** If [interest rates](/interest-rate) move easily with changes in supply and demand (as they do in normal times), government borrowing will raise [interest rates](/interest-rate) substantially.

**Credit supply is inelastic:** If there is a limited amount of loanable funds available, government borrowing bids up [interest rates](/interest-rate) sharply.

**[Investment](/stock) is sensitive to [interest rates](/interest-rate):** If businesses reduce [investment](/stock) plans significantly when [interest rates](/interest-rate) rise, crowding out effects are large.

## When crowding out is weak

Crowding out is weak when:

**[Interest rates](/interest-rate) are at the zero bound:** During severe [recessions](/recession), [interest rates](/interest-rate) are already at or near zero and cannot fall further. In this scenario, government borrowing does not need to raise [interest rates](/interest-rate) to attract credit; there is simply more credit available. Crowding out effects are minimal.

**Central bank accommodates:** If the [central bank](/central-bank) increases the money supply in response to government borrowing (called "monetizing" the deficit), [interest rates](/interest-rate) need not rise. Crowding out is prevented.

**Credit supply is elastic:** If lenders are willing to provide much more credit at only slightly higher [interest rates](/interest-rate), government borrowing causes [interest rates](/interest-rate) to rise only modestly, minimizing crowding out.

**[Investment](/stock) is insensitive:** If businesses do not reduce [investment](/stock) plans much when [interest rates](/interest-rate) rise (e.g., if [investment](/stock) is driven by other factors like animal spirits or technological opportunity), crowding out effects are small.

## Crowding out vs. crowding in

Paradoxically, government spending can sometimes **encourage** private [investment](/stock) — a phenomenon called [crowding in](/crowding-in). This occurs if:

- Government spending on infrastructure (roads, ports, utilities) makes private [investment](/stock) more profitable.
- Government spending boosts [aggregate demand](/central-bank) and optimism, encouraging businesses to invest.
- Government spending expands the size of the market, allowing businesses to achieve greater economies of scale.

In these cases, the stimulus from government spending is amplified, not reduced, by private [investment](/stock) responses.

## Empirical evidence

The strength of crowding out has been heavily debated and empirically studied. Findings vary:

**Short-run studies:** In normal times with positive [interest rates](/interest-rate), government borrowing does appear to raise [interest rates](/interest-rate) modestly, suggesting some crowding out.

**Recession studies:** During severe [recessions](/recession), crowding out appears minimal or absent, as suggested by theory.

**Monetary policy response:** When central banks accommodate government borrowing by expanding money supply, crowding out is reduced or prevented.

Most evidence suggests that crowding out is real but partial — it reduces (but does not eliminate) the effectiveness of fiscal stimulus.
