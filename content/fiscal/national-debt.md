*The **national debt** is the total stock of money a government has borrowed from investors, foreign governments, and its own agencies. It accumulates whenever a [budget deficit](/budget-deficit/) forces the government to borrow, and shrinks only when [budget surpluses](/budget-surplus/) allow debt repayment.*

## How national debt accumulates

A [budget deficit](/budget-deficit/) means the government spends more than it collects in revenue. To pay its bills, it must borrow. The US government borrows by issuing Treasury securities — bonds, notes, and bills that investors buy. Each year the government runs a [deficit](/budget-deficit/), it adds that shortfall to the [national debt](/national-debt/). The debt stock is the cumulative sum of all past deficits minus all past surpluses.

In the US federal system, total [national debt](/national-debt/) has two components:

- **[Debt held by the public](/debt-held-by-the-public/):** Treasury securities owned by individuals, corporations, foreign governments, and institutional investors. This is the part that matters for creditor risk and [interest rate](/interest-rate/) dynamics.
- **[Intragovernmental debt](/intragovernmental-debt/):** Money the federal government has borrowed from its own trust funds (primarily Social Security). These are internal IOUs; the government owes the money to itself in a sense.

Together, these constitute the total [national debt](/national-debt/).

## Why national debt matters

National debt matters for three reasons:

**Interest payments.** As the [national debt](/national-debt/) grows, the government must pay [interest](/interest-rate/) on it. These [interest payments](/interest-rate/) are [mandatory spending](/mandatory-spending/), rising automatically as debt grows or [interest rates](/interest-rate/) climb. At some point, interest payments can dominate the [budget](/budget-deficit/), crowding out spending on [discretionary programs](/discretionary-spending/) like defense or infrastructure.

**Crowding out private investment.** When the government borrows heavily, it bids up [interest rates](/interest-rate/), making borrowing more expensive for businesses and households. This can reduce private [investment](/stock/) and growth — a phenomenon called [crowding out](/crowding-out/).

**Fiscal sustainability.** If [national debt](/national-debt/) grows faster than the economy, the [debt-to-GDP ratio](/debt-to-gdp-ratio/) rises. Eventually, a rising ratio can trigger a fiscal crisis: investors lose confidence, [interest rates](/interest-rate/) spike, the government cannot refinance, and [sovereign default](/sovereign-default/) looms.

## National debt in practice

The US [national debt](/national-debt/) has grown nearly every year since the 1980s. It surged during major wars (World War II, the 2001–2014 wars in Iraq and Afghanistan) and financial crises (the 2008–2009 Great Recession, the COVID-19 pandemic). The [debt-to-GDP ratio](/debt-to-gdp-ratio/) is the more meaningful metric, as it adjusts for inflation and economic growth.

Other developed nations have different debt levels. Japan has the highest [debt-to-GDP ratio](/debt-to-gdp-ratio/) among large economies, yet borrows at very low [interest rates](/interest-rate/) because investors believe in repayment. Emerging markets with weaker institutions often face much higher borrowing costs at lower debt levels.

## Debt vs. deficit

Confusion often clouds the distinction:

- **[Budget deficit](/budget-deficit/):** The annual shortfall (revenues minus spending). It is a flow: how much the government borrowed *this year*.
- **[National debt](/national-debt/):** The cumulative stock. It is a level: how much the government has borrowed *in total*.

The relationship: Change in [national debt](/national-debt/) = [Budget deficit](/budget-deficit/) + adjustments (principal repayment, asset sales, etc.).
