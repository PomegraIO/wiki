*An **austerity** policy involves deliberate cuts to government spending and/or increases to taxes with the goal of reducing the [budget deficit](/budget-deficit) and slowing [national debt](/national-debt) growth. Austerity is typically pursued during fiscal crises or when [debt-to-GDP ratios](/debt-to-gdp-ratio) become unsustainable.*

## Forms of austerity

**Spending cuts:** Reduce [discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending) on defense, infrastructure, education; reduce [mandatory spending](/mandatory-spending) on [entitlements](/entitlement-spending); reduce public sector employment.

**Tax increases:** Raise income taxes, value-added taxes, excise taxes, or other revenues.

**Mixed:** Combine spending cuts and tax increases.

## Austerity in practice

**Portugal (2010–2014):** Implemented IMF-mandated austerity including spending cuts and tax increases. Result: deep [recession](/recession), 15% [unemployment](/central-bank), but eventual return to growth.

**Greece (2010–2015):** Even more severe austerity (40%+ cuts to public sector wages, large tax increases). Result: severe [depression](/recession), 25%+ [unemployment](/central-bank), political upheaval.

**Spain (2012–2014):** Austerity measures. Result: initial deepening of [recession](/recession), eventual recovery as conditions improved.

**US (2013):** [Sequestration](/sequestration) and fiscal tightening during weak growth. Result: slower growth, but manageable.

## The austerity debate

Economists fiercely debate austerity's effectiveness and costs:

**Pro-austerity argument:**
- Large [deficits](/budget-deficit) and [debt](/national-debt) are unsustainable.
- Austerity is necessary to rebuild creditor confidence, lower [interest rates](/interest-rate), and avoid [fiscal crisis](/sovereign-default).
- Short-run pain (reduced growth) is worth long-run stability.

**Anti-austerity argument:**
- Austerity is contractionary, reducing demand, employment, and growth.
- [Fiscal multipliers](/fiscal-multiplier) are large; austerity reduces output more than the deficit reduction.
- During weak growth, austerity makes things worse, not better.
- Better to wait for growth to improve before tightening.

Evidence is mixed. Some studies show that austerity during [recessions](/recession) deepens downturns; others show it can be necessary to prevent [fiscal crisis](/sovereign-default) and can accelerate growth recovery if creditor confidence improves enough.

## Austerity and credibility

A key channel: austerity can improve credibility with creditors and markets.

If a government implements aggressive austerity, [interest rates](/interest-rate) on its [debt](/sovereign-debt) may fall as investors gain confidence. This can provide short-term relief, partially offsetting austerity's contractionary effect.

If austerity is credible and [interest rates](/interest-rate) fall sharply, the growth drag from austerity can be reduced or eliminated.

If credibility is not gained (growth falls but [interest rates](/interest-rate) don't), austerity's costs are borne without offsetting benefits.

## Austerity vs. stimulus

Austerity is the opposite of fiscal stimulus:

**Stimulus:** Increase spending or cut taxes to boost demand and growth (but widen deficit).

**Austerity:** Cut spending or raise taxes to reduce deficit (but slow growth).

The right policy depends on context: during [recessions](/recession) with slack demand, stimulus is often preferred; during [inflation](/inflation) or unsustainable debt, austerity is more appropriate.

## Austerity and politics

Austerity is extremely unpopular:

- Spending cuts reduce public services and employment.
- Tax increases reduce household incomes.
- [Unemployment](/central-bank) rises.
- Electoral punishment is severe.

As a result, austerity is typically pursued only when forced (IMF conditions, [fiscal crisis](/sovereign-default) pressures, or when the alternative — default — is worse).
