*A **contractionary fiscal policy** is when government reduces spending or raises taxes to cool aggregate demand and reduce [inflation](/inflation) or [budget deficits](/budget-deficit). It narrows the [budget deficit](/budget-deficit) but can slow growth and increase [unemployment](/central-bank) in the short run.*

## How contractionary policy works

When aggregate demand is excessive — pulling the economy above its potential capacity, or when [inflation](/inflation) is rising — the government can cool demand by:

**Cutting [discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending):** Government buys fewer goods and services, reducing demand directly.

**Raising taxes:** Households and businesses have less income to spend, reducing demand indirectly.

**Reducing [transfer payments](/transfer-payment):** Government sends less money to [entitlement](/entitlement-spending) recipients, reducing their spending.

All of these narrow the [budget deficit](/budget-deficit) — government spends less or collects more revenue. The goal is to reduce demand-pull [inflation](/inflation) and cool an overheating economy.

## Short-term pain vs. long-term stability

Contractionary policy comes with a cost: it slows growth and can raise [unemployment](/central-bank). This is why contractionary policy is politically difficult. Voters dislike slower growth and rising [unemployment](/central-bank), even if inflation is falling.

However, if inflation is not contained, the long-run damage can be severe — [inflation](/inflation) erodes savings, distorts [investment](/stock) decisions, and forces the [central bank](/central-bank) to raise [interest rates](/interest-rate) dramatically to suppress it. Contractionary fiscal policy combined with [central bank](/central-bank) tightening can contain inflation while distributing the burden.

## Contractionary policy in practice

**Early 1980s:** Contractionary fiscal policy (budget cuts) combined with the Fed's tight monetary policy broke the high [inflation](/inflation) of the 1970s but triggered a severe [recession](/recession).

**2010–2015:** Many developed countries pursued [austerity](/austerity) (contractionary fiscal policy) to reduce [deficits](/budget-deficit) accumulated during the 2008–2009 crisis. This slowed recovery in Europe and elsewhere.

**Post-COVID:** As [inflation](/inflation) spiked in 2021–2022, some countries considered contractionary policies to cool demand while central banks raised [interest rates](/interest-rate).

## When is contractionary policy appropriate?

Contractionary policy works when:

**[Inflation](/inflation) is high:** Demand is exceeding supply; reducing demand cools prices.

**[Interest rates](/interest-rate) are high:** The [central bank](/central-bank) is tight; fiscal contraction helps it avoid even tighter (and more disruptive) monetary policy.

**[Budget deficits](/budget-deficit) are unsustainable:** Government debt is out of control and markets are losing confidence; reducing the deficit is necessary to stabilize debt.

Contractionary policy is less appropriate when there is slack in the economy (high [unemployment](/central-bank)), because it will worsen the employment situation without much benefit.

## The [fiscal multiplier](/fiscal-multiplier) in contraction

The negative effects of contractionary policy depend on the [fiscal multiplier](/fiscal-multiplier). If the multiplier is 1.5, a $1 billion spending cut reduces GDP by $1.5 billion. Multipliers are larger during [recessions](/recession) (when spare capacity amplifies the negative effect) and smaller during full employment.

This is why contractionary policy is most acceptable when the economy is at or above full capacity. If applied during a [recession](/recession), it can deepen the downturn.
