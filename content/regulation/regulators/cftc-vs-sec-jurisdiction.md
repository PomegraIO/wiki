*The **CFTC** (Commodity Futures Trading Commission) regulates [futures contracts](/futures-contract/), swaps, and commodity derivatives; the **SEC** (Securities and Exchange Commission) regulates securities and security-based derivatives. The line between them was clarified by the Dodd-Frank Act but remains contested for crypto assets and hybrid products, creating regulatory uncertainty and overlapping claims of authority.*

## The foundational split: commodities vs. securities

The regulatory divide between the CFTC and SEC traces to different statutory mandates rooted in different eras. The SEC, created in 1934 during the Great Depression, was designed to police securities markets—ensuring disclosure, preventing fraud, and protecting investors. Stocks, [bonds](/bond/), and later mutual funds fell squarely in its purview.

The CFTC, created in 1974 during agricultural and commodity-price volatility, oversees the trading of [futures contracts](/futures-contract/) on agricultural, energy, metal, and financial commodities. Its mandate emphasizes price discovery and prevention of market manipulation in commodity derivatives. The split reflects a philosophical divide: the SEC protects investors in securities; the CFTC protects price integrity and market efficiency in derivatives.

Because futures and swaps are derivative instruments—contracts whose value depends on an underlying asset—rather than direct ownership claims, Congress and the agencies have treated them as a separate regulatory domain. A person who buys a stock is a *securities investor* and receives SEC investor protections. A person who enters a crude-oil futures contract is a commodities trader and receives CFTC oversight.

## CFTC jurisdiction: futures, options on futures, and swaps

The CFTC regulates exchange-traded futures contracts, which are standardized contracts to buy or sell a commodity (or financial index) at a fixed price on a future date. It also regulates options on those futures, swaps (bilateral agreements to exchange cash flows), and security-based [swap](/swap/) agreements that do not fall to the SEC.

The CFTC licenses and oversees futures exchanges (like the Chicago Mercantile Exchange), clearinghouses that guarantee performance, futures commission merchants (brokers), commodity pool operators (collective investment managers), and commodity trading advisers. It sets position limits to prevent excessive speculation, requires disclosure of large positions, and polices market manipulation.

Because swaps are customized and typically trade over-the-counter, the CFTC established a registration framework for swap dealers and major market participants. Swap dealers must register with the CFTC and comply with capital and margin rules. This framework, largely completed by 2013 after Dodd-Frank, brought previously unregulated derivatives trading into view.

## SEC jurisdiction: securities and security-based derivatives

The SEC regulates securities—equity shares, [bonds](/bond/), investment company shares (including ETFs and mutual funds)—and derivative contracts whose underlying asset is a security or security index. This includes options on stocks, [call options](/call-option/) on bond futures (when they exist), and swaps whose reference is a single security or broad security index.

The SEC enforces disclosure rules (companies must file 10-Ks, quarterly reports), antifraud rules, and insider-trading prohibitions. It oversees broker-dealers, investment advisers, and exchanges. When a derivatives product is deemed "security-based," the SEC has jurisdiction even if it trades over-the-counter or looks like a swap.

For example, if you trade an option contract on Tesla stock, that falls under SEC jurisdiction. If you trade a swap referencing the S&P 500 index, the SEC and CFTC both claim partial jurisdiction (because the S&P 500 is a broad index, not a single security). This overlap creates practical friction and legal uncertainty.

## The Dodd-Frank clarification and ambiguity

After the 2008 financial crisis, Congress passed the Dodd-Frank Act to bring swaps—blamed for fueling the crisis—under regulatory light. The Act gave the CFTC authority over most interest-rate swaps, commodity swaps, and credit-default swaps, with a key exception: the SEC retained authority over "security-based swaps" (swaps on individual securities, narrow security indexes, or groups of securities).

Dodd-Frank also required central clearing and exchange trading of standardized swaps, created the swap-dealer registration regime, and imposed margin and position-limit rules. The CFTC's role expanded dramatically, making it co-equal with the SEC in many respects.

However, the definition of "security-based swap" remains contentious. Is a swap on a narrow-based equity index a security-based swap? What about a swap on a cryptocurrency? The ambiguity has led to regulatory gray zones and occasional turf battles.

## Cryptocurrency and the gray zone

Crypto assets present the most acute jurisdictional conflict. The SEC treats [Bitcoin](/bitcoin/) as a commodity (under CFTC jurisdiction) when traded as a spot asset, but treats many tokens as securities (under SEC jurisdiction) when they function as investment contracts. If you trade Bitcoin [futures](/futures-contract/), the CFTC oversees the market; if you trade security tokens, the SEC does.

Derivatives on crypto are even more tangled. A Bitcoin futures contract falls to the CFTC. But if a decentralized platform offers leveraged token swaps or synthetic derivatives linked to cryptocurrency, neither agency has clear authority, and both have tried to claim it. Some regulators argue that staking derivatives or yield-bearing tokens are securities; others argue they are commodities. This legal ambiguity has slowed innovation and led to enforcement actions against platforms that did not register or comply with one agency's rules.

## Institutional and practical overlaps

Both agencies have some overlapping responsibilities. They both regulate position limits (the CFTC on futures and swaps, the SEC on certain derivatives). They both enforce anti-manipulation rules. And they both cooperate on cross-asset investigations—for instance, if a trader manipulates both the Treasury-bond futures market (CFTC) and the Treasury-bond spot market (SEC), both agencies become involved.

In practice, major financial institutions maintain separate compliance programs for CFTC and SEC rules, duplicate reporting, and sometimes contradictory guidance. A large bank's derivatives desk may operate under CFTC rules for commodity swaps and SEC rules for equity swaps simultaneously, with different capital and margin requirements for each.

## How the boundary affects market participants

For an investor, the distinction is crucial. If you buy a stock or a fund, you receive SEC investor protections: mandatory disclosure, fraud liability, and a regulatory framework designed to protect retail buyers. If you trade futures, you enter a less paternalistic CFTC regime that assumes sophistication and emphasizes transparent price discovery and position disclosure.

For a dealer or broker, the boundary determines which licenses, capital rules, and compliance programs apply. A firm that wants to trade commodity swaps must register with the CFTC; a firm that trades security-based derivatives must register with the SEC. Many firms do both.

For a technology platform or decentralized application offering derivatives, the ambiguity creates real risk. If regulators later deem a product a "security-based swap," the platform may face enforcement action and be required to register retroactively—a costly and sometimes impossible task.
