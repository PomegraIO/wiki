*The **Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC)** is the independent U.S. federal regulator governing [futures contracts](/wiki/futures-contract/), [options](/wiki/option/), and derivatives tied to commodities, currencies, and financial instruments. Established in 1974, it polices fraud, enforces [position limits](/wiki/position-limit-regulations/), and promotes market integrity across [exchanges](/wiki/stock-exchange/) and [over-the-counter](/wiki/over-the-counter-market/) (OTC) venues.*

## Authority and jurisdiction boundaries

The CFTC's mandate spans futures and derivatives where commodities—crude oil, wheat, copper—underlie the contract, plus financial futures and [swaps](/wiki/swap/). Notably, the [Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)](/wiki/securities-and-exchange-commission/) shares authority over equity and debt derivatives, creating occasional friction. [Swap execution facilities](/wiki/swap-execution-facility/) and [clearinghouses](/wiki/central-counterparty-clearing/) register with the CFTC, which sets [initial margin](/wiki/initial-margin/) and [variation margin](/wiki/variation-margin/) standards alongside [clearing member risk](/wiki/clearing-member-risk/) rules.

## Position limits and speculation controls

A cornerstone of CFTC oversight is capping how much [notional exposure](/wiki/position-limit-regulations/) single traders can accumulate in key commodities. These limits prevent [market manipulation](/wiki/market-surveillance/) and [flash crashes](/wiki/flash-crash-2010/). Exemptions exist for commercial hedgers—farmers using [futures](/wiki/futures-contract/) to lock in crop prices—but speculative traders face hard caps. The CFTC enforces these through surveillance of [clearing firm](/wiki/clearing-firm/) reports.

## Enforcement and anti-fraud authority

CFTC [enforcement actions](/wiki/cftc-enforcement-action/) target insider trading, fraudulent solicitation, and price manipulation. The agency investigates spoofing (placing then canceling orders to fake demand), layering (multiple deceptive orders), and wash trades. Penalties range from disgorgement and civil fines to criminal referrals. The [Dodd-Frank implementation](/wiki/dodd-frank-implementation/) significantly expanded CFTC power over OTC derivatives, including new registration and reporting requirements.

## Swap regulation and mandatory clearing

[Dodd-Frank](/wiki/dodd-frank-act/) tasked the CFTC with regulating standardized [interest-rate swaps](/wiki/interest-rate-swap/), [credit-default swaps](/wiki/credit-default-swap/), and commodity swaps. Regulated [swap dealers](/wiki/swap-execution-facility/) must meet capital requirements, position reporting, and customer protection rules. Standardized swaps face mandatory clearing through [central counterparties](/wiki/central-counterparty-clearing/), reducing counterparty risk. Complex bilateral swaps remain less regulated, though [enhanced due diligence](/wiki/enhanced-due-diligence/) and reporting apply.

## Position transparency and market surveillance

The CFTC collects [Commitments of Traders](/wiki/market-risk/) reports disclosing large positions by category—commercials, non-commercials (speculators), and index funds. This transparency tools helps distinguish genuine hedging from speculative accumulation. Large traders file position reports to the CFTC, which monitors for concentration that might signal manipulation. During commodity [spikes](/wiki/black-monday-1987/), the CFTC investigates whether financial speculation or fundamental supply shocks drove prices.

## Relationship with international regulators

The CFTC coordinates with [European regulators](/wiki/european-banking-authority/), the UK [Financial Conduct Authority](/wiki/financial-conduct-authority/), and others on [derivatives](/wiki/credit-derivative/) standards. [Dodd-Frank international equivalence](/wiki/dodd-frank-international-equivalence/) provisions allow CFTC to recognize foreign regulatory regimes as equivalent, reducing duplicative compliance. Cross-border [swap](/wiki/commodity-swap/) transactions and [forward contracts](/wiki/forward-contract/) still face jurisdictional gray zones, particularly when trades occur on non-U.S. [exchanges](/wiki/cme-group/).

## Cryptocurrency and digital assets

The CFTC claims authority over crypto derivatives—Bitcoin and Ethereum [futures](/wiki/currency-futures/) and [options](/wiki/option/)—when they trade on registered venues. This splits oversight: futures fall under CFTC; underlying spot markets may escape federal jurisdiction if they operate as unregistered [money transmitters](/wiki/fiat-money/). The CFTC has prosecuted major [crypto exchange](/wiki/cryptocurrency-exchange/) operators for operating unregistered derivatives platforms.
