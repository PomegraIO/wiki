*The **National Futures Association** (NFA) is the industry-run regulator of US [futures contract](/futures-contract/) participants, enforcing standards for brokers, commodity trading advisors, and pool operators below the federal [Commodity Futures Trading Commission](/regulators/#cftc). Neither fully private nor fully governmental, the NFA delegates practical compliance work to a body nominated by industry members but subject to CFTC oversight.*

## The self-regulatory model in futures

The NFA is one of a handful of major [self-regulatory organizations](/securities-and-exchange-commission/) (SROs) in US finance—a quirk of delegated oversight that traces to the 1970s futures boom. Rather than creating a massive federal futures police force, Congress delegated the National Futures Association's creation to the [CFTC](/regulators/#cftc), which granted it authority to police its own members. This arrangement saves the government money and leans on the assumption that industry participants themselves are motivated to keep bad actors out and maintain investor confidence. In reality, it means the NFA is a hybrid creature: industry-funded and industry-nominated, but operating under strict CFTC rules and subject to appeals, reversals, and oversight when the CFTC deems the NFA too lenient or corrupt.

## Membership and jurisdiction

The NFA's reach covers four broad classes of market participants. [Futures commission merchants](/futures-contract/)—the brokers who execute trades and hold customer margin—must register with the NFA and submit to routine audits. Commodity trading advisors and commodity pool operators, who manage accounts or pools of customer money on a discretionary basis, face similar membership requirements and record-keeping scrutiny. More recently, the NFA absorbed authority over retail forex dealers and leverage traders, extending its net to the riskier, less-regulated corners of the leveraged derivatives market.

Membership is not free. The NFA collects registration fees (scaled by firm size and business line) and ongoing annual dues, plus an assessments from fined members. This creates a perverse incentive structure: the NFA's revenue partly depends on catching wrongdoing, which can create pressure to police members aggressively—or, cynically, to ignore quiet rule-breaking in favour of high-profile public cases that justify the fee base.

## Rule-making and enforcement

The NFA publishes its own rulebook—a thick document governing ethical conduct, capital adequacy, record retention, and trading practices—which sits atop federal CFTC rules. When a broker violates either the NFA rulebook or CFTC regulations, the NFA initiates disciplinary proceedings: a formal investigation, a notice of charges, and often an arbitration hearing before an NFA-appointed panel.

Sanctions range from warnings and fines to suspension or permanent bar from the industry. Unlike the [SEC](/securities-and-exchange-commission/), which prosecutes fraud criminally through the Justice Department, the NFA's arsenal is mostly civil—fines, restitution, and license revocation. Serious criminal breaches (theft, outright fraud) are still referred to federal prosecutors, but the NFA handles the bulk of garden-variety compliance failures: inadequate capital, sloppy record-keeping, unsuitable customer advice, or biased order execution.

The NFA also runs an arbitration system for customer disputes. A customer who believes a futures broker stole their margin or executed a trade with reckless disregard can file a claim with the NFA, which appoints arbitrators—often retired industry veterans—to hear the case and award damages. This is faster and cheaper than federal court but can favour the broker if arbitrators are too cosy with the industry.

## Conflicts and criticism

The self-regulatory model has long been controversial. The NFA's members are both the regulated and the regulators: futures commission merchants dominate the board and nominating committee, creating an obvious incentive to go easy on larger, well-connected firms. Smaller brokers and independent traders complain that NFA rules are enforced more aggressively against them than against the Too-Big-To-Regulate futures divisions of banks like [JPMorgan Chase](/jpmorgan-chase/) or [Goldman Sachs](/goldman-sachs/).

The 2008 financial crisis exposed another vulnerability: the NFA's capital and liquidity checks, while tighter than they'd been in the 1990s, failed to catch the systemic risks brewing inside large futures merchants. [Lehman Brothers](/regulators/#lehman) operated under NFA jurisdiction until it collapsed, and the NFA's post-mortem was widely criticized as too little too late. More recently, the NFA has been pushed by the CFTC to harden stress-testing and [counterparty-risk](/counterparty-risk/) assessments, especially after the 2022 FTX implosion raised questions about whether self-regulators can monitor crypto derivatives venues in real time.

## Relationship to the CFTC

The CFTC retains a veto power that keeps the NFA nominally honest. The CFTC approves the NFA's rulebook amendments, can overturn NFA disciplinary decisions on appeal, and conducts periodic audits of the NFA's enforcement practices. In practice, the CFTC's small staff (a few hundred people covering a multi-trillion-dollar futures market) relies heavily on the NFA to do the grunt work: routine exams, first-line investigations, and minor disciplinary cases.

This division of labour works reasonably well as long as the NFA and CFTC align on priorities. But when they diverge—say, the NFA wants to loosen margin requirements to help smaller brokers while the CFTC fears systemic risk—the relationship can fray. The CFTC can ultimately force compliance, but it must expend political capital and court challenges to do so.

## Global context

Futures and derivatives markets exist worldwide, and most major jurisdictions now have their own SROs or quasi-SROs. The UK's futures dealers once answered to the NFA (leveraged retail forex traders still do if they operate in both markets), but now fall under [prudential regulation](/prudential-regulation-authority/) and conduct rules from the Financial Conduct Authority. Japan's self-regulatory bodies for futures and options are weaker than the NFA and more tightly controlled by the Ministry of Finance. This patchwork means that a derivatives trader working across borders may face multiple, sometimes conflicting, compliance regimes—a complexity that the NFA itself navigates by maintaining mutual recognition agreements with overseas regulators.
