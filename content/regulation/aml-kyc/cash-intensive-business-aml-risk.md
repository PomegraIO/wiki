*Cash-intensive businesses—those that process large volumes of small bills and coins with minimal documentary backing—trigger heightened AML risk profiles because their transaction patterns can obscure the true source and destination of funds. Banks and compliance officers treat laundromats, car washes, restaurants, and similar operations as higher-risk customers, requiring deeper due diligence and continuous monitoring.*

## Why cash-intensive businesses attract regulatory attention

The core issue is **transaction opacity**. A laundromat processing $50,000 per month in coins and small bills generates minimal documentary evidence of legitimacy—no invoices, no supplier contracts, no clear supply chain. By contrast, a software company that deposits $50,000 in the form of customer invoices and purchase orders provides a verifiable audit trail.

Money launderers have long exploited this opacity. A criminal enterprise can commingle illicit proceeds with legitimate cash revenue, making it nearly impossible for a teller or compliance officer to discern what portion of a day's deposits came from illicit activity. Because cash-intensive businesses operate at the intersection of legitimacy and vulnerability, regulators and banks treat them as inherently higher-risk customers.

This risk is not theoretical. The FinCEN guidance and [Credit Risk](/credit-risk/) assessments issued by federal banking agencies explicitly list laundromats, casinos, car washes, and restaurants as "high-risk" or "high-risk as a category" businesses. The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) similarly identifies these sectors in its risk warnings.

## How cash-intensive customers trigger enhanced due diligence

When a business owner applies for a bank account, the compliance team conducts **Know Your Customer (KYC)** screening. For a cash-intensive business, this process goes deeper:

- **Background verification**: Personal credit reports, litigation history, sanctions list checks (OFAC, FinCEN, etc.)
- **Source of funds**: Proof that the business owner has legitimate sources of wealth; e.g., prior employment records, property ownership history
- **Business legitimacy**: Lease agreements, utility bills, licenses, tax filings, proof of customer volume
- **Beneficial ownership**: Identification of all true owners, including hidden partners or shell-company investors
- **Ongoing relationship review**: More frequent re-screening, lower tolerance for anomalies

Some banks require cash-intensive customers to undergo **Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD)**, which adds another layer: site visits, interviews with management, review of operational controls, and validation of the reported transaction volumes.

## Transaction monitoring and red flags

Once a cash-intensive account is open, compliance teams watch for patterns that suggest mingling of illicit proceeds:

- **Volume spikes**: Deposits that exceed the customer's historical average or documented business capacity
- **Round-dollar transactions**: Particularly round amounts (e.g., exactly $10,000 deposits on consecutive days) suggest intentional structuring or [Structuring vs Smurfing: Key Differences](/structuring-vs-smurfing-difference/)
- **Off-hours deposits**: Deposits outside normal business hours or patterns
- **Third-party involvement**: Cash brought to the bank by people other than the registered owner, without clear business reason
- **Frequency clustering**: Deposits clustered to avoid $10,000 reporting thresholds
- **Geographic anomalies**: Cash deposits in cities where the business has no presence

When these patterns emerge, the bank files a [Suspicious Activity Report (SAR)](/suspicious-activity-report-threshold/) to FinCEN, even if the transaction itself is below any fixed dollar threshold.

## Banking-industry response and friction

The upshot for cash-intensive business owners is friction:

1. **Account termination**: Banks increasingly close accounts of laundromats, car washes, and similar businesses preemptively, citing AML risk.
2. **Higher fees**: Some accounts carry elevated monthly maintenance fees or per-deposit charges.
3. **Transaction limits**: Banks may cap the monthly dollar volume deposited into the account.
4. **Clearing delays**: Deposits may be held for extended clearing periods while compliance teams verify legitimacy.
5. **Frequent re-screening**: Annual or quarterly [KYC](/perpetual-kyc-vs-periodic-review/) refreshes become standard, requiring updated documentation.

For legitimate business owners, this creates a real compliance burden. A car wash owner must document not just their deposits, but the business model underlying them: customer volume, pricing, operating hours, and staffing. Failure to do so triggers account closures or SAR filings.

## The tension between risk management and financial inclusion

Regulators and banks face a genuine tension. Cash-intensive businesses are **legitimate** and economically important—laundromats, restaurants, and car washes employ millions and serve essential markets. Yet the same characteristics that make them legitimate also make them vulnerable to abuse.

Some jurisdictions have experimented with **risk-based proportionality**: recognizing that a $2,000-per-month laundromat has a fundamentally different risk profile than a $500,000-per-month casino. This approach allows compliance teams to apply reasonable controls without wholesale account closure.

The challenge is scalability. A small bank with limited compliance staff may find it operationally easier to close all laundromat accounts than to develop a nuanced risk model for each one. Larger banks with dedicated AML teams can afford more tailored approaches.
