*A dollar earning 8% in a tax-sheltered [401(k)](/401k-plan/) accumulates differently than the same dollar earning 8% in a taxable brokerage account. **Asset location strategy** is the practice of placing each investment in the account type that minimizes lifetime taxes on that specific holding—a lever for adding 0.5% to 1.5% in annual returns without changing your portfolio's overall risk.*

## Asset allocation vs. asset location

Many investors conflate two distinct concepts:

- **Asset allocation** answers *what* you own: the ratio of stocks, bonds, real estate, and alternatives.
- **Asset location** answers *where* you hold it: the account structure.

A 60-40 stock-bond portfolio is a choice of allocation. Whether you place that 60-40 mix identically across your [401(k)](/401k-plan/), [IRA](/traditional-ira/), and taxable brokerage is a location choice. The location decision can cost or gain you tens of thousands over a career while leaving your fundamental risk exposure unchanged.

## Tax efficiency of different investments

Different securities generate different tax consequences:

**Bonds and bond funds** produce mostly ordinary income (interest payments). In a taxable account, that interest is taxed at your marginal income-tax rate—up to 37% federally. Inside a [401(k)](/401k-plan/) or [IRA](/traditional-ira/), the interest accrues tax-deferred; you pay no tax until withdrawal (and in a Roth, never). Bonds should therefore live in tax-advantaged accounts whenever possible.

**Growth stocks and equity funds** that generate [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) are more tax-efficient in taxable accounts. Long-term [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) are taxed at 15–20%, well below ordinary-income rates. Moreover, if you hold a growth stock until death, your heirs receive a "step-up in basis"—they inherit at the market price, avoiding tax on all accumulated gains. This is a massive tax advantage unique to taxable accounts.

**High-dividend stocks and funds** pay taxable dividends. In taxable accounts, [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/) (from US stocks held 60+ days) are taxed at long-term capital-gains rates (15–20%), whereas non-qualified and international dividends face ordinary income tax (up to 37%). A bond [ETF](/etf/) that distributes 4% annual income (taxed as ordinary income) is painful in a taxable account; a stock [ETF](/etf/) that distributes 1.5% in qualified dividends is tolerable.

**[Real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/) (REITs)** produce mostly ordinary income, much of it depreciation recapture. They are tax monsters in a taxable account and should live in a [401(k)](/401k-plan/) or [IRA](/traditional-ira/).

## The core location framework

A basic strategy places these investments where they do the most good:

| Investment Type | Best Location |
|---|---|
| Bonds, bond funds, [bond ETFs](/bond-etf/) | [401(k)](/401k-plan/), [IRA](/traditional-ira/), or Roth [IRA](/traditional-ira/) (tax-deferred) |
| [REITs](/real-estate-investment-trust/) | [401(k)](/401k-plan/) or [IRA](/traditional-ira/) (shelter ordinary income) |
| High-turnover active funds | [401(k)](/401k-plan/) or [IRA](/traditional-ira/) (avoid short-term capital-gains tax drag) |
| Growth stocks, [index funds](/index-fund/), low-turnover [ETFs](/etf/) | Taxable account (benefit from long-term capital-gains rates and step-up basis) |
| [Dividend-paying stocks](/dividend-yield/) (qualified dividends) | Taxable account or [IRA](/traditional-ira/) (either works; depends on other holdings) |
| Tax-loss harvesting candidates | Taxable account (losses offset gains; cannot harvest in [IRAs](/traditional-ira/)) |

## Practical constraints

Most workers cannot optimize location perfectly because their [401(k)](/401k-plan/) investment menu is limited. If your employer's plan offers only a few [stock ETFs](/equity-etf/) and a bond fund, you cannot place all bonds inside the 401(k) if you want a globally diversified portfolio. In that case, you compromise: max out the bond fund inside the [401(k)](/401k-plan/) and place remaining bonds in a taxable account or [IRA](/traditional-ira/).

Similarly, if you have limited [IRA](/traditional-ira/) contribution room, you must choose between sheltering high-dividend stocks or bonds. Most experts say bonds win—sheltering 4% annual interest beats sheltering 1.5% annual dividends.

Another constraint is the "need to sell" problem. A stock that has a large unrealized capital gain should stay in a taxable account to preserve the step-up basis for heirs, but if you need that cash before death, you face a large tax bill. Conversely, placing a stock with a big loss in a taxable account lets you [harvest](/tax-lot/) that loss against other gains—a valuable option.

## Interaction with rebalancing

Asset location becomes especially powerful during rebalancing. If your target allocation is 60-40 (stock-bond), a market rally that drives it to 65-35 requires rebalancing. If you rebalance within tax-advantaged accounts ([401(k)](/401k-plan/) or [IRA](/traditional-ira/)), there is no tax. But if you sell appreciated stock in a taxable account to buy bonds, you trigger a capital-gains-tax bill.

A tax-aware rebalancer would instead:

1. Sell bonds inside the [401(k)](/401k-plan/) and buy stock (no tax).
2. Leave the taxable account alone.
3. Redirect new contributions (match, 401(k) deferrals) into bonds.

This achieves the same 60-40 target without triggering a tax event. Over decades, this discipline adds up.

## The Roth factor

A [Roth IRA](/traditional-ira/) or [Roth 401(k)](/401k-plan/) is tax-free growth. This makes it uniquely valuable for high-growth, high-turnover investments and for young workers in low tax brackets. A 25-year-old earning $50,000 putting growth-stock [ETFs](/etf/) into a Roth [IRA](/traditional-ira/) will pay minimal tax today and zero tax on decades of compounding. A 55-year-old in a high bracket might instead use a traditional [401(k)](/401k-plan/) for bonds (sheltering high ordinary-income tax rates) and keep growth stocks in taxable accounts (for capital-gains rates and step-up basis).

## Catch-up contributions and location

Older workers using [catch-up contributions](/catch-up-contribution/) can aggressively shelter bonds and REITs in a [401(k)](/401k-plan/), reducing late-career ordinary-income tax burden. This is especially powerful for someone entering a higher tax bracket in their final working years.
