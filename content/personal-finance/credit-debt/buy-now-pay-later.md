*Buy now, pay later (BNPL) is an installment lending service offered directly at checkout, splitting a purchase into fixed payments without traditional credit card fees or interest—at least on the surface. It has grown into a mainstream alternative to credit cards, yet operates in a regulatory shadow that exposes borrowers to risks their card-using peers typically avoid.*

## How BNPL works differently from credit cards

When you swipe a credit card, the card issuer fronts the full amount to the merchant and you owe the issuer on your next statement. If you pay in full, you owe nothing extra. If you carry a balance, you pay interest. The credit card company assumes the risk of non-payment and profits from interest, annual fees, and merchant interchange.

BNPL inverts this slightly: the lender again fronts the merchant's funds immediately, but the consumer never sees a credit card. Instead, you authorize a series of automatic payments—typically four equal instalments due every two weeks. You commit to the payment schedule at purchase; there is no revolving balance to pay down whenever you choose. The lender's profit comes from a merchant fee (2–8% of the transaction), not from consumer interest, at least nominally. This absence of interest is BNPL's core marketing angle: no APR, no hidden finance charges.

Yet the comparison breaks down on closer inspection. A credit card offers a grace period if you pay the full statement balance on time. BNPL offers no such courtesy; miss a scheduled instalment and you incur a late fee, often £5–$20. Accumulate enough missed payments and the lender may refer you to a debt collector or report the delinquency to [credit reporting](/form-8949/) agencies—but only some BNPL lenders do this, and inconsistently.

## The credit reporting gap

The most significant hidden cost is not financial but informational. Most [credit card](/credit-risk/) issuers report your payment history to the three major credit bureaus ([Equifax](https://www.equifax.com/), Experian, TransUnion in the US). Every on-time payment builds your [credit rating](/credit-rating/); missed payments damage it for years. This creates accountability and a historical record.

Most BNPL lenders do not report to credit bureaus—neither positive nor negative payment history. This means your flawless payment record with Klarna or Affirm does nothing for your creditworthiness in the eyes of a mortgage lender. Conversely, if you default, the lender may refer you to collections without ever warning your credit score, leaving you blindsided when you later apply for a car loan.

Some newer entrants (including major bank-backed offerings) have begun reporting to bureaus, but this remains the exception. The regulatory vacuum here is intentional: many BNPL lenders operate as fintech companies, not banks, skirting the credit reporting and consumer protection rules that bind traditional lenders.

## Interest, late fees, and the cost illusion

BNPL's zero-interest pitch is technically accurate but misleading. You pay zero interest *if* you make every payment on schedule. Miss one, and you face a late fee plus potential accrued interest—some lenders charge 0–40% APR on delinquent balances. The terms are often buried in the app's fine print, discovered only when a payment fails.

Merchants, meanwhile, pay a hefty commission: 2–8% per transaction is nearly triple what they pay for credit card processing (which typically runs 1.5–3%). This cost is usually passed to consumers via higher prices at BNPL-friendly retailers. So the "no interest" promise obscures the reality that the service is subsidized by markup, not by lender benevolence.

For merchants, BNPL is attractive because it increases average transaction size (consumers spend more when splitting payments) and reduces cart abandonment. For consumers, the appeal is less clear if they already have a 0% APR credit card or sufficient cash. But for those without a credit card—or with a maxed-out limit—BNPL can feel like the only option at checkout.

## Fraud and merchant liability

Because BNPL lenders rely on soft [credit checks](/credit-risk/) (often no check at all) and proprietary underwriting, they accept higher fraud risk. A stolen identity or bogus purchase is technically a consumer dispute, but BNPL platforms vary widely in how they handle claims. Credit card networks have strict chargeback rules; BNPL platforms have no such standard. You may find yourself arguing with a fintech company rather than calling a bank's fraud department.

Merchant chargebacks, too, differ. If a customer disputes a BNPL transaction, the merchant may lose both the goods and the funds already paid by the BNPL lender. Chargeback rates for BNPL are higher than for traditional cards, putting pressure on smaller retailers.

## Debt accumulation and overspending

BNPL's invisibility at checkout creates psychological friction loss. Credit cards come with a statement; you see the total monthly damage. BNPL splits expenses across invisible future instalments, making it easy to accumulate multiple small debts across different lenders without a clear aggregate picture. A £100 purchase at one BNPL lender, £80 at another, and suddenly you owe £300+ across four platforms—none of it appearing on a traditional credit report or in a unified dashboard.

Surveys consistently find that BNPL users report higher overspending and debt stress than credit card users. The mechanism is not unique to BNPL (instalment lending has this risk), but the regulatory blind spot compounds it: debt collectors and credit bureaus often move slower to intervene.

## Regulatory status and future tightening

As of recent years, regulators in the US, UK, and EU have begun examining BNPL, citing consumer protection gaps. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has opened inquiries into deceptive marketing and data practices. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority proposed rules to bring BNPL under consumer credit regulation. None of these proposals have yet fully closed the regulatory gap, but the trajectory is clear: BNPL's era of minimal oversight is ending.

Some jurisdictions now require BNPL lenders to conduct [affordability checks](/budgeting-methods/), report to credit bureaus, and provide transparent terms—moving BNPL closer to traditional lending. This tightening will raise costs for consumers and reduce the profit margins that have fuelled BNPL's growth.
