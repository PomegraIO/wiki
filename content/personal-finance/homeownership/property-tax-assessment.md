*A **property tax assessment** is a periodic valuation of residential or commercial property by a local government assessor, intended to establish the tax base for annual property tax bills. The assessed value, multiplied by the local millage or tax rate, determines how much tax the owner owes.*

## How assessments are conducted

Local assessors—employees of county or municipal tax assessment offices—estimate property values using one or more standardized methods. The most common is the **sales comparison approach**, in which the assessor examines recent arm's-length sales of comparable properties in the same neighbourhood, adjusting for differences in size, condition, age, and amenities. A home that sold for €500,000 six months ago in a similar area serves as a benchmark; the assessor adjusts that comparable sale up or down based on specific features of the subject property.

The **cost approach** constructs a value estimate by taking the land value, adding the cost to rebuild the structure as new, and then subtracting accrued depreciation. This method works well for newer homes where reliable construction cost data exists, but struggles with older buildings where depreciation estimates become speculative.

The **income approach** capitalizes net rental income. If a rental property generates $2,000 per month in rent and local cap rates (the relationship between net operating income and property value) suggest a 5% return, the assessor might estimate the property value at $480,000. This method is most common for multi-family buildings and commercial property, less so for single-family homes.

## The role of assessed value and mill rates

The assessed value is not necessarily the market value—the price at which the home would sell today. In many jurisdictions, assessments lag behind market movements by a year or more, and some states cap annual assessment increases to a fixed percentage regardless of actual price movement. This creates a wedge between what a buyer actually paid and what the assessor values the property at for tax purposes.

From the assessed value, the local taxing authority applies a **mill rate** (or tax rate), usually expressed in mills per dollar of assessed value. A mill is one-thousandth of a dollar. If the mill rate is 15 mills and the assessed value is €200,000, the annual property tax is (15 ÷ 1000) × €200,000 = €3,000. Mill rates vary dramatically by location; wealthy suburbs often have low mill rates, while fiscally stressed municipalities impose high ones.

## Assessment cycles and notice

Most jurisdictions conduct full reassessments every three to five years, though some assess annually. When an assessment is completed, the assessor's office mails a notice to the property owner showing the assessed value. This notice is the owner's first opportunity to identify an error. If the assessed value seems grossly inflated—especially if the assessor has used comparable sales far outside your neighbourhood or made obvious mistakes about square footage or major improvements—an appeal may be warranted.

The notice also specifies a deadline for filing an appeal, often 30 to 60 days. Missing this deadline forecloses the right to challenge the assessment for that cycle, so owners must act promptly.

## The appeals process

The typical appeal follows a two-step process. First, the owner files a formal challenge with the local assessor or county board of review, presenting evidence that the assessed value is excessive. This evidence might include recent comparable sales showing lower values, a professional appraisal, photos of defects or deferred maintenance, or a recent purchase price substantially below the assessment. The assessor or board then reviews the submission and either adjusts the value downward or stands by the original assessment.

If dissatisfied, the owner can escalate to the county [tax](/interest-rate/) tribunal or state-level board of equalization, a quasi-judicial body that holds hearings and issues binding rulings. At this stage, owners often hire a property tax consultant or attorney. The threshold for winning an appeal is usually that the assessed value exceeds the property's "true cash value" (the market value at which it would sell between willing buyer and seller, neither under pressure).

## Why assessments matter and common disputes

Property taxes represent a significant cost of homeownership, often exceeding $3,000–$5,000 annually in high-tax states. A €500 reduction in assessed value can save €75–€100 per year in taxes (at a 15-mill rate), and that saving compounds over decades. Aggressive assessment appeals can substantially improve long-term ownership economics.

Disputes arise most frequently in rising markets. As home prices climb, assessors catch up to new values and assessments spike, even though owners did nothing to improve their property. An owner who bought for €300,000 and watches their assessment jump to €380,000 (and tax bill rise accordingly) often feels unfairly penalized for a passive price appreciation driven by neighborhood demand.

Assessors sometimes also make mechanical errors—recording square footage incorrectly, failing to account for a new roof or HVAC system, or misidentifying the property type (rating a single-family as a multi-family). These errors are usually correctable and worth challenging.

## Limitations and fairness critiques

Property tax assessment is inherently subjective. Two appraisers can examine the same home and arrive at different market-value estimates. Assessment offices with understaffing or poorly trained staff may produce assessments that miss genuine comparables or double-count improvements. Wealthier homeowners with resources to hire consultants appeal more often and successfully, while lower-income owners may never challenge an inflated assessment, effectively paying a higher tax rate on the same income.

Some jurisdictions have introduced **computer-assisted mass appraisal** (CAMA) systems that apply algorithms to comparable sales data and property characteristics to generate consistent, defensible assessments at scale. These reduce human bias but also obscure judgment calls and can mismatch nuanced local conditions.
