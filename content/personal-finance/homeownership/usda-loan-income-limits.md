*The **USDA loan income limits** are maximum household earnings that borrowers must not exceed to qualify for a USDA-guaranteed mortgage. The limits vary by county and household size, typically ranging from 80% to 115% of the area median income, and are designed to target the program toward moderate-income rural homebuyers.*

## Purpose and Structure of Income Limits

The USDA's guaranteed loan program, administered by Rural Development, aims to help moderate-income households buy homes in rural areas and small towns where credit is scarce. Because the program is explicitly designed for borrowers with limited means, income ceilings protect the program's subsidy from benefiting wealthier households. Unlike conventional mortgages, there is no upper income limit for loan amount—only for eligibility itself.

The income limits are not uniform across the country. The USDA sets them at a percentage of each county's area median income (AMI). Most counties use 80% AMI as the cutoff, though some use 90% or 115%, depending on local economic conditions and program policy. The limits account for household size; a four-person family has a higher limit than a one-person household in the same county. This escalation recognizes that larger families typically require higher gross income to afford housing relative to their size.

## How Limits Are Calculated and Updated

The USDA typically releases new income limits in February each year, based on the prior year's median income data from the US Census Bureau and other sources. Each county's limits are published in tables that cross-reference household size (1 to 8 members, with 8+ lumped together). To find the limit for a specific property, borrowers need to know the county of the property address—not their current address.

For a household to qualify, **all income sources are counted**: wages, salary, self-employment, rental income (net after expenses), Social Security, pensions, alimony, and child support. Some income types require averaging or documentation. Self-employed income is averaged over the prior two calendar years; an applicant cannot simply use recent high earnings if prior years were lower.

The USDA is conservative with non-wage income. Income that will expire or end before the mortgage is satisfied (certain contracts, seasonal work, unemployment benefits) may be excluded or discounted. Applicants should bring tax returns, W-2s, and verification of employment (VOE) letters from their lender.

## County-by-County Variation

Income limits can differ dramatically across counties. In a rural county with a lower median income, the 80% AMI limit might be $65,000 for a family of four. In a more prosperous county adjacent to a metropolitan area, the same family size might have a limit of $110,000 or higher. This county-level variation is why a borrower's income that exceeds limits in one county might qualify in another.

The USDA also allows lenders some discretion. Some counties near growing metros are classified as experiencing rapid urbanization; the USDA may grant temporary higher limits (often 115% AMI) to prevent the program from disappearing in areas losing their "rural" character. Lenders stay informed of these exceptions through USDA announcements and updates.

## Comparison to FHA and Conventional Limits

Unlike USDA loans, FHA mortgages and conventional loans have no income ceiling—only debt-to-income ratios and credit score requirements affect qualification. An applicant earning $500,000 annually can obtain an FHA loan if their [debt-to-equity ratio](/debt-to-equity-ratio/) and credit profile qualify. USDA's strict income limit is a key trade-off: the program offers lower down payments (0%) and lower [interest rates](/interest-rate/) in return for targeting moderate-income buyers.

This structural difference means USDA loans are not competitive for higher-income households, even if they intend to buy in an eligible rural area. That is by design—the program's subsidy (the government guarantees losses if the borrower defaults) is meant to unlock homeownership for those with fewer alternatives, not to reduce costs for wealthy households.

## Verifying Eligibility

Before applying, borrowers should verify two things: income and property location. Income verification is straightforward—run the numbers against the county table. Property eligibility is less obvious. Not all areas labeled "rural" by the USDA qualify; the agency has specific maps showing eligible areas and ineligible areas within rural counties. A property address on the border of an urban zone or within a city limits may be ineligible even if the broader county qualifies.

The USDA maintains an online property eligibility checker where borrowers can enter an address and receive immediate feedback. Many lenders also perform this check before processing an application to avoid dead-end paperwork. If a property is ineligible, the loan cannot proceed—income and credit cannot compensate for location.

## Life After Approval

Once a USDA loan closes, income limits do not apply to ongoing ownership or refinancing. If a borrower's income rises above the limit years later, they retain the loan and can refinance into conventional or FHA products if rates move favorably. The restriction is for entry only, not for life.

However, some USDA loan products include [prepayment penalties](/prepayment-risk/) or restrictions on refinancing (depending on the specific loan guarantee product), so borrowers should understand the terms at closing.
