*A **grantor retained annuity trust** (GRAT) is an irrevocable trust that allows a donor to transfer appreciating assets to heirs while paying little or no [gift tax](/estate-tax/). The donor retains annuity payments for a fixed term; if assets grow faster than an IRS discount rate, the appreciation passes to beneficiaries tax-free.*

## How a GRAT Is Structured

The GRAT works through a precise sequence:

1. **Funding**: The grantor (usually a parent or high-net-worth individual) transfers assets—typically growth stocks, real estate, or private equity—into an irrevocable trust.

2. **Annuity term**: The trust makes fixed annuity payments back to the grantor for a set period, typically 2–10 years, but sometimes decades. These payments are calculated as a level percentage of the initial trust value.

3. **IRS hurdle rate**: The IRS publishes a monthly Section 7520 rate used to value the trust for gift-tax purposes. The grantor is treated as having received a gift only to the extent that assets in the trust are expected to exceed the value needed to fund those annuity payments, discounted at the hurdle rate.

4. **Remainder to heirs**: Any trust assets remaining after the annuity period ends pass to designated beneficiaries (children, grandchildren, or a secondary trust) with no further gift or [estate tax](/estate-tax/).

## The IRS Discount Rate and Why It Matters

The critical mechanism is the 7520 rate, which adjusts monthly. When the rate is low (say, 2%), the value of the annuity payments the grantor is "getting back" is treated as larger for gift-tax purposes, leaving less to pass gift-tax-free. When the rate is high (say, 5%), the same annuity payments are discounted more sharply, so more of the initial transfer is treated as a gift to heirs.

**The GRAT bet**: A grantor funds a GRAT when they expect assets to grow faster than the 7520 rate. If assets outperform the hurdle rate, that excess growth passes to heirs untaxed. If assets underperform, the grantor simply receives all or most of their original contribution back through annuity payments, and heirs receive little or nothing—a benign failure.

### Worked Example

Suppose a grantor:
- Funds a 2-year GRAT with $1 million in high-growth stock.
- The 7520 rate at funding is 3%.
- The GRAT makes two equal annual payments of approximately $505,000 each, calculated so that their present value at 3% equals $1 million.

If the stock appreciates to $1.5 million over two years:
- The grantor receives the two $505,000 payments (totaling $1.01 million), recovering nearly the entire initial investment.
- The remaining $500,000 (the appreciation) passes to heirs gift-tax-free.
- No gift tax is owed on the original transfer, because the value of the annuity payments received nearly covered the initial $1 million gift.

If the stock falls to $800,000:
- The grantor still receives approximately $1.01 million in annuity payments (or the remaining trust assets if insufficient).
- Heirs receive little or nothing.
- The GRAT has failed, but there is no tax penalty for the grantor.

## Gift Tax and the Initial Transfer

A crucial detail: the grantor may still owe [gift tax](/estate-tax/) on the initial transfer to the trust, depending on the excess of the initial funding amount over the present value of the annuity payments owed back. However, this excess is typically small or zero if the 7520 rate is high and the annuity term is short.

Many GRATs are structured as "zeroed-out GRATs"—where the annuity value exactly equals the initial funding, leaving no taxable gift. This is legal and common when interest rates are higher, making the annuity value larger.

## Mortality Contingency and Sequencing

One risk: if the grantor dies before the annuity term ends, the remaining annuity payments revert to the grantor's estate for tax purposes, and heirs may not receive the appreciated assets tax-free. This is why GRATs are typically short-term (2–5 years) to reduce mortality risk, and why the strategy works best for younger or healthier grantors.

Some planners structure multiple short-term GRATs in sequence ("GRAT laddering"), allowing a single grantor to fund many trusts over time, each betting on outperformance.

## Advantages and Trade-offs

**Advantages**:
- Prospective wealth transfer with minimal or zero gift tax.
- Benign failure: underperformance does not penalize the grantor.
- Works with volatile, high-growth assets where appreciation is uncertain but hoped-for.
- Remains available even after the [annual gift tax exclusion](/gift-tax/) is exhausted.

**Trade-offs**:
- The trust is irrevocable; the grantor cannot change beneficiaries or retrieve assets early (except through annuity payments).
- If assets underperform the hurdle rate, the tax-planning goal fails, and heirs receive little.
- Requires upfront legal fees and annual trust accounting.
- Mortality risk means short-term GRATs are more common, reducing the benefit for assets with very long growth horizons.

## Modern Context

GRATs have become controversial with policymakers, as critics argue they allow wealthy families to transfer fortunes to heirs with minimal taxation. Proposals to restrict or eliminate GRATs appear periodically in tax reform discussions. Current law permits them, but future changes are possible.

Practitioners also note that the benefit varies with interest rates. When the 7520 rate is low, GRATs are less attractive because annuity payments are smaller, so more of the initial gift is taxable. When rates are high, GRATs are highly attractive. This rate sensitivity has made GRAT popularity cyclical.

## When a GRAT Makes Sense

A GRAT is typically most valuable when:
- The grantor expects specific assets to outperform considerably over the near term (2–10 years).
- The grantor wants to transfer wealth but lacks or has exhausted gift-tax exemption room.
- The grantor is young enough that the mortality risk is acceptable.
- The 7520 rate is favorable (relatively high, making the annuity value large and the taxable gift small or zero).
- The grantor can afford to lose access to the principal for the term, since the trust is irrevocable.
