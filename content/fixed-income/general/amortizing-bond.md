*An **amortizing bond** returns its principal to investors in regular scheduled payments throughout the life of the issue, rather than in one lump sum at maturity. This repayment structure shapes both the bond's cash flow profile and its price behaviour, making it distinct from traditional bullet bonds.*

## How the repayment schedule works

An amortizing bond's [coupon payment](/coupon-payment/) remains constant, but each payment includes an increasing fraction of principal. Early payments are weighted toward interest; later payments shift toward principal. This mirrors a residential mortgage: a borrower makes equal monthly payments, but the early cheques are mostly interest, whilst later cheques retire more principal each month.

The [indenture](/bond/) specifies the exact schedule at issuance. A 20-year amortizing bond might retire 5% of its face value annually, or follow a steeper curve that front-loads principal in the final five years. Investors receive a detailed [prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) showing the payment calendar.

## Why issuers favour amortization

From the issuer's perspective, amortizing bonds lower [refinancing risk](/refinancing-risk/) and default pressure. Borrowing institutions—particularly mortgage lenders and asset-backed securitizers—use amortization to match their own cash inflows. A bank holding mortgages naturally collects principal from homeowners; issuing amortizing [mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/) lets it pass that repayment straight to bondholders, reducing funding mismatches.

Amortization also signals creditworthiness. An issuer willing to retire debt steadily looks more disciplined than one pushing all repayment to the final day. [Credit rating](/credit-rating/) agencies factor in the amortization profile when assessing default risk.

## Prepayment risk and reinvestment traps

The chief investor peril is [prepayment risk](/prepayment-risk/). If interest rates fall, a homeowner (or the underlying borrower) has incentive to repay the mortgage early. The bondholder suddenly receives a return of capital sooner than expected, usually when new bonds offer lower [coupon rates](/coupon-rate/). The investor must [reinvest](/reinvestment-risk/) the proceeds in a lower-yield environment.

Conversely, if rates rise, prepayments slow. The bond holder extends further out into a high-rate future—not necessarily catastrophic, but it ties capital into a fixed-rate instrument whilst yields elsewhere have climbed. This extension risk mirrors [call risk](/callable-bond/) on corporate bonds.

## Amortizing bonds versus bullet bonds

A traditional [bullet bond](/bond/) pays no principal until maturity; all coupons are paid, then the full face value returns on the final day. A 30-year bullet bond gives the borrower 30 years before any principal must be repaid. Issuers benefit from this extended runway; investors bear reinvestment and [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) over the full period.

Amortizing bonds front-load principal recovery. An investor in a 20-year amortizing bond has returned, say, 60% of capital by year 10. This shortens the effective [duration](/duration/) of the instrument and reduces the risk that inflation or rising rates will erode the bond's real value over its full term.

## The mortgage-backed securities connection

[Mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/) are perhaps the most familiar amortizing bonds to retail investors. When a mortgage lender bundles residential home loans and sells them to investors, the resulting securities pass through monthly principal and interest payments from the mortgages. The bondholder receives a steady cash flow, front-weighted toward interest, gradually retiring the principal.

Agency mortgage-backed securities—those backed implicitly or explicitly by [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/) or [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/)—carry government support, lowering credit risk, but [prepayment risk](/prepayment-risk/) remains acute in a low-rate environment.

## Market price dynamics and [duration](/duration/)

Because amortizing bonds return capital early, they have lower [duration](/duration/) than equivalent bullet bonds. Lower [duration](/duration/) means less price sensitivity to [interest-rate](/interest-rate/) changes. A 20-year amortizing bond might behave like a 10-year bullet bond in terms of price volatility, since half its cash is returned by year 10.

This quality appeals to investors aiming to match future liabilities. A pension fund needing to pay benefits in five years can buy an amortizing bond and expect a large portion of its capital back on schedule, reducing the guesswork around reinvestment timing.

However, amortizing bonds also carry asymmetric payoff risk. If rates fall sharply, prepayments accelerate, capping upside price appreciation. If rates rise, prepayments slow and [duration](/duration/) extends—locking in a lower starting yield for longer. This negative [convexity](/option/) is characteristic of [mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/).

## The tax and accounting angle

For taxable bondholders, amortizing bonds offer a steady stream of [principal](/stock/) recovery, which is tax-free, interspersed with interest payments, which are ordinary income. This can be gentler on [cash flow](/cash-flow-statement/) than a bullet bond, where all principal comes at once and must be immediately reinvested.

Accountants and portfolio managers must track the [amortization](/amortization/) schedule closely. The [cost basis](/cost-basis/) is reduced with each principal payment, and the [holding period](/holding-period/) for [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) tax purposes runs from purchase to eventual maturity, not from the first principal repayment.
