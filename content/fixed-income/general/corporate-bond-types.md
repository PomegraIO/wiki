*Corporate bonds come in distinct flavors, each defining the creditor's claim in the event of [default](/wiki/sovereign-default/). The hierarchy runs from senior secured (first in line) to convertible bonds (with equity upside). Understanding these types is essential for [fixed-income-fund-strategy](/wiki/fixed-income-fund-strategy/) and [credit-risk](/wiki/credit-risk/) assessment.*

| Type | Seniority | Collateral | Typical Yield | Risk Level |
|------|-----------|-----------|---|---|
| **Senior Secured** | 1st | Pledged assets | Lower | Lowest |
| **Senior Unsecured** | 2nd | None | Medium | Medium |
| **Subordinated** | 3rd+ | None | Higher | Higher |
| **Convertible** | Variable | Often unsecured | Hybrid | Equity-linked |

## Senior Secured Bonds

Senior secured bonds are backed by specific assets—real estate, equipment, or subsidiary stock. If the company fails, secured bondholders have a [lien](/wiki/security-token/) on those assets and claim them before unsecured creditors. This collateral dramatically lowers [default-risk](/wiki/default-rate/), allowing companies to offer lower coupons.

Secured bonds are common in industries with tangible assets: airlines (backed by aircraft), real estate investment trusts ([mortgage-backed-security](/wiki/mortgage-backed-security/)), and utilities (power plants, pipelines). A secured bond rating might be investment-grade even if the company's overall [credit-rating](/wiki/credit-rating/) is junk, thanks to the collateral.

The trade-off: if the company deteriorates, the secured assets may be seized—a messy [liquidation](/wiki/liquidation/). The bondholder recovers principal but forgoes future [coupon](/wiki/coupon-payment/) payments during restructuring.

## Senior Unsecured Bonds

Senior unsecured bonds have no collateral but rank ahead of subordinated debt in the repayment pecking order. They are the default for investment-grade borrowers (rated BBB– and above by S&P, or equivalent). Most corporate [bond](/wiki/bond/) issuance falls into this category.

Senior unsecured investors rely on the company's overall ability to pay, not specific assets. If the company files for [bankruptcy](/wiki/lehman-brothers-bankruptcy/), senior unsecured holders split the proceeds after secured creditors and bankruptcy expenses are paid. In a typical workout, senior unsecured recovers 40–70 cents per dollar.

Because they lack collateral, these bonds carry higher [credit-spread](/wiki/credit-spread/) over [treasury](/wiki/treasury-bond/) rates than secured debt. But they offer more [liquidity](/wiki/liquidity-risk/) since most corporate [bond-market-liquidity](/wiki/bond-market-liquidity/) volume trades in senior unsecured names.

## Subordinated Bonds

Subordinated bonds rank below senior debt in the repayment hierarchy. If the company enters [liquidation](/wiki/liquidation/), subordinated creditors wait until senior creditors are paid. This subordination can be explicit—"subordinated to senior debt of more than $500 million"—or structural.

Subordinated bonds typically carry [junk-bond](/wiki/junk-bond/) ratings and offer materially higher yields to compensate for lower recovery odds. A subordinated bond rated BB might yield 6–8%, compared to 3–4% for a senior unsecured name at the same company.

Subordinated bonds are popular with equity-like investors seeking higher returns and can convert to stock in some structures. [Contingent-convertible](/wiki/mandatory-convertible/) bonds (CoCos) are a modern variant: they convert to equity if the bank's capital ratio falls below a threshold, creating a [tail-risk](/wiki/tail-risk/) scenario for bondholders.

## Convertible Bonds

A convertible bond is a [hybrid](/wiki/hybrid-reit/) security: it pays a coupon like a regular bond but can be converted into a fixed number of shares at the bondholder's option. The conversion price is typically set above the current stock price when issued; the bondholder profits if the stock rallies.

Convertibles are issued by companies wanting to raise capital at lower coupons. A company might issue a convertible at 4% when its straight senior debt would yield 6%, because investors pay a premium for the [embedded-option](/wiki/option-adjusted-spread/) on the upside.

From a bondholder's perspective, convertibles offer [protection](/wiki/protective-put/): if the stock stalls, the bond acts like a regular fixed-income instrument with a [coupon-payment](/wiki/coupon-payment/); if the stock surges, conversion becomes valuable. The [option-adjusted-spread](/wiki/option-adjusted-spread/) on a convertible is typically lower than straight debt, reflecting the embedded equity call.

Convertibles trade more like stocks than bonds when the conversion value is high. They are sensitive to both [interest-rate-risk](/wiki/interest-rate-risk/) and [equity](/wiki/common-stock/) volatility.

## Investment Grade vs. Junk Bonds

Corporate bonds rated BBB– and above (S&P) or Baa3 and above (Moody's) are [investment-grade-bond](/wiki/investment-grade-bond/). Those below are [speculative-grade](/wiki/speculative-grade/) or [junk-bond](/wiki/junk-bond/).

Investment-grade bonds face lower [default-risk](/wiki/default-rate/) and [rating-migration](/wiki/rating-migration/) risk; they are suitable for conservative [bond-etf](/wiki/bond-etf/) holders and [pension-liability](/wiki/pension-liability/) portfolios. Junk bonds offer higher yields but with meaningful [credit-event](/wiki/credit-event-sovereign/) risk. Most [municipal-bond](/wiki/municipal-bond/) investors stay investment-grade; [high-yield-investing](/wiki/high-yield-investing/) is for active managers.

## Maturity and call features

Bonds are also classified by [maturity](/wiki/bond-maturity-corporate/) and [call-risk](/wiki/call-risk/). A 30-year bond has longer [duration](/wiki/duration/) and [interest-rate-risk](/wiki/interest-rate-risk/) than a 5-year bond. [Callable-bond](/wiki/callable-bond/) issuers can refinance if rates fall, forcing early repayment on the bondholder—a negative feature priced into lower yields.
