*A **yield to put** is the [yield](/interest-rate/) an investor earns on a putable [bond](/bond/) if they exercise the [put option](/put-option/) at the earliest possible date. It represents the return the bondholder can guarantee by selling the bond back to the issuer at a predetermined price, usually when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) have risen and the bond's [coupon](/coupon-rate/) is no longer competitive.*

## How a putable bond works

A putable [bond](/bond/) grants the bondholder an embedded [put option](/put-option/): the right—but not the obligation—to sell the [bond](/bond/) back to the issuer at a specified price, usually par, on or after a certain date. This right protects the investor against [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/). If market rates rise sharply after purchase, the bond's price falls; rather than holding a depressed asset, the investor can force the issuer to buy it back at par, locking in a known loss and freeing capital to reinvest at higher rates.

Consider a 3% [coupon](/coupon-rate/) putable [bond](/bond/) issued at par with a put date in 3 years. If [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise to 5%, the bond's market value falls well below par because new issuances offer better coupons. The bondholder, facing a 2% annual "penalty" in forgone income, can exercise the put: the issuer buys the [bond](/bond/) back at par, and the investor recovers the original purchase price, though no gain has been made. The investor can then buy a 5% [bond](/bond/) with the proceeds.

## Computing yield to put

Yield to put is calculated identically to [yield to call](/callable-bond/) or [yield to maturity](/yield-to-maturity/), except the redemption date and price reflect when and at what price the bondholder chooses to put the [bond](/bond/) back.

**Example:**
- Par value: 100
- Coupon: 3%, paid annually
- Current price: 95
- Put date: 3 years
- Put price: 100

The investor receives three annual [coupon](/coupon-rate/) payments of 3, then forces the issuer to buy the [bond](/bond/) at 100. The cash flows are 3, 3, 103. Solving for yield, we get approximately 4.5%. This is the yield to put.

If the bond is instead trading at 105, the investor suffers a loss if the put is exercised (paying 105, recovering 100). In this scenario, yield to put would be negative relative to the purchase price, so the investor would not rationally exercise the put unless facing a far worse alternative (default, further price decline, or acute need for liquidity).

## When putable bonds appeal to investors

Putable [bonds](/bond/) are most attractive to investors in environments of rising or elevated [interest rates](/interest-rate/), heightened [credit risk](/credit-risk/), or uncertainty. They shift [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) and [credit risk](/credit-risk/) partially back to the issuer. The cost is paid implicitly: a putable [bond](/bond/) typically carries a lower [coupon](/coupon-rate/) than an equivalent non-putable [bond](/bond/) because the issuer has to compensate for the embedded [option](/option/).

Consider an investor who buys a putable [corporate bond](/corporate-bond/) shortly before an earnings disappointment or [credit-rating](/credit-rating/) downgrade is announced. If the issuer's [credit quality](/credit-rating/) deteriorates, the bondholder can exercise the put and avoid further loss. This "insurance" is particularly valuable for investors in [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) or emerging-market [debt](/sovereign-debt/), where [credit](/credit-risk/) shocks are more common.

Putable bonds are also embedded in some [mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/) and [floating-rate notes](/interest-rate/), where they protect investors against certain [prepayment](/refinancing-risk/) or [interest-rate](/interest-rate/) scenarios.

## Yield to put vs. other yield metrics

**[Yield to maturity](/yield-to-maturity/)** assumes the [bond](/bond/) is held to final maturity. For a putable [bond](/bond/) trading at a discount (or facing rising rates), this is likely overly optimistic because the bondholder will likely exercise the put before maturity.

**[Yield to call](/callable-bond/)** is relevant only for callable [bonds](/bond/), where the issuer holds the [option](/option/). A putable [bond](/bond/) may also be callable, in which case both metrics apply, but yield to put reflects the bondholder's rational decision-making.

**[Yield to worst](/yield-to-worst/)** is the minimum yield across all scenarios (call, put, maturity). For a putable [bond](/bond/) that is not callable, yield to put may also be the yield to worst if rates are expected to rise. For a [bond](/bond/) with both features, the issuer's call option and the bondholder's put option together determine the worst-case yield.

**[Current yield](/current-yield/)** is the [coupon](/coupon-rate/) divided by the current price and ignores both put and maturity. It is a crude metric and generally less useful than yield to put for a bond with embedded [options](/option/).

## Practical considerations

An investor contemplating purchase of a putable [bond](/bond/) must weigh the [put option's](/put-option/) value against its cost (the lower [coupon](/coupon-rate/)). If the investor expects [interest rates](/interest-rate/) to remain stable or fall, the put is unlikely to be exercised, and the lower [coupon](/coupon-rate/) is wasted. Conversely, if rates are expected to rise sharply, the put is very valuable, and accepting a lower [coupon](/coupon-rate/) in exchange is a good trade.

The put date is also critical. A put in 3 years is more valuable than a put in 10 years, all else equal, because [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) is concentrated in the near term and the bondholder can recover capital faster. A 30-year [bond](/bond/) with a put in 2 years is effectively a 2-year [bond](/bond/) (unless rates fall and the investor chooses to hold longer) but offers the [upside](/bull-market/) of [coupon](/coupon-rate/) payments if held.

Institutional investors and [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) use yield to put as one tool among many to assess [relative value](/relative-valuation/). A high yield to put on a creditworthy issuer may signal an attractive entry point, especially if the put date is near and [interest-rate](/interest-rate/) uncertainty is high.

## The option's economic cost

The issuer of a putable [bond](/bond/) effectively buys a [put option](/put-option/) from the bondholder. If rates rise and the [bond's](/bond/) value falls, the bondholder exercises, and the issuer is forced to refinance at higher rates. This is an expensive contingent liability for the issuer, which is why putable bonds are most common among highly creditworthy entities (sovereigns, [blue-chip corporations](/corporate-bond/)) and why the [coupon](/coupon-rate/) concession is material.

A putable [bond](/bond/) issued at a time of low [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and stable [credit](/credit-rating/) is particularly valuable to investors because the probability of exercise is high (rates are more likely to rise than fall from a low base), yet the [coupon](/coupon-rate/) may not fully reflect this skewed risk.
