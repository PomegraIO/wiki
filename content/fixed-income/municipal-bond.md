*A **municipal bond** — or **muni bond** — is a debt security issued by a state, city, county, school district, utility, or other local government entity to raise funds for public infrastructure, schools, hospitals, or other community projects. The defining feature is that interest income is exempt from federal income tax and, if you reside in the issuing jurisdiction, from state and local income tax.*

## Tax-exempt interest and the muni advantage

The federal tax exemption on municipal bond interest is the primary reason they exist as a distinct asset class. When an investor in a 40% combined federal and state tax bracket purchases a municipal bond yielding 4%, the after-tax yield is 4% (no taxes due). The equivalent [corporate bond](/corporate-bond) would need to yield 6.67% to deliver the same after-tax return.

This tax advantage allows state and local governments to issue debt at lower rates than corporations despite often lower creditworthiness. A city government might issue bonds at 4% and save substantially on borrowing costs compared to issuing taxable debt at 6.5%. Taxpayers capture that savings in the form of lower taxes funding infrastructure; wealthy taxpayers capture the benefit directly through lower taxes on their bond income.

The tax exemption is conditional: the proceeds must be used for legitimate public purposes (schools, roads, water systems, stadiums, libraries). Proceeds cannot be used for corporate facilities or private purposes, or the tax exemption is lost.

## General obligation vs. revenue bonds

Municipal bonds split into two broad categories based on repayment source. **General obligation bonds** (GO bonds) are backed by the full taxing authority of the issuing government — a city can raise taxes to repay them. **Revenue bonds** are backed only by the revenue from a specific project (parking meter revenues, water utility revenues, toll road revenues).

GO bonds carry lower default risk because of the taxing-power backing. Revenue bonds carry higher risk because they depend on project economics. A poorly conceived toll road that generates insufficient revenue faces default risk. Correspondingly, GO bonds trade at lower yields and revenue bonds at higher yields.

Most major municipal issuers use both: GO bonds for essential services and revenue bonds for self-supporting projects. The mix affects credit quality and investor demand.

## The credit spectrum and default risk

Municipal credit quality varies wildly. A bond from a wealthy, high-income suburb with stable revenues might carry a [credit rating](/credit-rating) equivalent to a [corporate bond](/corporate-bond) from an investment-grade firm. A bond from a declining industrial city with pension liabilities exceeding assets carries substantially higher default risk.

The 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic revealed the vulnerability of certain municipal issuers. While defaults remained rare (historically less than 1% per year), credit spreads on weaker issuers widened dramatically, causing significant price declines for holders.

Investors must evaluate municipal credit like [corporate bond](/corporate-bond) credit: examine financial statements, revenue trends, pension liabilities, and management quality. A $1 million portfolio of 50 randomly selected municipal bonds carries higher default risk than a $1 million Treasury note holding, requiring proportional compensation through higher yields.

## How munis are traded

Municipal bonds trade in a decentralized, over-the-counter market. Unlike [Treasury notes](/treasury-note), which trade in a centralized, highly liquid market, municipal bonds are less [liquid](/stock-exchange). An investor holding a $100,000 municipal bond from a small issuer might face difficulty selling quickly without accepting a significant [spread](/credit-spread).

This illiquidity is particularly acute for non-rated or poorly-rated bonds and for small issuances. On-the-run (recently issued) bonds from major issuers trade more actively. The liquidity discount is real — investors must accept lower prices to sell quickly — and should factor into purchase decisions.

The lack of transparency is another pain point. Unlike [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond), where issuers file extensive financial disclosures, municipal financial information is often fragmented, sometimes hard to access, and infrequently updated. Diligent investors must actively research credit quality rather than relying on ratings alone.

## The muni market's size and composition

The U.S. municipal bond market exceeds $3 trillion in outstanding debt, making it one of the world's largest bond markets. But the breadth is enormous: tens of thousands of issuers and millions of distinct bonds trade, with the majority held by high-income individuals, funds, and insurance companies.

Issuances span infrastructure (roads, water systems, airports, ports), public facilities (schools, police stations, hospitals), and utilities (electric, gas, water). The quality and stability of cash flows vary tremendously, creating opportunities for credit research to identify undervalued or overvalued bonds.

## Alternatives and complements to munis

For investors below the 32% federal tax bracket, [Treasury notes](/treasury-note) or [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond) often provide better after-tax returns than municipal bonds, despite the tax exemption. The calculation is: (Municipal yield) vs. (Treasury yield × (1 - tax rate)). For a 20% taxpayer, a 3% municipal bond offers 3% after-tax; a 3.5% Treasury offers 2.8% after-tax (3.5% × 0.8), making the Treasury superior.

For very high-income individuals, municipal bonds can be attractive. For retirees with low income in retirement, [Treasury securities](/treasury-bond) are usually preferable. The right instrument depends entirely on individual tax rates.

[Index funds](/index-fund) and [ETFs](/etf) holding broad baskets of municipal bonds are now accessible to retail investors, reducing the barrier to entry and providing better [diversification](/diversification) than holding individual bonds.
