## How the 3.8% Surtax Works

The Net Investment Income (NII) Surtax, enacted as part of the Affordable Care Act, applies a flat 3.8% tax on net investment income for individuals with modified adjusted gross income above certain thresholds. This is separate from ordinary income tax and applies to a broad category of investment returns: interest (including municipal bond interest), dividends, capital gains, rental income, and gains from the sale of investment property.

The key gotcha: **muni bond interest counts toward the income threshold even though it is not itself taxed as federal income.** You could earn $100,000 in muni interest, owe zero federal income tax on it, and still owe the 3.8% surtax if your total net investment income crosses the threshold. This creates a scenario where a tax-exempt bond can nonetheless trigger a tax—not a pleasant surprise in a tax return.

## Why Muni Interest Triggers the Surtax

The surtax was designed to capture all forms of investment return, not just taxable income. The statute defines net investment income broadly to include interest, dividends, gains, and certain other passive income. When Congress listed the types of income subject to NII, it included "interest"—without a carve-out for municipal bonds. 

The intent was to prevent high-income investors from entirely escaping the surtax by holding tax-exempt securities. If munis were excluded from the NII calculation, a wealthy investor could shelter investment returns from both regular income tax and the surtax simply by loading a portfolio with municipal bonds.

## Calculating Your NII and Surtax Exposure

To determine whether you owe the surtax, start by identifying your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) for NII purposes. For most individuals, MAGI is essentially AGI, but with a few adjustments. If your MAGI exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), you calculate NII on the excess amount.

Net investment income includes:
- Interest (including muni interest)
- Qualified and non-qualified dividends
- [Capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) and losses (net)
- Income from rental property and passive business interests
- Annuity income (with exceptions for certain annuities)
- Gains from the sale of investment real estate

It does not include wages, self-employment income, [qualified dividend](/qualified-dividend/) distributions from retirement accounts, or passive business losses.

You owe surtax on the *lesser of* (a) your net investment income or (b) the amount by which your MAGI exceeds the threshold. For a single filer with $250,000 in MAGI and $80,000 in net investment income (including $40,000 in muni interest), the surtax applies to only $50,000—the excess over the $200k threshold.

## Planning Around the Surtax

High-income earners and retirees transitioning into higher net investment income brackets often face a dilemma: muni bonds provide tax-free interest, but that very interest can trigger the surtax. This changes the calculus of muni ownership.

**Surtax-aware laddering** focuses on managing total MAGI, not just federal income tax. An investor on the cusp of the threshold might choose taxable bonds over munis if the after-tax yield is better once surtax is factored in. For example, a 3% muni bond yielding 3% tax-free looks attractive in isolation, but if it pushes you over the MAGI threshold and subjects $50,000 of other income to the surtax, the true cost is higher.

**Timing conversions** can also help. [Roth conversions](/medicare-irmaa-roth-conversion-planning/) and other discretionary income events can be deferred into years of lower investment income to avoid crossing the surtax threshold. Similarly, harvesting [capital losses](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) can offset investment gains and reduce NII.

**State and local tax planning** remains valuable even with the surtax present. A muni bond issued in your state is often exempt from both federal and state income tax, which still saves money—the surtax is an additional layer, not a replacement for ordinary income tax savings.

## The IRMAA Link: Surtax and Medicare Premiums

For Medicare enrollees, the surtax is often overshadowed by a related but separate concern: Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amounts (IRMAA). IRMAA uses modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) to determine whether you pay higher premiums for [Medicare](/medicare-irmaa-roth-conversion-planning/) Part B and Part D.

The two systems interact. Muni interest contributes to MAGI for both surtax and IRMAA calculations. A retiree earning $150,000 in W-2 income plus $60,000 in muni interest faces a MAGI of $210,000, which may trigger both the 3.8% surtax on investment income and IRMAA surcharges on Medicare premiums. This makes muni bonds less attractive for Medicare-eligible individuals than the tax-exemption alone might suggest.

## Thresholds and Indexing

The income thresholds for the surtax are indexed annually for inflation. As of 2024, the thresholds stand at $200,000 (single) and $250,000 (married filing jointly). These have risen modestly over the years but remain the defining boundary for surtax exposure. Investors earning below these thresholds face no NII surtax, regardless of muni holdings.

For married couples filing separately, each spouse faces a $125,000 threshold—a significant penalty that makes joint filing preferable in nearly all cases.

## Reporting and Compliance

The surtax is reported on Form 8960 (Net Investment Income Tax—Individuals, Estates, and Trusts), which feeds into your Form 1040. You will need to identify all components of net investment income, including muni interest and any capital gains or losses from sales during the year.

Many tax software packages now flagged NII exposure automatically, but it's worth reviewing your estimated surtax liability before year-end to see if any portfolio adjustments make sense.
