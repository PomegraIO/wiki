*A **variable rate demand obligation** (VRDO) is a municipal bond whose coupon resets frequently—usually weekly—and includes a put option allowing investors to redeem at par (face value) on short notice. VRDOs blend the credit risk of long-term bonds with the liquidity and interest-rate insulation of short-term instruments.*

## The dual nature: long maturity, short redemption windows

A typical VRDO matures in 20 or 30 years, but the coupon adjusts weekly. The issuer's interest cost is pinned to a short-term rate—often LIBOR (or its successor, [SOFR](/sofr/)) plus a spread. If short-term rates rise, the coupon rises; if they fall, so does the coupon. From the investor's perspective, the coupon is unpredictable, which sounds unappealing.

The cure is the put: every week (or sometimes every month), you can walk up to the bond dealer and redeem your holding at 100 cents on the dollar. You don't have to hold until maturity. You don't have to sell into the secondary market and accept whatever bid-ask spread the day offers. You demand repayment, the issuer pays, and you move on.

This structure trades off: you accept interest-rate risk over the life of your holding (your coupon might fall from 4% to 2%), but you gain daily market liquidity. It's an elegant fit for a specific investor: a money manager with short-time-horizon capital, or a bank's overnight reserve fund, where par-value certainty matters more than yield.

## Why issuers love them (and the dark side)

For an issuer, VRDOs are financier's gold. A 30-year VRDO allows the issuer to finance a project with a long useful life—a water treatment plant, a university dormitory—but fund it at short-term rates. If short rates stay low, the issuer saves money. If short rates spike, the issue becomes expensive, but by then the project is built and the issuer is locked in.

The issuer's risk is refinancing: if the underlying credit deteriorates or the money market freezes, the VRDO may no longer find a tender agent (the intermediary that facilitates redemptions), and investors may be unable to sell. In 2008, the financial crisis shut down the VRDO market for several months. Investors who expected weekly liquidity found themselves holding illiquid securities. Issuers who had planned to roll VRDOs were suddenly tapping emergency liquidity facilities.

## The tender agent and the demand option

The VRDO ecosystem has a critical middleman: the tender agent, usually a bank, who manages the weekly reset and buys back bonds from investors who exercise their put. The tender agent's credit risk is acute but short-term: they're exposed for only a week or so before the bonds roll forward or the issuer redeems them.

The structural dependency is stark. If the tender agent's credit rating falls sharply, or if it fails altogether, the put option can become worthless. An investor holding a VRDO backed by a failing tender agent is stuck. This is why VRDO documentation includes backup tender agents, but even backup credit can be strained under systemic stress.

## Auction rate securities: a cautionary tale

VRDOs have a close cousin: auction-rate securities (ARS), which reset monthly instead of weekly and offered longer put windows. In 2008, the ARS market collapsed. Auctions failed—nobody bid—and investors discovered their "liquid" holdings were illiquid. Issuers had to rescue the instruments or let them default. Banks faced lawsuits and settled for billions.

That crisis haunts the VRDO market. Issuers and dealers have added more safeguards: hard-put guarantees from strong banks, higher spreads to compensate investors for liquidity risk, and shorter reset windows. But the underlying structural fragility remains. VRDOs are only liquid if the credit system is functioning.

## Accounting and portfolio treatment

Banks and money-market-funds hold VRDOs as quasi-cash because the weekly put gives them par certainty. Accountants treat them as short-term for liquidity purposes, even though they're legally 20-year bonds. A bank's trading desk might hold $100 million of VRDOs against overnight deposit outflows.

The accounting is pragmatic: if you can truly redeem weekly at par, the credit risk you're exposed to is limited to one week's floating-rate reset plus the issuer's ability to pay. But stress tests have revealed the folly. If redemption channels close (as they did in 2008), the bonds lock into long-term holding periods, and the credit risk becomes 20 years, not one week. The gap between accounting treatment and reality can be brutal.

## Pricing and yield

A VRDO typically yields 10–40 basis points above the underlying short-term rate (SOFR + 2%, say, might become SOFR + 2.35% for a VRDO). The spread compensates investors for liquidity risk, credit risk of the issuer, and the tender agent's risk.

In benign credit conditions, VRDOs trade rich—spreads compress—because the put option is perceived as valuable and the money-market appetite for floating-rate paper is strong. During stress, spreads widen sharply. Issuers may have to pay 100+ basis points over SOFR to place a VRDO, and even then demand is tepid. The spread is the market's estimate of the likelihood and severity of a liquidity freeze.

## Modern variations: credit enhancements and guarantees

Modern VRDOs often include "hard put" guarantees from a bank or insurer: a legal commitment that the put option will be honoured even if the tender agent fails. This removes one layer of structural risk. Some VRDOs have tiered floating rates—a higher coupon if the issuer's credit rating falls, or if the tender agent's credit deteriorates.

These enhancements are valuable, but they add cost. An issuer that can issue cheaper taxable corporate debt may find that a VRDO with all these bells and whistles isn't the bargain it appears. Conversely, a [municipal-bond](/municipal-bond/) issuer with stable credit and access to a strong tender agent may use VRDOs as a genuine source of cheap long-term funding.

## Who holds them today

Despite (or because of) 2008, VRDOs remain a fixture. Money-market funds, bank trading desks, and short-duration [mutual-fund](/mutual-fund/) managers hold them. Institutional investors with strong risk management and real-time monitoring can use them effectively. Retail investors rarely hold them directly—the weekly reset and put mechanics are too complex, and most brokers don't facilitate easy redemptions for individuals.

Pension funds and endowments, which have more stable liabilities, generally avoid VRDOs. They prefer [bond-etf](/bond-etf/) or [income-fund](/income-fund/) exposure with predictable coupons. The VRDO suits rolling, short-term portfolios, not buy-and-hold strategies.
