*The **overnight repo rate vs fed funds rate** distinction matters because both are short-term [interest rates](/interest-rate/) that anchor the money market, yet they operate on fundamentally different mechanics. The [federal funds rate](/federal-funds-rate/) is an unsecured lending rate between banks; overnight repo is secured lending backed by Treasury collateral. Their spreads fluctuate based on credit conditions, and the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) actively influences both.*

## The Federal Funds Rate: Unsecured Overnight Lending

The [federal funds rate](/federal-funds-rate/) is the interest rate at which commercial banks lend reserve balances to each other overnight. It is **unsecured**—the lending bank has no collateral claim if the borrowing bank defaults.

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) does not directly set the fed funds rate but rather sets a **target range** (e.g., 5.00%–5.25%) and uses [open-market operations](/), reverse repos, and other tools to keep the actual rate trading within that band. The Fed publishes the effective fed funds rate daily, which is a volume-weighted average of all fed funds transactions reported by dealers.

Because fed funds lending is unsecured, the rate reflects the creditworthiness of borrowing banks and general money-market conditions. In stressed markets (like 2008 or March 2020), the fed funds rate can spike or become volatile as banks hoard cash and hesitate to lend to each other without collateral.

## Overnight Repo: Secured Overnight Lending

An overnight **repurchase agreement (repo)** is a secured loan. Party A (the cash lender) provides cash and receives [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/), [Treasury bills](/treasury-bill-discount-vs-investment-yield/), or agency [mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/) as collateral. Party B (the cash borrower) pays interest on the cash and agrees to buy back the collateral the next day at a higher price. That price difference is the repo rate, expressed as a percentage.

**Example:** A dealer borrows $100 million overnight, pledging $101 million of Treasury securities as collateral (the 1% haircut protects the lender). The next day, the dealer repays $100.01 million and receives the securities back. The 0.01% overnight interest is the repo rate.

The overnight repo market is enormous—typically exceeding $1 trillion in outstanding volume on any given day. The most active segment is the overnight Treasury repo rate (also called the [SOFR, or Secured Overnight Financing Rate](/sofr/), which the Fed uses as a replacement benchmark after LIBOR discontinuation).

## Why the Spread Matters

The overnight repo rate is typically *lower* than the fed funds rate—often by 5 to 50 basis points—because repo is collateralized. The lender has a direct claim on [Treasury](/treasury-bond/) securities, which carry minimal credit risk, so the lender is willing to accept a lower return.

The spread between the two rates is called the **repo–fed funds spread** or **OIS–SOFR spread** (depending on which repo rate you use). This spread widens when:

1. **Bank credit stress increases:** Banks become reluctant to lend unsecured money, pushing the fed funds rate higher while the secured repo rate remains stable.
2. **Treasury demand surges:** Flight-to-safety buying of Treasuries can temporarily tighten the repo market (fewer Treasuries available as collateral), pushing repo rates higher and narrowing the spread.
3. **Liquidity evaporates:** If the financial system experiences a shock, banks hoard cash and avoid unsecured lending, widening the spread sharply.

The September 2019 repo market crisis is the clearest modern example: the overnight repo rate spiked to 10% as lending dried up, while the fed funds rate remained near its target. The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) intervened by injecting liquidity directly into the repo market.

## The Fed's Tools and Both Rates

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) influences both rates through different mechanisms:

**Fed Funds Rate:** The Fed sets its target range and uses:
- **Open-market operations (OMOs):** Buying and selling securities to adjust the supply of reserves in the banking system.
- **The discount rate:** The rate at which the Fed's "discount window" lends directly to banks.
- **Reserve-requirement adjustments:** Changing the amount of reserves banks must hold (though the Fed set requirements to zero in 2020).

**Overnight Repo Rate:** The Fed uses:
- **Overnight reverse repo operations (RRPs):** The Fed lends out Treasuries and borrows cash from money-market funds and foreign central banks, effectively setting a floor under repo rates.
- **Quantitative easing (QE) and large-scale asset purchases:** The Fed's balance sheet size and composition affect Treasury availability in the repo market.

When the Fed raises its target fed funds rate, it typically expects both the fed funds rate and overnight repo rate to rise in tandem. But the spread can still widen or narrow depending on financial conditions.

## SOFR and LIBOR Replacement

Until 2021, the financial industry used **LIBOR** (the London Interbank Offered Rate) as the standard benchmark for short-term lending. LIBOR was an **unsecured** rate, similar in concept to fed funds. Regulatory concerns about manipulation and declining use prompted a shift to **SOFR**, which is based on actual repo transactions—mostly overnight Treasury repos.

SOFR is more transparent (it is derived from real transaction data) and less prone to manipulation. It is also secured by Treasuries, making it a more stable benchmark during stress. Mortgages, [bonds](/bond/), and other financial products are now typically referenced to SOFR instead of LIBOR.

## Comparing the Two in Practice

| Aspect | Fed Funds Rate | Overnight Repo Rate |
|--------|---|---|
| **Collateral** | None (unsecured) | Treasury securities (highly secured) |
| **Typical level** | Higher, more volatile | Lower, more stable |
| **Driven by** | Bank credit risk, reserve scarcity | Treasury scarcity, flight-to-safety |
| **Benchmark use** | Fed's policy tool | SOFR, money-market funds |
| **Stress reaction** | Spikes sharply | May spike if Treasuries scarce; usually stable |

In normal times, the overnight repo rate trades 10–40 basis points below fed funds. In stress, the spread can widen to 100+ basis points as banks shun unsecured lending.

## The Broader Money Market

Both rates are anchors for the entire [money market](/money-market-fund/). If the fed funds rate rises, rates on [commercial paper](/), [banker's acceptances](/), and other short-term instruments typically follow. The overnight repo rate particularly influences the cost of short-term borrowing for dealers, asset managers, and hedge funds—anyone who needs secured funding.

Understanding the relationship between these two rates helps investors and analysts interpret central-bank communication, assess financial-system stress, and evaluate the attractiveness of short-term fixed-income investments like [money-market funds](/money-market-fund/) and [Treasury bills](/treasury-bill-discount-vs-investment-yield/).
