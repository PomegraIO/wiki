*The **federal funds market** is the interbank lending market for reserve balances held at the [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/). Banks with excess reserves lend overnight to banks with shortfalls at the [federal funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/), the most important [interest rate](/wiki/interest-rate/) in the US financial system and the primary tool through which the [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) implements [monetary policy](/wiki/monetary-policy/).*

## How the federal funds market functions

A bank that falls short of its [reserve requirement](/wiki/reserve-requirements/) can borrow reserves overnight from a bank with excess reserves. The transaction is simple: the lending bank transfers reserve balances from its account at the [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) to the borrowing bank's account, with agreement to reverse the transfer the next day with interest.

Example: It's 3:00 PM on Tuesday. Bank A realizes it will be short of required reserves at the Fed's end-of-day settlement. Bank B has excess reserves. They negotiate a rate (say, 4.75%) and Bank B lends $50 million in reserves to Bank A overnight. At 9:00 AM Wednesday, the transaction reverses: Bank A returns $50 million plus interest (~$500 in interest on a $50 million overnight loan).

This seems mechanical, but the federal funds rate is critical: it is the [interest rate](/wiki/interest-rate/) at which banks *must* operate. A higher [fed funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/) makes it expensive for banks to borrow, so they tighten lending to customers. A lower rate makes borrowing cheap, so lending expands. This transmission mechanism is how the [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) controls the entire financial system.

## The Federal Reserve's role

The [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) does not directly set the [federal funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/). Instead, it sets a **target range** (e.g., 4.50–4.75%) and uses [open market operations](/wiki/open-market-operations/) and [interest on reserves](/wiki/interest-on-reserves/) to push the market rate toward that target.

**Tools used:**

1. **[Discount window rate](/wiki/discount-window/)**: The [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) lends directly to banks at the discount window at a rate (the discount rate) set above the target range. Banks will not borrow at the discount window if the [fed funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/) is lower, so setting the discount rate above the target acts as a ceiling.

2. **[Interest on reserves](/wiki/interest-on-reserves/)**: The [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) pays interest on reserve balances banks hold at the [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/). By paying interest slightly below the target range, the [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) discourages banks from lending at lower rates (acts as a floor).

3. **[Open market operations](/wiki/open-market-operations/)**: The [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) buys and sells Treasury securities, injecting or draining reserves from the banking system. Injecting reserves pushes the [fed funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/) lower; draining reserves pushes it higher.

## Market dynamics and the [repo market](/wiki/reverse-repo-facility/) connection

The federal funds market is tightly linked to the [repo market](/wiki/repurchase-agreement/) (repurchase agreements), where banks and investment firms borrow via short-term collateralized loans. Many participants use repos instead of or alongside federal funds.

In normal times, the two rates move together. In stressed markets (2008 financial crisis, March 2020), the repo market can seize (rates spike) while the federal funds market is propped up by [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) intervention.

## The [federal funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/) as anchor

The [federal funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/) is the bedrock [interest rate](/wiki/interest-rate/) anchoring all others:

- **Prime rate** (charged to creditworthy borrowers): Typically 3 percentage points above the [fed funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/).
- **[LIBOR](/wiki/libor/)** and **[SOFR](/wiki/sofr/)** (interbank lending benchmarks): Track closely with [fed funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/).
- **[Mortgage rates](/wiki/mortgage-personal/)**, **[credit card](/wiki/credit-card-rewards/) rates**, **auto loan rates**: All adjust in response to changes in [fed funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/) expectations.

A 25-basis-point raise in the [fed funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/) ripples throughout the economy: mortgages become more expensive, business loans tighten, consumer [credit cards](/wiki/credit-card-rewards/) rise. This is the [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/)'s transmission mechanism.

## Reserve requirements and post-2008 evolution

Historically, [reserve requirements](/wiki/reserve-requirements/) forced banks to maintain a minimum fraction of deposits as reserves, driving daily demand for federal funds. In 2020, the [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) cut [reserve requirements](/wiki/reserve-requirements/) to zero, reducing structural demand for federal funds borrowing.

Post-2008, the [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) also implemented [quantitative easing](/wiki/quantitative-easing/), flooding the banking system with excess reserves. With plenty of reserves available, the federal funds market became less operationally necessary and more of a policy signaling mechanism.

## SOFR transition and modernization

In 2023, the [Fed](/wiki/federal-reserve/) transitioned benchmark rates away from [LIBOR](/wiki/libor/) to [SOFR](/wiki/sofr/) (Secured Overnight Financing Rate), a rate based on actual overnight [repo](/wiki/repurchase-agreement/) transactions rather than quotes. While [SOFR](/wiki/sofr/) is not identical to the [fed funds rate](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/), the two are closely aligned and [SOFR](/wiki/sofr/) is now the primary overnight rate reference.

The shift reflects a desire to use transaction-based (not quoted) rates and to reduce reliance on the declining federal funds market volume.
