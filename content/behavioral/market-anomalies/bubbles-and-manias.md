*[Bubbles and manias](/wiki/bubbles-and-manias/) are explosive rallies in asset prices—stocks, commodities, or real estate—driven by collective excitement and overconfidence in perpetual appreciation. Prices detach from [intrinsic value](/wiki/intrinsic-value/), often by 100–300%, fueled by [herd behavior](/wiki/herding-investors/), easy credit, and FOMO. The inevitable deflation leaves widespread financial damage and loss of confidence in markets.*

| Phase | Characteristics |
|-------|-----------------|
| **Discovery** | Novel asset, new narrative (tech, crypto) |
| **Expansion** | Prices double, media hype, retail inflows |
| **Mania** | Parabolic rises, stories replace fundamentals |
| **Denial** | "This time is different," valuations soar |
| **Crack** | Sentiment flip, forced selling, panic |
| **Crash** | Steep declines over weeks to months |

## Historical precedents and pattern recognition

The Dutch [Tulip Mania](/wiki/tulip-mania/) (1630s) saw rare bulb prices spike to absurd levels, then collapse. The [South Sea Bubble](/wiki/south-sea-bubble/) (1720) destroyed fortunes when investors realized the trading company's value was fictional. The [Dot-Com Bubble](/wiki/dot-com-bubble-2000/) saw profitless startups valued at billions; the [NASDAQ Crash](/wiki/nasdaq-crash-2000/) destroyed $5 trillion in market cap. The [Housing Bubble 2008](/wiki/housing-bubble-2008/) embedded in [subprime mortgages](/wiki/subprime-mortgage-crisis/) imploded, triggering the financial crisis. Each bubble followed the same arc: discovery, expansion, mania, crack, crash.

## Sentiment and behavioral drivers of excess

Bubbles aren't accidents—they reflect systematic psychological errors. [Herding](/wiki/herding-investors/) makes individuals abandon independent judgment and follow crowds. [Anchoring](/wiki/anchoring-bias/) leads investors to extrapolate recent trends: if a stock rises 50% in a year, anchored minds assume 50% annual returns forever. [FOMO](/wiki/fomo/) (fear of missing out) drives late entrants to chase momentum even at absurd valuations. [Overconfidence](/wiki/overconfidence-bias/) convinces participants they understand the trend and can exit before the crash. Mass psychology overwhelms rational models; a [discounted cash flow](/wiki/discounted-cash-flow-valuation/) valuation becomes irrelevant when sentiment is frothy.

## Credit expansion and liquidity fueling

Bubbles require credit. The 2008 housing bubble was fueled by cheap mortgages and loose lending standards; subprime borrowers with no down payment accessed $500,000 homes. The [cryptocurrency bubble 2017](/wiki/cryptocurrency-bubble-2017/) rode on easy leverage and margin trading. Low [interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate/) reduce the [discount rate](/wiki/discount-rate/) in valuations, mathematically justifying higher prices. Central banks tightening policy often pops bubbles—when rates rose in 2022, crypto and high-growth tech crashed.

## The "new paradigm" narrative and denial phase

Bubbles are sustained by narratives that feel revolutionary: "The internet changes everything"; "Blockchain will eliminate intermediaries"; "Cryptocurrencies are the future of money." These may contain truth, but the *magnitude* of expected returns is absurd. Believers dismiss warnings as outdated thinking: "You don't understand the technology." [Confirmation bias](/wiki/confirmation-bias/) reinforces the narrative—believers ignore contradictions, focus on supporting evidence. This denial phase lasts longest when new technology makes genuine value hard to assess. Valuation becomes secondary to storytelling.

## [Momentum](/wiki/momentum-investing/) and reflexivity

Bubbles are self-reinforcing in upswings. Rising prices attract new buyers, pushing prices higher. Euphoria breeds confidence, encouraging margin borrowing and leverage. [Positive feedback loops](/wiki/accumulation-distribution/) create parabolic price moves. Leverage amplifies gains on the way up but forces [liquidation](/wiki/liquidation-value/) on the way down. When sentiment flips—even a small trigger—forced selling cascades. Margin calls force liquidation at any price. [Circuit breakers](/wiki/circuit-breaker-halt/) halt trading; exchanges may close. Panic selling overwhelms any buyer, and prices free-fall.

## Sectoral concentration and contagion risk

Bubbles are often sectoral: internet stocks in 2000, real estate in 2008, cryptocurrency in 2017. While the rest of the market remains rational, one sector becomes detached. Spillover occurs when: (1) margin calls force investors to sell other holdings, (2) confidence in all risky assets evaporates, or (3) the bubble is so large it affects the whole economy (housing in 2008). Diversification limits risk, but [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) approaches 1 during crashes—assets sell indiscriminately.

## Recognition and defensive strategies

Identifying bubbles in real-time is notoriously difficult. Prices can stay irrational longer than investors can stay solvent. Defensive tactics: (1) avoid [concentration](/wiki/concentration-risk/) in hot sectors, (2) lock in gains when valuations reach extremes, (3) buy puts or hedge with [tail-risk](/wiki/tail-risk-hedging/) instruments, (4) maintain dry powder to buy in crashes, (5) track [credit aggregates](/wiki/monetary-base/) and [margin debt](/wiki/margin-trading-crypto/)—surging credit precedes bubbles.
