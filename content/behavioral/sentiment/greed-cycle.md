*A **greed cycle** is a self-reinforcing period of rising [asset prices](/wiki/price-discovery/), falling [risk premiums](/wiki/market-risk-premium/), and expanding [leverage](/wiki/leveraged-etf/) that culminates in excess and sudden [capitulation](/wiki/capitulation-selling/). It is the mirror image of [fear cycles](/wiki/fear-index/), driven by behavioral herding and [availability bias](/wiki/availability-bias-investing/).*

<aside class="wiki-infobox">

| Phase | Driver | Market signal |
|-------|--------|---------------|
| **Accumulation** | Institutional positions build; sentiment stable | [Spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) stable; leverage steady |
| **Early greed** | Retail flows accelerate; confidence rises | [Volatility](/wiki/implied-volatility/) declines; [margin debt](/wiki/margin-trading-crypto/) rises |
| **Peak greed** | Euphoria; leveraged speculation dominates | [Crowded trades](/wiki/crowded-trade/); extreme positioning |
| **Realization** | Unexpected shock or profit-taking trigger | [Capitulation](/wiki/capitulation-selling/); forced selling |
| **Fear** | Margin calls; [deleveraging](/wiki/leveraged-buyout/); negative feedback loop | [Spreads](/wiki/credit-spread-corporate/) spike; [volatility](/wiki/historical-volatility/) soars |

</aside>

## The anatomy of greed

A greed cycle is not synonymous with a [bull market](/wiki/bull-market/). Bull markets can be driven by fundamental improvement—rising [earnings](/wiki/earnings-yield/), [productivity](/wiki/productivity/) gains, or policy tailwinds. A greed cycle, by contrast, is driven by *extrapolation* and [herding](/wiki/herding-investors/). As [asset prices](/wiki/asset-allocation/) rise, investors become convinced that prices will continue rising, so they increase bets. Confidence in continued appreciation attracts marginal buyers, many financed by [debt](/wiki/margin-call-forex/). This dynamic pushes prices higher, validates the narrative, and attracts more leverage.

The cycle is self-feeding until the feedback loop reverses. Once a shock—earnings disappointment, policy shift, or [contagion](/wiki/systemic-risk/)—arrives, [momentum](/wiki/momentum-investing/) traders and leveraged players face margin calls. Selling accelerates, validating fears, and attracting more selling. The greed cycle inverts into a fear cycle.

## Historical patterns: 2000, 2008, 2021

**The dot-com bubble** (1999–2000) epitomized greed. No revenues required. [Valuations](/wiki/relative-valuation/) were infinite multiples of non-existent earnings. Retail investors opened brokerage accounts and bought any stock with ".com" in its name. [Margin debt](/wiki/margin-call-forex/) surged. When growth stalled and the first large-cap tech companies missed earnings, the cycle reversed violently. The [Nasdaq](/wiki/nasdaq/) lost 78% peak-to-trough.

**The 2008 financial crisis** involved extreme greed in real estate and [mortgage-backed securities](/wiki/mortgage-backed-security/). Home [leverage](/wiki/leverage-ratio-forex/) ratios were 20:1+. [Credit ratings](/wiki/credit-rating/) on [subprime](/wiki/subprime-mortgage-crisis/) bonds were inflated by rating agencies' conflicts of interest. When housing prices flattened, defaults cascaded, and [leverage](/wiki/leveraged-etf/) became a liability. Forced asset sales and [bank failures](/wiki/banking-crisis-of-1933/) followed.

**The 2021 meme-stock surge** and [cryptocurrency bubble 2017](/wiki/cryptocurrency-bubble-2017/) showed greed's evolution in retail-driven markets. Extreme [short squeezes](/wiki/short-squeeze/) and viral narratives drove valuations detached from fundamentals. When [volatility](/wiki/volatility-index-futures/) normalized and [profit-taking](/wiki/capitulation-selling/) began, prices collapsed.

## Behavioral drivers

Greed cycles exploit cognitive biases:

- **[Availability bias](/wiki/availability-heuristic/)**: Recent strong returns are mentally "available," so investors extrapolate. If stocks rose 20% last year, the mind anchors to that rate.
- **[Herding](/wiki/herd-behavior/)**: Seeing others profit triggers [FOMO](/wiki/fomo/)—fear of missing out—and compels entry even late in the cycle.
- **[Momentum bias](/wiki/momentum-investing/)**: Price momentum itself becomes a signal that attracts [algorithmic](/wiki/algorithmic-trading/) and human followers.
- **[Anchoring](/wiki/anchoring-bias/)**: Investors fix on "reasonable" valuations (e.g., historical [P/E multiples](/wiki/price-to-earnings-ratio/)) and ignore the fact that fundamentals have deteriorated.
- **[Confirmation bias](/wiki/confirmation-bias/)**: Investors cherry-pick bullish news and dismiss bearish signals.

## Leverage as an amplifier

Greed cycles are supercharged by access to cheap [debt](/wiki/debt-ceiling/). When [interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate-parity/) are low and [credit spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) are tight, [margin](/wiki/margin-trading-crypto/) is abundant and affordable. [Leverage](/wiki/leverage-ratio-forex/) ratios rise. Every 1% move in an asset class is amplified 5x, 10x, or higher for levered players. This attracts more speculators and pushes prices further.

When the cycle reverses, leverage becomes a curse. [Margin calls](/wiki/margin-call-forex/) force sales at the worst moment. [Short-sellers](/wiki/short-squeeze/) cover in panic, accelerating upward moves on any technical bounce. Institutions report losses, triggering further redemptions. The [multiplier](/wiki/money-multiplier/) works in reverse.

## Crowded trades and liquidity mirage

Greed cycles concentrate positions. In late 2000, nearly every tech fund held the same large-cap tech stocks. In 2008, mortgage professionals were overweight housing-linked assets. In 2021, retail traders piled into highly [correlated](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) meme stocks.

When the cycle breaks, the perceived [liquidity](/wiki/liquidity-risk/) evaporates. Everyone tries to exit simultaneously, but buyers vanish. Asset prices plunge to levels where supply finds demand—often far below the bubble peak.

## Policy and timing challenges

Central banks and regulators watch greed cycles closely. The dilemma: early intervention (raising [rates](/wiki/interest-rate-hedging/), tightening [leverage limits](/wiki/concentration-limits/)) may abort the bull market, stoking political pressure. But inaction allows leverage and [systemic risk](/wiki/systemic-risk/) to build unchecked.

The [2017 cryptocurrency bubble](/wiki/cryptocurrency-bubble-2017/) saw global regulators try containment; the [2021 reflation surge](/wiki/inflation-surprise/) saw [central banks](/wiki/central-bank/) hesitate to tighten into perceived "transitory" momentum.

## Measuring greed

Practitioners use proxies:

- **[Margin debt](/wiki/margin-call-forex/)**—rising levels signal greed
- **[Credit spreads](/wiki/credit-spread-corporate/)**—compressed spreads show risk-seeking
- **[Volatility](/wiki/implied-volatility/)**—declining VIX often marks late-cycle greed
- **[Positioning](/wiki/exposure-limit/)**—extreme longs signal crowded trades
- **[Fund flows](/wiki/fund-distribution/)**—surging inflows into equities mark retail enthusiasm

## The inevitability question

Are greed cycles inevitable? Some theorists argue yes: [behavioral finance](/wiki/anchoring-in-trading/) suggests that emotions and herd dynamics will forever drive boom-bust cycles. Others contend that better [transparency](/wiki/etf-portfolio-exposure/), improved [regulation](/wiki/financial-regulation-and-supervision/), and [circuit breakers](/wiki/circuit-breaker-halt/) can dampen extremes. The data is mixed. Cycles persist, but catastrophic blowups are rarer post-2008 thanks to [stress tests](/wiki/stress-testing/) and stricter [leverage rules](/wiki/position-limit-regulations/).
