*The **loss spillover effect** describes how losses in one asset class or market dampen investors' appetite for risk across unrelated assets. A sharp drawdown in equities, commodities, or credit spreads triggers a flight to safety that affects everything from emerging-market currencies to [high-yield bonds](/wiki/high-yield-bond/), regardless of those assets' individual fundamentals.*

## The distinction between contagion and spillover

Loss spillover differs from [correlation-driven contagion](/wiki/correlation-risk/). Contagion is mechanical: asset A's move mechanically causes asset B's move via shared exposures or balance-sheet linkages. Spillover is psychological and portfolio-driven. An investor sees her equities down 5%; her [risk appetite](/wiki/market-risk/) drops; she cuts exposure to [emerging-market currencies](/wiki/emerging-market-currency-pairs/), [corporate bonds](/wiki/corporate-bond/), and [small-cap stocks](/wiki/micro-cap-etf/) even though none of these assets are causally related to the original decline. Spillover is the mechanism behind "risk-off" days when nearly all risky assets sell off in tandem, despite disconnected fundamentals.

## How losses propagate through mental accounting

Behavioral finance explains loss spillover through [mental accounting](/wiki/mental-accounting/) and [loss aversion](/wiki/loss-aversion/). Investors do not evaluate each asset in isolation; they maintain a mental portfolio and experience gains and losses holistically. A major loss in one corner (e.g., a cryptocurrency crash) damages the entire portfolio's perceived safety and increases risk aversion across all holdings. This is heightened when investors are at their [loss-aversion peak](/wiki/loss-aversion/)—meaning they feel losses more acutely than equivalent gains.

The mechanism also involves [herding](/wiki/herding-in-markets/). Once some investors begin derisking, their sell orders appear as liquidity stress to others, who interpret it as a signal to derisked themselves. Within hours, [momentum](/wiki/momentum-investing/) carries spillover across regions and asset classes.

## Real-world evidence: 2020 and 2022 episodes

The March 2020 COVID crash saw all risk assets crater together—equities, [high-yield credit](/wiki/junk-bond/), emerging-market debt, and commodities—despite radically different underlying risks. The spillover was so severe that even [Treasury bonds](/wiki/treasury-bond/), normally safe havens, temporarily sold off due to [forced selling](/wiki/capitulation-selling/) and [margin calls](/wiki/margin-call-forex/). 

In 2022, losses in the equity market due to [quantitative tightening](/wiki/quantitative-tightening/) and rate hikes triggered spillover into [credit spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/), [real-estate investment trusts](/wiki/real-estate-investment-trust/), and emerging markets. Again, the spillover was disproportionate to each asset's exposure to the underlying Fed policy shock.

## The role of margin and forced selling

Leverage amplifies spillover. When [margin call](/wiki/margin-call-forex/) thresholds are breached, forced selling cascades across a portfolio regardless of whether the forced asset shares the blame for the initial loss. A [prime broker](/wiki/prime-broker/) may require a hedge fund to deleverage across multiple asset classes after a loss in one, driving selling pressure where no fundamental deterioration occurred.

Institutional [asset allocation](/wiki/asset-allocation/) rules, such as floor triggers or [rebalancing discipline](/wiki/rebalancing-discipline/) bands, also mechanically force sales of unrelated assets once one leg declines. This creates [contagion](/wiki/correlation-risk/) that is rules-driven rather than belief-driven.

## Why spillover matters for portfolio construction

Investors pursuing [diversification](/wiki/diversification/) expect uncorrelated assets to stabilize returns. Loss spillover undermines this benefit. A portfolio split between equities and [emerging-market bonds](/wiki/emerging-markets-fund/) appears uncorrelated in normal times; during a spillover event, both move sharply down together as investors globally derisked. This correlation drift is greatest in acute stress, precisely when diversification is most needed.

The phenomenon also explains why [volatility spikes](/wiki/volatility-smile/) correlate across markets even when underlying volatility drivers are distinct. When loss spillover is acute, the [VIX](/wiki/fear-index/) and [emerging-market currency volatility](/wiki/currency-volatility/) often surge in unison, not because currencies have become more macro-sensitive, but because marginal selling pressure has become uniform across risky assets.

## Mitigating spillover risk

Sophisticated investors hedge spillover through explicit diversification into uncorrelated risk factors (e.g., [trend-following](/wiki/trend-following/), [volatility strategies](/wiki/volatility-index-futures/)) that profit when risk appetite collapses, offsetting [drawdowns](/wiki/drawdown-risk-measure/) in core holdings. Others employ [tail-risk hedging](/wiki/tail-risk-hedging/) to protect against the cascade. Portfolio managers also monitor correlation drift and rebalance during benign periods to avoid forced selling during crises.

Understanding loss spillover highlights an uncomfortable truth: [perfect diversification](/wiki/minimum-variance-portfolio/) is impossible. In crises, all risky assets exhibit systemic dependence on global risk appetite, rendering their individual fundamentals temporarily irrelevant. Building resilient portfolios requires acknowledging this spillover dynamic rather than assuming statistical [correlation matrices](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) remain stable in the bad states that matter most.
