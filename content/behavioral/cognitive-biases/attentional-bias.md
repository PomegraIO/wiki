*Investors do not process all available information equally. Assets that have featured prominently in news, social media, or conversation receive disproportionate money flows and attention, regardless of whether the coverage reflects genuine value. This **attentional bias**—the systematic mismatch between attention received and fundamental importance—helps explain speculative bubbles, momentum trading, and why seemingly identical assets trade at different valuations simply because one is more "visible."*

## Limited attention and portfolio selection

Investors face a torrent of information: thousands of publicly traded securities, countless economic metrics, endless news flow. No rational person can analyse everything. In practice, investors focus on a small subset of the investable universe—stocks in their sector, companies they work for, assets they have heard about. This necessity of selection is itself neutral; the bias arises because selection correlates not with importance but with salience.

Attentional bias is rooted in a simple fact: attention is scarce. Only so many market participants can hold a position in a stock at once; only so much capital can flow into an asset on any given day. When attention floods a particular security—due to news coverage, viral social media, analyst upgrades, or celebrity endorsement—more investors allocate their limited attention budget to it. They buy more of it than its fundamental valuation justifies, driving the price above intrinsic value.

The classic mechanism is the "attention-grabbing" event. A company announces an exciting acquisition, a scandal breaks, or a CEO makes a provocative statement. Media coverage spikes, retail traders pile in, and the stock rallies sharply. Months later, once attention has faded to something else, the stock often underperforms as the initial euphoria or panic unwinds.

## Empirical evidence of overweighting high-attention assets

Research by Gur Huberman and others in the 1990s and 2000s found that US stocks receiving disproportionate news coverage showed abnormally high trading volumes and short-term outperformance, followed by mean reversion. Stocks mentioned frequently in The Wall Street Journal, or those that were household names (Apple, Microsoft), commanded attention premiums: they traded at higher valuations relative to fundamentals than obscure firms with similar risk and profitability.

More recent work has quantified this effect precisely. Stocks receiving high news-media attention (number of news articles) show elevated trading volume and temporary price spikes, consistent with a flood of retail attention. Crucially, this "attention effect" is strongest among retail investors and smallest among institutional investors with research teams—reinforcing the idea that institutional actors can afford to process information more broadly, whilst retails buyers rely on salient headlines.

High Google search volume for a ticker also predicts short-term outperformance: stocks heavily searched by retail investors that day or week tend to rise on that attention spike, then revert. This is pure attention-driven trading, divorced from valuation changes. Over the medium term (months), these attention-driven gainers typically underperform, as the initial excitement fades and the market reprices based on fundamentals.

## Manifestations: momentum, bubbles, and sector rotations

Attentional bias manifests visibly in [momentum trading](/momentum-trading/), where recent winners attract more attention and capital inflows, driving further price rises until some catalyst (e.g., earnings disappointment, sector rotation) shifts attention away. The technology bubble of the late 1990s was partly fuelled by attentional bias: internet companies received an outsized share of news coverage and retail attention, driving some firms to billion-dollar valuations on no revenue. Once attention shifted (via the sharp 2000–2002 correction), the repricing was severe.

Sector rotations also reflect attention dynamics. When commodity prices spike due to geopolitical events or supply shocks, commodity equities receive a flood of media coverage and analyst upgrades. Investors overwhelmingly allocate capital to energy or metals stocks, sometimes at stretched valuations, because attention is there. When commodity prices stabilise or fall, attention evaporates, and the money flows reverse.

Meme stocks (GameStop, AMC) are perhaps the purest example of attentional bias in action. These companies received minimal institutional attention for years, but social media (Reddit, Twitter) made them household names among retail traders. The resulting inflows and short squeezes drove prices to levels divorced from discounted cash flow by any reasonable standard. Once retail attention moved to the next meme stock, the original rallies collapsed.

## The salience effect and psychological mechanisms

Attentional bias is closely linked to salience—the psychological ease with which an idea comes to mind. Assets and events that are recently in the news, visually striking (e.g., a logo everyone knows), or personally relevant (a company you work for, or a favourite brand) come to mind effortlessly. By [availability heuristic](/availability-heuristic/), investors overestimate the probability and importance of salient items.

A related mechanism is "narrative bias": investors are swayed by compelling stories. A company with a charismatic founder, a revolutionary product, or a big macro narrative (e.g., "AI will transform everything") captures attention and attracts capital disproportionately. The narrative need not be false; it just needs to be told and retold in news and forums, making it salient.

Yet another factor is "ticker symbolism": even the stock's ticker symbol affects attention and trading. Stocks with appealing tickers or tickers that spell words (LOVE, GOLD, WORK) receive higher trading volumes than otherwise similar firms, a pure attentional effect. Brokerages that list a stock prominently (e.g., on the home page of a trading app) see higher trading volumes from retail clients, again independent of the stock's merits.

## The role of social media and information cascades

Social media has amplified attentional bias dramatically. Twitter, Reddit, and TikTok allow memes and narratives to propagate virally within seconds. A stock mentioned positively by an influencer or in a trending thread can attract thousands of retail traders' attention simultaneously. Information cascades—where each new buyer's attention-driven purchase signals "this must be worth it," attracting more—can drive prices far above fundamentals before reversing sharply.

The phenomenon has become so pronounced that some retail traders explicitly track news sentiment and social-media activity as trading signals, chasing "hot stocks." This is rational from an individual trader's perspective if she believes she can time the attention cycle before others; but in aggregate, it amplifies volatility and occasional mispricings.

## Medium-term returns and market efficiency

A key finding is that attentional bias generates predictable return patterns. High-attention stocks tend to outperform in the short term (weeks to months) due to attention-driven buying, then underperform over longer horizons (6–18 months) as the market reprices based on fundamentals. This creates a "drift" that sophisticated investors can exploit: selling attention-driven rallies and buying the resulting dips.

This pattern violates the weak form of the efficient market hypothesis, which posits prices reflect all publicly available information. Yet it is consistent with behavioural finance: markets are "efficient" in that the attention-driven mispricing is not permanent, but they exhibit predictable short-term biases driven by cognitive limits.

## Implications for individual investors and portfolio construction

For individual investors, awareness of attentional bias suggests caution when an asset is in the news. Attention spikes may offer tactical selling opportunities (ride the momentum, exit before reversal) but rarely justify long-term overweighting. Professional investors often explicitly avoid "hot" sectors during attention peaks, viewing the attention as a sign of overvaluation.

For portfolio construction, attentional bias argues for diversification into less-salient, smaller-cap, or international assets that may be underweight in retail portfolios but offer better fundamental value. Many value investors deliberately target "neglected" stocks—those receiving minimal analyst coverage or media attention—betting that the attention deficit has pushed valuations below intrinsic value.
