*A **fixed income fund strategy** invests primarily in [bonds](/wiki/bond/), [floating-rate notes](/wiki/floating-rate-note/), and other debt instruments, aiming to generate steady [coupon](/wiki/coupon-payment/) income while preserving capital and capturing [price appreciation](/wiki/bond-price-formula/) as rates decline or credit quality improves. The strategy's returns derive from [yield](/wiki/yield-to-maturity/), [credit spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/), [duration](/wiki/duration/) management, and timing across the [yield curve](/wiki/yield-curve/).*

For equity-focused income, see [dividend investing](/wiki/dividend-investing/). For strategies blending stocks and bonds, see [balanced fund](/wiki/balanced-fund-strategy/).

## Core mechanics

A [bond](/wiki/bond/) pays periodic [coupons](/wiki/coupon-payment/) (interest) and returns principal at maturity. A [fixed income fund](/wiki/fixed-income-fund-strategy/) pools these bonds, delivering a dividend stream to shareholders. The fund's net asset value (NAV) fluctuates with bond prices, which move inversely to interest rates and as credit risk shifts.

**Example:** A fund holds $100 million in bonds yielding 4% = $4 million annual coupon income. After expenses (0.5% = $500k), the fund distributes $3.5 million as dividends. If bond prices rise 2% (due to falling rates), the fund's NAV appreciates 2%, and shareholders gain both income and capital appreciation.

## Duration and interest rate sensitivity

A key bond property is [duration](/wiki/duration/) — a measure of how much the bond's price swings when rates shift. A 10-year bond with 5% duration drops ~5% if rates rise 1%.

Fixed-income fund strategies are classified partly by duration:

- **Short-duration funds** (2–4 year duration): less sensitive to rate moves, lower yield, popular when rising-rate environments loom.
- **Intermediate-duration funds** (4–7 years): balanced yield and rate sensitivity; the core [intermediate-maturity](/wiki/bond-maturity-corporate/) segment.
- **Long-duration funds** (10+ years): highly sensitive to rate moves, much higher yield, attractive in falling-rate environments but risky if rates spike.

## Credit quality and spread strategy

Bonds are ranked by credit quality:

- **Government bonds:** U.S. Treasuries, rated AAA (highest safety, lowest yield).
- **Investment-grade corporate:** rated BBB– or higher, balance safety and yield.
- **High-yield (junk) bonds:** rated BB or lower, high coupon to compensate for default risk.

A [fixed-income fund](/wiki/fixed-income-fund-strategy/) might:
- **Own 100% Treasuries** (safest, ~3–4% yield in normal environments).
- **Blend Treasuries and investment-grade corporate** (higher yield, minimal default risk).
- **Emphasize [high-yield bonds](/wiki/high-yield-bond/)** ("junk bond funds," 6–10% yield, higher default risk).

Fund managers earn alpha by identifying [credit spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) poised to tighten (bonds undervalued, likely to appreciate as investor confidence rises) or widen (overvalued, likely to fall if conditions deteriorate).

## Sector and duration positioning

A fund manager might:
- **Overweight financials** if bank [capital ratios](/wiki/capital-adequacy/) are strong and [credit spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) are fat.
- **Tilt toward floating-rate bonds** if expected to rise (manager predicts rate hikes).
- **Steepen the [yield curve](/wiki/yield-curve/) positioning** by buying 30-year bonds and selling 5-year bonds (benefiting if long-end yields fall faster than short-end, a common pattern).

## Types of fixed-income funds

**[Bond ETFs](/wiki/bond-etf/):** Passive tracking of broad indexes (Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index, high-yield index) or factor-tilted indexes (credit quality, maturity, geography).

**[Income funds](/wiki/income-fund/):** Actively managed, focused on maximizing current yield while managing [default risk](/wiki/default-rate/). Common in retirement contexts.

**[Multisector bond funds](/wiki/multisector-bond-implicitly/):** Blend of government, corporate, mortgage-backed, and emerging-market bonds, giving managers flexibility to rotate across sectors.

**[TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities)](/wiki/tips/) funds:** Own inflation-protected bonds, appropriate for investors fearing inflation erosion.

**[Municipal bond](/wiki/municipal-bond/) funds:** Own tax-exempt bonds, appealing to high-income earners in high-tax states.

**[Emerging market debt](/wiki/emerging-market-debt-implicitly/) funds:** higher-yielding but higher-risk debt from developing-economy issuers.

## Distribution and reinvestment

Most [fixed-income funds](/wiki/fixed-income-fund-strategy/) distribute income monthly (many [income funds](/wiki/income-fund/)) or quarterly. Shareholders can reinvest dividends in additional fund shares (automatic via [dividend reinvestment plan](/wiki/dividend-reinvestment-plan/)) or take cash.

During rising-rate environments, older [bond funds](/wiki/bond-etf/) with high portfolio yields (purchased when rates were higher) may continue paying high dividends *while* the fund's NAV declines (because those bonds' prices fall as rates rise). This creates a disconnect — shareholders get high income but declining principal.

## Interest rate risk and ladders

A [bond ladder](/wiki/bond-ladder/) strategy (holding bonds maturing in 1, 3, 5, 10 years) is simple and popular: it creates a steady income stream, caps [reinvestment risk](/wiki/reinvestment-risk/), and avoids the need to predict rate direction.

In contrast, a [bullet strategy](/wiki/bullet-strategy/) concentrates in one maturity (e.g., all 10-year bonds), betting rates won't spike sharply.

## Duration management in a changing-rate world

If a fund expects rising rates, managers shorten [duration](/wiki/duration/) (sell long-dated bonds, buy short-dated) to minimize losses. If they expect falling rates, they extend [duration](/wiki/duration/) (buy long-dated, sell short-dated) to capture appreciation.

This active [duration](/wiki/duration/) management is the primary driver of outperformance or underperformance versus a passive [bond index](/wiki/bond-etf/).

## Performance drivers and risks

**Positive drivers:** falling rates, tightening [credit spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/), improving credit fundamentals, [coupon](/wiki/coupon-payment/) income.

**Negative drivers:** rising rates, widening [credit spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/), credit downgrades, defaults in the portfolio, rising [inflation](/wiki/inflation/) eroding real returns.

## Fixed income vs. equities

[Fixed-income funds](/wiki/fixed-income-fund-strategy/) typically offer lower volatility and more stable income than equity funds, making them suitable for conservative investors or those in retirement. However, they sacrifice the upside of [equity](/wiki/common-stock/) [growth investing](/wiki/growth-investing/). A [balanced fund](/wiki/balanced-fund-strategy/) blends both for moderate risk and growth.
