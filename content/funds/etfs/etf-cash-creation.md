*An **ETF Cash Creation** mechanism allows [authorized participants](/authorized-participant/) to create [ETF](/etf/) shares by depositing cash instead of a basket of securities. This approach is standard for [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/)s, [inverse ETF](/inverse-etf/)s, and other derivatives-heavy funds where [in-kind transfer](/etf-in-kind-transfer/) of the underlying exposure would be impractical or impossible.*

## Why cash creation exists

The standard [ETF](/etf/) creation model hinges on [authorized participants](/authorized-participant/) assembling a [creation unit](/etf-creation-unit/) of securities and delivering it to the fund. The fund issues new shares in exchange. This works seamlessly for an [index fund](/index-fund/) holding 500 stocks or a [bond ETF](/bond-etf/) holding a basket of bonds. The AP can buy those securities in the market, hand them over, and walk away with new shares to sell.

But a [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) holding futures contracts, or an [inverse ETF](/inverse-etf/) using [swap](/swap/) agreements, or a commodity [ETF](/etf/) holding physical crude oil cannot function this way. An AP cannot easily buy three-times-leveraged S&P futures and deliver them as a basket. Nor can an AP assemble a set of short equity swaps the way it would assemble stocks.

For these funds, cash creation is the only practical approach. Instead of transferring securities, the AP deposits cash equal to the [creation unit](/etf-creation-unit/) value. The fund receives the cash, uses it to buy futures, swaps, or other derivatives that generate the desired exposure (3× leverage, inverse return, commodity upside), and issues new shares.

## The cash creation flow

The process mirrors standard creation in structure but diverges in mechanics. The AP identifies a creation opportunity—the fund trading at a premium to NAV—and deposits cash with the fund (or through a custodian). The cash amount must equal the current [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) of the [creation unit](/etf-creation-unit/), just as with in-kind creation.

The fund receives the cash and immediately deploys it. A [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) fund manager buys Treasury [futures](/futures-contract/) or equity index futures, borrowing to create the extra leverage. An [inverse ETF](/inverse-etf/) buys short swaps or puts. The fund's portfolio now includes the new derivative position, and the fund issues shares equivalent to the cash amount at NAV.

The AP, holding the newly created shares, sells them into the market at the premium price and keeps the spread. The fund holds the cash and has converted it into derivatives that track the fund's promised exposure.

Redemptions work similarly in reverse. When an AP redeems shares, instead of receiving securities, it receives cash equal to the [redemption](/redemption-rights-equity/) NAV. The fund must then liquidate some derivatives—sell futures, close swaps—to raise the cash. Those sales may realise gains or losses.

## Tax consequences and the advantage

The cash creation mechanism introduces a wrinkle absent from [in-kind transfer](/etf-in-kind-transfer/): the fund must sell assets to raise cash for redemptions. When a [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) closes a [futures contract](/futures-contract/) that has appreciated, the fund realises a [capital gain](/capital-gains-tax-investor/). These gains can flow through to remaining shareholders.

This is why cash-creation [ETF](/etf/)s often have higher [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) distributions than their [in-kind](/etf-in-kind-transfer/) cousins. A [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) holding 3× S&P 500 futures might distribute realised gains annually if the fund has been liquidating winners to pay redeeming APs.

However, some commodity [ETF](/etf/)s using cash creation are held in accounts structured to defer gains—[IRA](/traditional-ira/)s, [401(k)](/401k-plan/)s, or non-U.S. investor accounts—where the gain distribution doesn't matter to the end investor. For those accounts, cash creation is as efficient as [in-kind](/etf-in-kind-transfer/).

Cash creation also incurs a second cost: the fund must actively manage its cash and derivatives, not merely hold a static basket of securities. A [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) must continuously rebalance its futures position to maintain the promised leverage multiple, rolling contracts as they approach [expiration](/expiration-contracts/). These rebalances incur trading costs that are embedded in the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/).

## Redemption efficiency

One advantage of cash creation is speed and certainty. An AP redeeming from a [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) knows it will receive cash, not a basket of futures contracts it must liquidate itself. The fund handles the forced selling and delivers cash in a standard settlement cycle.

This matters for [inverse ETF](/inverse-etf/)s in particular. Imagine an AP trying to redeem shares from a fund holding short swaps. The AP cannot easily liquidate short swaps in secondary markets; most swaps are bespoke dealer instruments. But the fund can, because it has an ongoing relationship with swap dealers. The fund closes the swaps it wrote, pockets or pays the difference, and delivers cash.

In this regard, cash creation is more operationally straightforward than forcing an AP to handle exotic derivatives. The cost—potential [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) distributions and higher trading friction—is offset by the fact that the creation-redemption mechanism can function at all.

## Comparison to in-kind transfer

[In-kind transfer](/etf-in-kind-transfer/) is generally preferable for tax-sensitive investors because the fund avoids realised gains. But [in-kind](/etf-in-kind-transfer/) only works when the underlying portfolio consists of tradeable securities. A [bond ETF](/bond-etf/) can use [in-kind](/etf-in-kind-transfer/); a [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) cannot.

Most [index fund](/index-fund/)s and [active-etf](/active-etf/)s holding equities or bonds use [in-kind](/etf-in-kind-transfer/). Cash creation dominates [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/)s, [inverse ETF](/inverse-etf/)s, commodity [ETF](/etf/)s, and [thematic ETF](/thematic-etf/)s with illiquid or hard-to-source holdings.

The distinction also affects how investors should think about [ETF](/etf/) tax efficiency. A straightforward large-cap [ETF](/etf/) using [in-kind](/etf-in-kind-transfer/) creation can hold for decades with minimal [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) drag. A [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) using cash creation and rebalancing will generate annual gains—one reason [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/)s are best held in [tax-deferred](/traditional-ira/) accounts.

## Custody and operational logistics

The fund's custodian plays a central role in cash creation. The AP deposits cash with the custodian (who holds the fund's assets), the custodian credits the fund's account, and the fund manager instructs the custodian to buy derivatives. The custodian may also handle margin requirements, funding costs, and [futures contract](/futures-contract/) rollovers for the manager.

This arrangement means the fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) includes not just management fees but also custody costs, financing charges, and rebalancing slippage. A simple [index fund](/index-fund/) using [in-kind](/etf-in-kind-transfer/) transfer keeps these costs minimal. A [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) using cash creation bears larger operational overhead.
