*A **bond ETF** is an [ETF](/etf/) that holds a basket of [bonds](/bond/) — government, corporate, or a mix — and trades on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) throughout the day. Bond ETFs give individual investors access to diversified fixed-income portfolios with low cost and the daily liquidity of an exchange-traded security.*

## Why bond ETFs exist

Before bond ETFs arrived, accessing a diversified fixed-income portfolio was difficult for individuals. You could buy individual bonds—but minimum purchases were often $1,000 or $5,000, and the bid-ask spreads on the bond market were wide. Alternatively, you could own a [bond mutual fund](/mutual-fund/), but these typically cost more and priced only once per day.

Bond ETFs solved this problem. They let you:

- **Own dozens or hundreds of bonds** with a single purchase, spreading credit risk and interest-rate risk across many issuers.
- **Trade at tight spreads** throughout the day, just like stocks, thanks to [authorized participants](/authorized-participant/) continuously creating and redeeming shares.
- **Pay minimal fees.** A broad bond ETF might charge 0.05% annually, a fraction of what a mutual fund or direct bond ownership costs.
- **See holdings daily,** so you know exactly what credit exposure you have.

## Common types of bond ETFs

Bond ETFs come in multiple flavors, each with a different risk-return profile:

**Treasury ETFs.** Hold US government bonds, ranging from short-term (bills and notes) to long-term (30-year bonds). These carry almost no credit risk—the US government is the safest borrower—but are sensitive to [interest rate](/interest-rate/) changes. A Treasury ETF holding long bonds will drop sharply if the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises rates.

**Corporate bond ETFs.** Hold bonds issued by companies, offering higher [yield](/bond/) than Treasuries but with credit risk. An investment-grade corporate ETF holds bonds rated BBB or higher; a high-yield (or "junk") bond ETF holds below-investment-grade debt from riskier issuers.

**Municipal bond ETFs.** Hold state and local government bonds, many of which are tax-exempt at the federal level (and sometimes state level). These appeal mainly to high-income investors in high-tax jurisdictions.

**Emerging market bond ETFs.** Hold bonds issued by governments or corporations in developing nations, offering higher yields but with currency risk and [inflation](/inflation/) risk.

**Short-duration, intermediate, and long-duration ETFs.** These differ by the weighted average maturity of their holdings. Shorter duration means less sensitivity to rate changes; longer duration means more.

## How bond ETFs price

Unlike stocks, which reflect the expectations of millions of traders, bonds are less liquid and prices move more on [interest rate](/interest-rate/) changes. A bond ETF's price reflects:

1. **The yield of its holdings.** If the fund holds bonds yielding 4% annually, the fund itself typically yields close to 4%, minus [expenses](/expense-ratio/).
2. **Interest rate direction.** If the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) begins hiking rates, newly issued bonds will offer higher yields, making existing bonds (which pay fixed coupons) less attractive. The price of the ETF falls.
3. **Credit spreads.** If the financial system weakens and investors demand higher compensation for credit risk, corporate bond prices fall, pulling down the ETF.

This inverse relationship between bond prices and rates is crucial: in a rising-rate environment, bond ETF prices decline; in a falling-rate environment, they rise.

## Bond ETFs in a portfolio

Bond ETFs typically serve as the fixed-income anchor in a [diversified](/diversification/) portfolio. Because [stocks](/stock/) and bonds tend to move in opposite directions over long periods—stocks soaring when growth is strong, bonds rallying when growth stalls and rates fall—owning both reduces portfolio volatility.

The split between [equity](/equity-etf/) and bond ETFs is usually driven by [asset allocation](/asset-allocation/): a conservative 60/40 portfolio holds 60% in stocks and 40% in bonds; an aggressive portfolio might be 80/20 or even 90/10.

## Risks and limitations

Bond ETFs carry several distinct risks. **Interest rate risk** is the most obvious: if rates rise, bond prices fall, and the ETF price falls with them. A fund holding long-term bonds amplifies this effect.

**Credit risk** applies to corporate, municipal, and emerging market bond ETFs. If an issuer defaults or its credit rating falls, the bond (and the ETF) loses value.

**Inflation risk** is subtle but real. If inflation accelerates and erodes the purchasing power of fixed coupons, bond returns suffer. Long-duration bonds are most vulnerable.

**Liquidity risk** in the underlying bonds can create [bid-ask spreads](/etf-bid-ask-spread/) in the ETF itself, especially for specialty bond ETFs with less liquid holdings.
