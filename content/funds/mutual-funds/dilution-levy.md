*A **dilution levy** is a charge applied to investors making large subscriptions or redemptions in a mutual fund, designed to protect remaining shareholders from bearing the transaction costs of those trades. Unlike management fees, which are ongoing, a dilution levy is a one-time adjustment imposed on the entry or exit of significant capital, making the investor who triggers the trade pay its true cost rather than socializing it across the fund.*

## Why large trades damage remaining shareholders

When an investor redeems a large block of shares, the fund must sell securities to raise cash. That sale incurs brokerage commissions, market-impact costs, and bid-ask spreads. In the absence of a levy, the fund absorbs these costs, which reduces the net asset value (NAV) of all remaining shares — a form of hidden dilution. The redeeming investor walks away unscathed, while the stayers absorb the damage.

A similar problem occurs on subscription. A large inflow of cash must be invested, and the act of buying securities incurs transaction costs. Without a levy, those costs are borne by the fund and depress returns for existing shareholders.

The dilution levy solves this by charging the transacting investor a percentage (typically 0.1% to 1% of the transaction) that is paid directly into the fund to offset these costs.

## How it works in practice

A fund might apply a dilution levy of 0.5% on redemptions above a threshold—say, $1 million or 5% of daily net inflows. An investor redeeming $10 million would pay an additional 0.5%, or $50,000, on top of the NAV value of their shares. That $50,000 flows into the fund, reducing the impact of the sale on remaining shareholders' NAV.

Dilution levies are sometimes called **anti-dilution charges** or **swing pricing** adjustments, though swing pricing is technically a broader mechanism that adjusts the fund's NAV itself rather than charging a separate fee. The economic outcome is similar: the cost of the transaction is borne by the person causing it.

Most major mutual fund providers disclose their dilution-levy policies in the prospectus. Some funds apply them only to redemptions; others apply them symmetrically to both subscriptions and redemptions. The threshold for triggering a levy varies widely—from as low as $250,000 to several million dollars.

## The fairness argument

Dilution levies are broadly accepted as equitable. The economic logic is sound: if your trade creates costs, you should pay them. Without the levy, a fund's returns gradually degrade as large traders exit, imposing an invisible tax on long-term shareholders.

In markets where funds face frequent large redemptions (a common stress during downturns), dilution levies can be material. Conversely, in stable funds with modest flows, levies may rarely be charged and carry little economic impact.

## Transparency and disclosure

The SEC requires funds to disclose their dilution-levy policies clearly, including the conditions that trigger them and the rate applied. However, levies are sometimes conflated with redemption fees—which also charge exiting investors but are typically used to discourage short-term trading rather than to offset transaction costs. Understanding the fund's stated purpose is essential.

Some funds publish their actual transaction costs annually, allowing investors to assess how much dilution levy protection is truly needed. This transparency has become more common as funds compete on cost and fairness.

## Relationship to other fund costs

A dilution levy is distinct from [management fees](/management-fee/), which fund managers charge regardless of trading activity. It differs from [expense ratios](/expense-ratio/), which bundle all fund costs into a single percentage charged to all shareholders annually. And it complements [redemption fees](/redemption-rights-equity/), though the two address different problems: redemption fees discourage frequent trading; dilution levies compensate for unavoidable transaction costs.

In multi-class funds, where different share classes pay different [expense ratios](/expense-ratio/) and advisory fees, dilution levies are typically applied uniformly across all classes to protect the fund's NAV fairly.

## When levies are not charged

Small funds, index funds with low turnover, or funds in periods of net inflows rarely charge meaningful dilution levies because transaction costs are minimal. A [bond fund](/bond-etf/) holding highly liquid Treasuries might incur negligible transaction costs and forgo the levy entirely. An [equity fund](/equity-etf/) investing in large-cap stocks with tight bid-ask spreads may charge levies only during severe redemptions.

The decision to impose a dilution levy is a policy choice. Some fund managers argue that precise NAV adjustment (swing pricing) is fairer and more transparent than a levy; others prefer the levy as a simpler, more predictable charge.
