*An **interval fund** is a pooled investment vehicle that restricts investor redemptions to specific, pre-announced windows — typically quarterly — while allowing managers to hold illiquid or long-duration assets that a traditional [open-end mutual fund](/open-end-fund/) could not practically own. Understanding these liquidity rules is essential before committing capital to what appears to be a more liquid fund but operates under stricter redemption constraints.*

## Why redemption windows exist

Interval funds hold assets — middle-market loans, distressed debt, private placements — that lack a deep, liquid trading market. If the fund faced redemption requests daily, like a [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/), it would either have to hold expensive cash reserves or forced-sell illiquid assets at fire-sale prices. Redemption windows solve this problem by batching investor exit requests and allowing the manager to liquidate holdings in an orderly fashion.

A typical interval fund might offer a redemption window on the last business day of each quarter, with shareholders notifying the fund manager 30 days in advance of their intent to redeem. The manager then has months, if needed, to sell positions and pay redemptors from the proceeds. This structure allows the fund to invest in assets that might take weeks or months to convert to cash without the constant pressure of liquidity demands.

## How the windows work in practice

The redemption window is not optional for all shareholders. Interval funds are required to offer redemptions to all investors at least once every 13 months; many offer quarterly windows. Within each window, an investor may request redemption of up to a stated percentage of [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) (often 5–10 per quarter, sometimes higher). If total redemption requests exceed the fund's capacity — say, 20% of NAV is requested but the fund caps redemptions at 10% per quarter — all requests are proportionally reduced.

Once a shareholder submits a redemption request, it is binding. The fund is obligated to redeem at the [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) calculated on the redemption date (typically the last day of the window), though payment may be deferred for 30–90 days as the fund liquidates holdings. Early redemptions outside the window are generally not permitted, making interval funds unsuitable for investors who might need rapid access to capital.

Lock-up periods are common: a new investor in an interval fund might be unable to redeem for 12–24 months after initial purchase, even if a redemption window opens. This reinforces the fund's need for capital stability and ensures that money committed at inception can be put to work in longer-duration deals.

## Secondary market trading

Because regular redemptions are capped and infrequent, investors seeking early liquidity sometimes turn to the secondary market — over-the-counter trading of interval fund shares among investors. Unlike a [closed-end fund](/closed-end-fund/), which trades continuously on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/), interval fund shares typically trade on alternative venues or via direct negotiation.

Secondary-market prices for interval fund shares may diverge significantly from [net asset value](/net-asset-value/). If an investor is desperate to exit before the next redemption window, they may accept a steep discount (selling at 90 cents for every dollar of NAV). Conversely, in a strong market for the fund's strategy (e.g., strong middle-market lending), shares might trade at a slight premium.

The secondary market adds a layer of risk: finding a buyer may be difficult, spreads can be wide, and the buyer may demand a significant discount. An investor cannot count on secondary trading as a reliable exit; it is available only if another investor is willing to buy at a price both parties accept.

## Comparing interval funds to related structures

Interval funds differ from [tender offer funds](/tender-offer-fund-vs-interval-fund/), which also restrict redemptions but typically allow quarterly or semi-annual offers to repurchase shares at NAV and require the fund to retire a portion of outstanding shares if insufficient capital is available. Tender offer funds are sometimes more investor-friendly in that the fund is obligated to retire shares at NAV, not just offer the opportunity. [Closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/) trade daily on exchanges, offering higher liquidity but with potential [discount-to-NAV](/closed-end-fund-discount-to-nav/) volatility. Traditional [open-end mutual funds](/open-end-fund/) offer daily [redemption rights](/redemption-rights-equity/) but typically invest in liquid securities.

## Fee transparency and risks

Interval funds charge [management fees](/management-fee/) ranging from 1% to 2% annually, roughly in line with [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) or [private equity funds](/private-equity-fund/), plus occasional performance or [incentive fees](/performance-fee/). Because the manager controls a stable, long-term capital base, the economics can support investments in illiquid, high-touch assets — loans requiring covenant monitoring, distressed situations, or long-duration bonds.

However, the fee burden compounds the liquidity sacrifice. An investor who cannot redeem for two years and pays 1.5% annual fees has sunk 3% before any market gains. Over longer periods, that fee drag is material. Moreover, if the fund's strategy underperforms and the investor cannot redeem, the penalty is locked in.

Performance risk is magnified by lack of liquidity. An investor who realizes a portfolio mistake cannot simply exit; they must wait for the next window and potentially face a redemption cap or secondary-market discount. The fund structure assumes a long-term, patient investor; it penalizes those who need to rebalance quickly.

## Regulatory oversight and disclosure

Interval funds are regulated under the [Investment Company Act of 1940](/investment-company-act-of-1940/) and SEC Rule 23c-3, which prescribes the frequency and mechanics of redemption offers. Managers must disclose portfolio holdings periodically, estimate portfolio turnover, and publish [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) quarterly at minimum, though daily NAV calculation is not required.

The prospectus must clearly state redemption windows, caps, lock-up periods, and fee structures. Investors should read carefully, as terms vary widely. Some funds may have tighter caps (5% per quarter) than others (10%), and some may have longer lock-ups. These details materially affect the true liquidity available to different investor types.

## Who should consider interval funds?

Interval funds suit investors with a multi-year time horizon, stable capital (money not needed within the foreseeable redemption windows), and a willingness to sacrifice liquidity for potentially higher returns. Those seeking exposure to middle-market loans, restructured debt, or illiquid corporate securities cannot easily gain that exposure elsewhere except through [private equity](/private-equity-fund/) or [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/), which may have much higher minimums.

Conversely, investors who might need to access capital on short notice, or who dislike fee drag and locked-in positions, are better served by [open-end mutual funds](/open-end-fund/) or [ETFs](/etf/), even if those vehicles offer less exotic return profiles. The illiquidity of interval funds is a feature for the manager (allowing investment in longer-duration, higher-returning assets) and a cost for the investor (delayed access, potential redemption caps, secondary-market discounts).
