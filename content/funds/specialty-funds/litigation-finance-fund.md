*A **litigation finance fund** (also called "legal finance," "litigation funding," or "litigation finance vehicle") pools capital from investors to finance legal disputes on behalf of plaintiffs or law firms. The fund provides upfront capital for case costs (expert testimony, document discovery, court fees) and receives a negotiated percentage of the eventual settlement or judgment as repayment plus profit. Returns depend entirely on case outcomes: a successful settlement or judgment generates proceeds, while a loss or dismissal destroys the investment. This asset class blends insurance logic (diversifying risk across dozens of cases), venture-capital returns (high upside on winners), and long and uncertain holding periods.*

## How Litigation Finance Funds Work

A litigation finance fund operates as follows:

**1. Capital sourcing**: The fund raises capital from institutions (pension funds, endowments, insurance companies) or high-net-worth individuals. Initial commitments often range from $10 million to $500 million or more.

**2. Case selection and funding**: The fund identifies lawsuits where it can provide funding. A plaintiff or their law firm applies, providing case details, evidence, and a rough damage estimate. The fund's in-house attorneys and case analysts evaluate the lawsuit's merit, likely recovery, and timeline. If approved, the fund advances capital—say, $500,000 to $5 million—to cover discovery, depositions, expert witnesses, trial preparation, and other litigation costs.

**3. Funding agreement terms**: The fund negotiates a repayment structure. A typical arrangement: the fund receives 20–40% of the gross settlement or judgment if the case settles or wins at trial. If the case loses, the fund loses its capital investment (total loss). Some agreements also cap the fund's recovery or include clawback provisions if appeals reverse a judgment.

**4. Portfolio diversification**: Rather than betting on a single case, a fund holds a diversified portfolio of 30–100+ active cases across practice areas (commercial disputes, personal injury, employment law, environmental claims, etc.). This diversification smooths outcomes: when some cases win and others lose, the portfolio-level return depends on the aggregate hit rate and average recovery ratio.

**5. Resolution and distributions**: As cases settle or conclude, the fund receives its agreed share of proceeds. Distributions flow back to investors proportionally. A case might take 2–5 years from funding to settlement, creating a lumpy, long-dated return profile.

## The Risk and Return Profile

Litigation finance is a **high-risk, long-duration asset class** with binary outcomes per case but portfolio diversification at the fund level.

**Return drivers:**
- **Win rate**: Percentage of funded cases that result in settlement or judgment in the plaintiff's favor. A strong fund might achieve a 60–75% win rate; a weak fund, 40–50%.
- **Recovery ratio**: The percentage of claimed damages actually recovered. A case claimed to have $10 million in damages might settle for $3 million (30% recovery). Recovery ratios vary by practice area and stage of litigation.
- **Funding percentage**: The proportion of the settlement the fund claims. Funding 30% of litigation costs at 25% of recovery is more favorable than funding 80% of costs at 30% of recovery.

A well-run fund with a 65% win rate, 40% average recovery ratio, and 25% take of proceeds might achieve a 25–35% gross IRR across its portfolio. After fund fees (typically 2–2.5% annually plus 15–20% of profit), net IRR to investors might be 15–25%.

**Risk drivers:**
- **Case loss**: Total loss of capital invested in any single case. Unlike equities, there is no partial recovery if the case is dismissed.
- **Delay and holdover**: If a case drags on longer than expected (appeals, settlement renegotiations), the investor's capital is locked up and earns no interim return, suppressing IRR.
- **Adverse judgment or appeal reversal**: Even a settlement can be vacated or reduced on appeal, reducing or eliminating recovery.
- **Concentration risk**: If the fund backs a few mega-cases (e.g., a major class action claiming $500 million in damages), one adverse ruling affects the entire portfolio IRR dramatically.
- **Liquidity risk**: No secondary market exists for litigation finance stakes; capital is illiquid for the duration of the underlying cases.

## Practice Areas and Case Types

Litigation finance funds back a variety of disputes:

- **Commercial litigation**: Contract disputes, breach of fiduciary duty, antitrust claims. Often higher damages, but longer and more complex.
- **Personal injury**: Catastrophic injury claims (wrongful death, permanent disability). Faster settlement, but lower average damages than commercial cases.
- **Employment law**: Discrimination, wrongful termination, wage-and-hour class actions. Medium timeline and damages.
- **Product liability and mass torts**: Consumer injury claims (defective products, pharmaceutical injuries). Large damages but high defense costs and long trials.
- **Environmental and toxic tort**: Claims of contamination or environmental damage. Very long timelines (5–10 years) and highly uncertain outcomes.
- **Intellectual property**: Patent disputes, trademark infringement. Unpredictable juries and appeal courts; high volatility in outcomes.

A diversified fund spreads bets across practice areas, geographies, and defendant types (large corporates, mid-cap companies, individuals). This diversification is critical to smoothing portfolio returns.

## Due Diligence and Case Evaluation

Litigation finance funds employ or contract with experienced attorneys and case analysts who evaluate every submission. Key criteria include:

- **Strength of evidence**: Is there documentary proof, witness testimony, or expert analysis supporting the plaintiff's claim?
- **Damages assessment**: Can the plaintiff quantify the harm credibly? Are damages claimed reasonable relative to past settlements in similar cases?
- **Defendant's ability to pay**: Even a strong case is worthless if the defendant is judgment-proof. Funds prefer defendants with deep pockets (insurers, large corporations).
- **Litigation timeline**: How far along is the case? Cases in early discovery have higher uncertainty but also longer duration and more uncertainty. Cases at summary judgment or trial stage have clearer outcomes but imminent resolutions.
- **Legal representation**: Is the plaintiff's counsel experienced and well-resourced, or is this their first big case? Attorney quality correlates with success rates.
- **Court jurisdiction and judge**: Some courts and judges have reputations for favoring plaintiffs or defendants. Venue matters.

Rigorous due diligence reduces downside risk but also excludes many cases, meaning funds typically back only 5–15% of submitted proposals.

## Fee Structure and Return Calculations

A typical litigation finance fund charges:

- **Management fee**: 2–2.5% of committed capital annually, covering operational costs and attorney salaries.
- **Carry (performance fee)**: 15–20% of profits above a hurdle rate (often 0% or a low single digit), aligning the manager's interests with investors.
- **Interest or funding spread**: Sometimes the fund negotiates a 1–2% annual interest charge on the capital it advances, compensating for the time value of the advance.

An investor committing $100 million to a 3-year fund with a 2% management fee and 20% carry pays $2 million per year upfront ($6 million over three years). If the fund generates $40 million in gross proceeds (40% total ROI), carry is 20% × $40M = $8 million. Net to the investor: $40M gross proceeds minus $6M in management fees minus $8M in carry = $26M returned, plus the initial $100M, for a 26% total return over three years (roughly 8% annualized IRR). Returns are highly sensitive to carry structure and gross recovery.

## Tax and Accounting Treatment

Litigation finance investments are typically structured as partnerships, so investors receive pass-through allocations of gains and losses. The character of income depends on the underlying lawsuit and the fund's structure, but it is often ordinary income rather than preferentially taxed [capital gains](/long-term-capital-gain-tax/). Detailed tax planning is important, especially for large commitments.

When a case is settled, the fund typically recognizes the gain at settlement. If an investor is in a high [marginal tax rate](/marginal-tax-rate-investor/), the lump-sum recognition can trigger a large tax bill in a single year. Some funds stagger settlements or allow investors to defer distributions, but liquidity is the primary constraint, not tax optionality.

## Correlation and Portfolio Role

Litigation finance returns are nearly uncorrelated with equities, bonds, or traditional [alternative investments](/liquid-alternative-fund-explained/). Legal outcomes are determined by courts and juries, not macroeconomic cycles. A recession does not improve or worsen a defendant's liability in a negligence claim. This makes litigation finance attractive as a diversifier within an institutional portfolio.

However, litigation finance is not a solution for investors seeking steady income or capital appreciation in line with GDP growth. The asset class is lumpy, illiquid, and highly concentrated—ideal for very long-term, flexible investors (endowments, pension funds) but unsuitable for anyone needing liquidity within 5 years.

## Risks and Criticisms

**Market-level risks**: The fund's returns depend on case outcomes, which are inherently uncertain. Unlike equities, there is no mechanism for a partial recovery if a lawsuit is dismissed mid-trial. Also, the pool of available cases varies by economic cycle and litigation volume, creating supply-side constraints.

**Manager quality**: Litigation finance is labor-intensive, requiring deep legal expertise. A fund managed by mediocre attorneys or analysts will have poor case selection and lower returns. Track record matters more than in traditional investments.

**Ethical concerns**: Some critics argue that litigation finance enables frivolous lawsuits by removing the plaintiff's financial stake. Regulatory bodies (especially in Australia, the UK, and Canada) have debated whether and how to regulate this industry.

**Concentration in mega-cases**: Large funds sometimes back a few blockbuster cases (e.g., a class action claiming billions) expecting outsized recovery. If these mega-cases lose or settle below expectations, the entire fund's IRR suffers.
