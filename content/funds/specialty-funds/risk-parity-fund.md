*A **risk parity fund** distributes capital across [equities](/stock/), [bonds](/bond/), and other assets such that each contributes equally to overall portfolio risk rather than holding equal dollar amounts in each. Because bonds are less volatile than stocks, a risk-parity portfolio holds more bonds in dollar terms; leverage is often used to boost bond returns to parity with equity [volatility](/historical-volatility/).*

## Why dollar weighting fails

A traditional 60/40 [portfolio](/asset-allocation/) holds 60% in [equities](/stock/) and 40% in [bonds](/bond/). In most market environments, equities are roughly three to five times more volatile than bonds. This means equity movements dominate portfolio swings, even though bond dollars are substantial. A 10% equity drawdown combined with a 2% bond rally can still hurt the overall portfolio because equity [volatility](/historical-volatility/) overwhelms bond stability. The investor is unknowingly betting heavily on stocks.

Risk parity asks: what if we instead allocate so that stocks and bonds contribute equally to the portfolio's ups and downs? Because bonds are quieter, we would need to hold more bonds in dollar terms. An extreme version might hold 35% equities and 65% bonds, with leverage applied to the bond sleeve to boost its return volatility to match equities. Both sleeves now pull the portfolio's overall volatility equally; neither dominates.

## The leverage question

Leverage is central to most risk-parity designs. Without it, equal-risk-contribution portfolios are often merely heavier on bonds with reduced overall returns. Using modest leverage—borrowing at low rates to increase bond positions—allows the fund to amplify bond returns without reducing equity exposure. The trade-off is [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) and the cost of financing, but proponents argue that the benefit of true [diversification](/diversification/) outweighs the friction.

During periods of rising interest rates and volatile bond markets, leverage amplifies losses. A bond position that falls 5% under pressure hits harder when it is leveraged 1.5x. This is the chief drawback: risk parity portfolios can suffer outsized losses when inflation spikes and bonds decline sharply, which is precisely when diversification is most needed.

## Correlations that shift

Risk parity assumes correlations between asset classes remain relatively stable. Historically, equities and bonds have been somewhat inversely correlated—when stocks crash, bond prices often rise as central banks cut rates. This inverse relationship is the reason diversification works. However, during periods of stagflation or simultaneous equity-and-bond selloffs (uncommon but not impossible), both asset classes can fall together. When correlations shift, the equal-risk framework no longer protects as expected.

A dramatic example occurred in early 2022, when [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) tightening pushed both equities and bonds downward simultaneously. Risk-parity portfolios suffered unusually large losses because the diversification benefit evaporated. The lesson is that no allocation strategy is crisis-proof; all assume some stability in the relationships that make diversification effective.

## Multi-asset expansion

Pure 60/40 risk parity has evolved. Modern funds often add commodities, real assets, and [cryptocurrencies](/cryptocurrency-exchange/) to the mix. Each new sleeve brings its own [volatility](/historical-volatility/) profile and correlation structure. Adding commodities, which have their own supply-and-demand dynamics, can enhance diversification in some environments but also introduce tracking complexity and [operational risk](/operational-risk/).

The broader trend is toward "all-weather" or "all-seasons" approaches that aim to perform reasonably in any inflation or growth regime. Some funds use factor-based tilts within each asset class (value vs. growth in equities, short-duration vs. long-duration bonds) to further refine the risk contribution.

## Cost and rebalancing

Risk-parity funds incur costs from frequent rebalancing. As market prices move, the [volatility](/historical-volatility/) of asset classes drifts, and the equal-risk balance deteriorates. The fund must periodically buy and sell to restore parity. This turnover generates trading costs and [tax drag](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) (especially in taxable accounts). Some funds use derivatives like futures to adjust positions more efficiently, reducing transaction costs.

[Management fees](/management-fee/) for risk-parity funds range from 0.5% to 1.5%, reflecting the complexity of ongoing optimization. Passive or index-based versions may be cheaper, but they sacrifice dynamic rebalancing and factor tilts.

## Performance in different regimes

Risk-parity funds shine when traditional [asset allocation](/asset-allocation/) fails—typically when stocks and bonds sell off together or when bonds outperform equities dramatically. In bull-market years dominated by tech equities, risk-parity lags because its equity weighting is modest. In periods of stable low inflation and falling rates, the leverage drag becomes visible; a simpler bond-heavy portfolio might outperform after fees.

The fund type is best suited to investors who prioritize steady returns and reduced drawdowns over maximum upside, and who can tolerate occasional periods of underperformance relative to a pure-equity or concentrated portfolio.
