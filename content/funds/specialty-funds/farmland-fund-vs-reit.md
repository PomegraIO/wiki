*Both **farmland funds** and **[REITs](/real-estate-investment-trust/)** offer exposure to productive land, but they work in fundamentally different ways. Farmland funds typically buy land directly and lease it to farmers, holding for the long term with minimal liquidity. REITs hold portfolios of real estate assets (commercial, residential, or agricultural) and distribute most income as dividends. The choice hinges on liquidity needs, tax preference, and whether you want agricultural or urban property exposure.*

## Structure and ownership

A **farmland fund** is typically a private partnership or LLC that acquires physical farmland and holds it long-term. Investors become limited partners (or shareholders) without owning the land directly; the fund holds title and manages operations. The fund leases land to farmers via multi-year contracts, collecting annual rental income. Capital appreciation accrues as land values rise.

A **[REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/)** is a publicly traded corporation that owns and operates real estate assets (apartments, warehouses, offices, or farmland) and distributes at least 90% of taxable income to shareholders as dividends. REITs are securities traded on stock exchanges with minimal liquidity friction; you can buy or sell within a day.

The practical difference: buying a farmland fund is like writing a large check to a fund manager and trusting them to deploy it over months or years; buying a REIT is like buying a stock.

## Liquidity and redemption terms

Liquidity is the primary structural dividing line. Farmland funds typically allow redemptions once per quarter or once per year, often with 30–90 days' notice and sometimes a redemption fee. If you need cash on short notice, you may face a delay or penalty. This illiquidity is priced in: farmland funds target higher returns to compensate for the liquidity friction.

REITs, by contrast, are liquid—you sell them in real time at market-determined prices, just like stocks. This liquidity comes with a cost: REIT prices are volatile, driven by interest-rate expectations, sector sentiment, and macroeconomic cycles. A farmland fund, with no public trading, avoids this daily repricing.

For an investor who knows they will not touch the capital for 10+ years, illiquidity is not a problem; farmland may offer better long-term returns. For an investor who may need liquidity or values the ability to rebalance quickly, a REIT is far more practical.

## Income streams and tax efficiency

Both produce income, but the character differs.

**Farmland funds** generate rental income from lease payments. Because the fund owns the land directly, it can claim [depreciation](/depreciation/) (even though farmland technically does not depreciate, depreciation is often allowed for tax purposes). Depreciation shelters some rental income from taxation, creating a tax-deferred component. When land is eventually sold, gains are taxed at [long-term capital gains](/long-term-capital-gain-tax/) rates.

**REITs** distribute at least 90% of taxable income; nearly all REIT distributions are taxed as ordinary income (at rates higher than [capital gains](/long-term-capital-gain-tax/)), though a small portion may be return of capital or long-term gains. The REIT itself pays no corporate tax (in exchange for the 90% distribution requirement), but shareholders bear the tax burden. For a REIT held in a taxable account, the tax drag is substantial.

**Tax-advantaged accounts** (401k, IRA, etc.) make this moot; both farmland and REITs are suitable, and the ordinary-income component of REIT distributions is no worse inside a tax-deferred wrapper.

For a taxable account, farmland funds can be more tax-efficient over long holding periods because depreciation shelters income and the eventual capital gain is taxed at preferential rates.

## Inflation and real returns

Both farmland and REITs are often pitched as inflation hedges. The logic differs:

**Farmland inflation hedge**: As inflation rises, so do crop prices and land rents. Farmers' output becomes more valuable; they can afford higher lease payments or land prices rise to capitalize higher future cash flows. Over 20+ years, farmland rents and values have historically tracked inflation reasonably well.

**REIT inflation hedge**: As inflation rises, so do rents (for apartments, offices, warehouses). If a REIT's lease portfolio reprices frequently, rents track inflation. However, long-term fixed leases break this link; if a REIT has 10-year leases signed at today's rates, inflation over those 10 years erodes returns. Also, rising inflation typically triggers higher [interest rates](/interest-rate/), which [hurt REIT valuations](/dividend-discount-model/) (REITs are sensitive to [discount rates](/discount-rate/)).

Empirically, farmland has been a better inflation hedge than REITs over recent decades, partly because farmland leases reprice more frequently and farmland has less financial leverage.

## Sector and property-type exposure

This is crucial for portfolio fit.

**Farmland funds** focus on productive agricultural land: row crops (corn, soybeans), orchards, dairies, vineyards. They own relatively few properties (sometimes 10–50 farms), so each is material. Geographic diversification is limited to arable regions (US Midwest, California, etc.). An investor in a farmland fund is placing a concentrated bet on agricultural commodity prices and rural land values.

**REITs** span many sectors:

- **Apartment REITs**: Residential multifamily
- **Office REITs**: Commercial offices
- **Logistics REITs**: Warehouses and fulfillment centers
- **Retail REITs**: Shopping centers
- **Agricultural REITs**: Farmland (but also other property)
- **Data center REITs**: Server farms for cloud computing
- **Specialty REITs**: Cell towers, self-storage, senior housing

A diversified REIT portfolio naturally provides property-type diversification that a single farmland fund cannot. But a concentrated farmland fund allows a thesis bet: "I believe farmland is undervalued relative to other real estate."

## Valuation and pricing mechanisms

Farmland fund valuations are often appraised once per year or per redemption period by independent appraisers. Valuations move slowly, reflecting underlying land values and rental income, with less volatility than public markets. This is a feature (no daily price shock) or a bug (you may be valuing at outdated prices).

REIT valuations move daily based on supply and demand, interest-rate expectations, and sentiment. A REIT trading below [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) signals skepticism; one trading above NAV signals enthusiasm. Over a full cycle, prices eventually converge to fundamentals, but the path is volatile.

For a risk-averse investor, farmland's slower appraisal process feels safer. For a tactical trader, REIT volatility creates opportunities.

## Minimum investment and access

Farmland funds typically require minimum investments of $25,000 to $100,000 or more, making them accessible mainly to accredited investors or wealthy individuals. REITs are accessible to any investor with a brokerage account, including fractional-share investing that allows positions as small as a few dollars.

For portfolio diversification, REITs are practical; farmland requires meaningful capital.

## Concentration and volatility characteristics

Farmland funds are concentrated in fewer, larger holdings (a handful of farms). Their valuations are less volatile, but loss recovery can be slow if land values decline. Returns compound steadily but modestly.

REITs are more liquid and offer broader diversification, but exhibit sector-driven volatility. A logistics REIT rallies during e-commerce boom; a retail REIT falls during brick-and-mortar decline. Interest-rate swings (which flow through the [discount rate](/discount-rate/) used to value real estate) can cause sharp price moves.

## Blended approach

Many investors use both. REITs provide [liquid real-estate exposure](/real-estate-investment-trust/) with diversification and no lock-up; farmland provides long-term, inflation-hedged returns with tax efficiency. A simple allocation might be:

- 40% diversified real-estate ETFs (REIT index)
- 10–15% farmland fund
- Rest in stocks, bonds, alternatives

This balances liquidity (REITs are instant) with long-term appreciation (farmland) and tax efficiency.
