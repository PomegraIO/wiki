*A **gate provision** is a clause in a fund's prospectus that allows the manager to cap the total redemption volume in a given period—typically a quarter or month—as a percentage of the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/). During acute market stress, gates prevent a rush of simultaneous redemptions from forcing the fund to liquidate positions at fire-sale prices.*

## The problem: redemption runs and fire sales

Imagine a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) managing a concentrated portfolio of illiquid credit securities, private equity stakes, and other complex assets. The market outlook darkens. Investors, spooked, rush to pull capital. The fund receives redemption requests for 40% of its NAV in a single week.

Without a gate, the manager faces an impossible choice: either meet redemptions by liquidating illiquid positions at whatever price they can fetch (inflicting losses on remaining shareholders), or impose ad hoc restrictions that feel arbitrary and unfair.

A gate provision short-circuits this dilemma by building redemption restrictions into the fund contract upfront. It says plainly: you may redeem up to X% per period, and if total requests exceed that, you wait.

This arrangement protects the majority of investors. By slowing the pace of redemptions, the fund preserves portfolio liquidity, avoids forced sales, and prevents a downward spiral where sales beget further panic.

## How gates work

A gate typically specifies a redemption cap as a percentage of NAV, calculated over a defined period. Common examples:

- 25% of NAV per month
- 50% of NAV per quarter
- 15% of NAV per week

When redemption requests exceed the gate, they are processed on a "first-in, first-out" basis or on a pro-rata basis. The most common approach is pro-rata: if the gate allows 30% of NAV to be redeemed and redemption requests total 60% of NAV, each investor redeeming receives 50% of their requested redemption in that period. The other 50% is queued for the next period.

Investors who don't request redemption are unaffected. They continue holding shares and (ideally) benefit from the fund's stabilized portfolio and lack of forced liquidations.

Gates can be invoked at the manager's discretion or, in some fund documents, at the board's discretion or automatically if certain triggers are met (net outflows exceeding a threshold, for example). The prospectus specifies the terms.

## Why investors accept gates upfront

It may seem odd that investors would voluntarily accept restrictions on their own capital. Yet gates are standard in [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) and sophisticated investment vehicles, and most investors agree to them knowingly.

The reason is straightforward: the alternative is worse. A fund without a gate, facing heavy redemptions, either performs poorly (because it's forced to liquidate at bad prices) or imposes emergency gates retroactively (which feels unfair and may face legal challenges). Agreeing to a gate upfront is a form of insurance: you sacrifice maximum liquidity in exchange for certainty that the fund won't wreck your returns by panic-selling into a crashing market.

Investors also appreciate that a gate applies to everyone equally. It prevents the "first-mover advantage" where the fastest redemptions get out at full NAV while slower investors suffer the consequences. Pro-rata queuing is seen as fairer.

## Gate provisions vs. side pockets

Gates and [side pockets](/side-pocket/) serve complementary purposes.

A gate is a **temporary** mechanism. It slows redemptions across the board during acute stress. Once stress subsides, the gate is lifted and redemptions return to normal.

A [side pocket](/side-pocket/) is a **permanent** structural separation. It quarantines illiquid assets that are too difficult to manage in the main portfolio, isolating their valuation risk and redemption effects.

Many sophisticated funds use both. The gate handles periodic stress (e.g., a market downturn or unexpected large redemption). The side pocket handles chronic problems (e.g., a distressed loan that will take years to resolve). During normal times, gates are inactive; side pockets sit quietly in the background until an asset needs to be moved into them.

## The investor perspective on gate fairness

Investors have legitimate concerns about gates. The main worry is asymmetric information: the manager might invoke a gate to protect favored investors or to hide poor performance, rather than to genuinely protect the fund.

Transparent gate policies address this. The prospectus should clearly state:
- The gate threshold (e.g., "25% of NAV per quarter")
- The trigger condition (e.g., "at manager discretion" vs. "automatic if net outflows exceed 30%")
- How redemptions are queued (pro-rata, FIFO, etc.)
- How long a gate remains in effect
- Whether side pockets will be created instead if an asset proves genuinely illiquid

A fund that uses gates rarely, with clear triggers, is more trustworthy than one that uses them opportunistically or keeps them in place indefinitely.

## Historical examples

The 2008 financial crisis exposed the value of gates for some funds and highlighted their fairness problems for others. Hedge funds with serious illiquidity were forced to choose between gate provisions and side pockets. Some invoked gates to slow redemptions, protecting remaining shareholders from forced liquidations. Others created side pockets and moved troubled assets into them.

A few funds attempted to impose gates *retroactively*—claiming emergency powers to restrict redemptions without explicit prior consent. These cases generated litigation and damaged investor trust. Modern prospectuses are more careful to establish gate terms upfront.

## Gate provisions in mutual funds and ETFs

Retail [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) and [ETFs](/etf/) rarely use gate provisions. Regulators generally view gates with skepticism in the retail context, since they suspect it protects managers more than investors. Instead, retail funds must maintain sufficient [liquidity](/liquidity-risk/) to meet redemptions daily or (for ETFs) in a timely manner.

This is a key difference between retail and institutional investing. An institutional [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) investor accepts gates as part of the bargain for access to illiquid, higher-returning strategies. A retail fund investor expects to be able to exit whenever they like.
