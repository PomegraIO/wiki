*The **two-and-twenty** fee structure charges [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) 2% of assets under management annually, plus 20% of net profits (the "carry"). Adopted as the standard in the 1960s, it aligned manager and [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) interests but has become a source of friction as returns have compressed and alternatives have emerged.*

## Origins and rationale

The two-and-twenty structure originated with [Alfred Winslow Jones](/hedge-fund/), a journalist turned [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) pioneer, who established the first modern hedge fund in 1949. Jones charged 20% of profits to align his incentives with [investors](/investment-grade-bond/); a 2% management fee was added later to cover operations and staff. The model spread rapidly through the 1960s and 1970s, becoming the market standard by the 1980s.

The theory was elegant: managers kept a significant slice of profits, creating powerful incentive alignment. A manager earning 20% of gains would work harder to generate returns than one earning a fixed salary. The 2% management fee covered operational costs (compliance, research, technology, office rent) regardless of performance. Combined, the fees promised to align manager and [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) interests in a way that passive [fund](/hedge-fund/) structures could not.

## The mechanics

The **2% management fee** is straightforward: on a $1 billion fund, the manager collects $20 million annually, whether the fund gains or loses money. This covers payroll, trading costs, and overhead. Fees are typically deducted monthly or quarterly, so the [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) sees [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) decline by roughly 0.17% per month.

The **20% performance fee** is more complex. If the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) rises from $1 billion to $1.1 billion (a 10% gain), the manager takes 20% of the $100 million profit: $20 million. An [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) with $10 million in the fund would receive $9 million of the gain; the manager captures $1 million of that $10 million return—27% of the [investor](/investment-grade-bond/)'s gain.

Most [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) employ a "high-water mark" rule: the manager collects [performance fees](/hedge-fund-two-and-twenty/) only once the fund surpasses its previous peak [net asset value](/net-asset-value/). If a fund falls 20% and then recovers 20%, the manager typically does not collect [performance fees](/hedge-fund-two-and-twenty/) on the recovery until the fund surpasses its old high. This prevents managers from taking fees on what amounts to recouping an [investor](/investment-grade-bond/)'s losses.

## The investor's true cost

The fee bite is severe over time. A [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) returning 10% annually in gross terms costs [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) roughly 2.4–2.8% in two-and-twenty fees (2% management plus an estimated 1.6–1.8% [performance fee](/hedge-fund-two-and-twenty/)). Net [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) return: 7–8%. A [passive index fund](/index-fund/) earning 10% in the same period, after 0.1% in fees, delivers 9.9%. Over a decade, the fee differential compounds substantially.

This became a serious problem when [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) returns fell after the 2008 [crisis](/recession/). A [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) earning 6% gross after fees and [costs](/cost-of-equity/) was essentially identical to a [stock index](/sp-500-index/), but [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) paid far more in fees and faced [lock-up periods](/hedge-fund/) and [gates](/hedge-fund-gates/). Pensions and endowments began to question whether the fee structure made sense.

## The high-water mark limitation

The high-water mark is meant to protect [investors](/investment-grade-bond/), but it has loopholes. If a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) manager leaves and is replaced, some [prospectuses](/fund-prospectus/) reset the high-water mark, allowing the new manager to collect [performance fees](/hedge-fund-two-and-twenty/) on a recovery immediately. Similarly, if a fund's "share class" is closed to new [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) and older [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) redeem, new [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) may enter at a lower high-water mark, creating inequity. These gaps have led to litigious disputes between managers and [investors](/investment-grade-bond/).

## Fee evolution and negotiation

As mega-[hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) grew and competition intensified, fee structures began to erode. Large [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) (pensions, endowments, funds of funds) now often negotiate custom fee schedules. A $10 billion [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) might charge 1.5% management + 18% carry, or even 1% + 15%, depending on the [investor](/investment-grade-bond/)'s bargaining power and the fund's track record.

Smaller emerging funds sometimes charge higher fees (2.5% + 25%) to cover startup costs and attract early capital. Established mega-[funds](/hedge-fund/) like [Goldman Sachs](/goldman-sachs/) spin-offs often charge lower rates because capital is abundant and competition for large [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) is intense.

Some [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) have introduced "tiered" fee schedules: the first $500 million might be charged at 2% + 20%, while assets above that are charged 1.5% + 15%. This reflects the principle that managing more capital is cheaper per dollar. A few funds now tie [performance fees](/hedge-fund-two-and-twenty/) to hurdle rates (e.g., only 20% on returns above 5%) or to [indices](/sp-500-index/) (charge on outperformance versus the [S&P 500](/sp-500-index/)).

## Incentive distortions

The [performance fee](/hedge-fund-two-and-twenty/) creates subtle perverse incentives. A manager earning 20% of profits but bearing none of the downside is incentivized toward tail risk—bets that pay a lot in some years and crater in others. If the bet works, the manager collects huge [performance fees](/hedge-fund-two-and-twenty/). If it fails, [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) lose capital and the manager simply moves on. This was implicated in several hedge fund blowups in the 2000s and 2010s.

The 2% management fee also creates pressure to grow assets, since the manager's compensation rises with assets under management regardless of performance. A manager earning $20 million annually on a $1 billion fund has incentive to grow to $2 billion, even if the larger size makes the strategy harder to execute and returns suffer.

## The case for two-and-twenty

Defenders of the model argue that two-and-twenty is not a tax on [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) but a price for alpha. A [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) generating 300 basis points of excess return (after fees) versus a [market index](/stock-market/) is worth the fees. The problem is proving ex ante that alpha exists; most [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) underperform after fees, and few demonstrate consistent outperformance over long periods. The burden of proof now rests on the [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/), not the [investor](/investment-grade-bond/), and many funds have failed that test.
