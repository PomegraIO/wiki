*A **risk parity strategy** allocates capital so that each asset class contributes equally to overall portfolio risk, rather than receiving equal dollar investments. Instead of the traditional 60/40 stocks-bonds split, risk parity asks: given stocks are far more volatile than bonds, how much capital should each class receive to create equal risk footprints?*

## Why equal dollars fail

A standard 60/40 portfolio places $60 in stocks and $40 in bonds. But stocks have historically moved 2–3 times more sharply than bonds. That $60 stock allocation typically drives 90–95% of the portfolio's fluctuations, while bonds anchor just 5–10%. The result: you think you're diversified, but you're mostly taking a single bet on equity risk.

Risk parity inverts this logic. Instead of asking "what dollar weight should each asset get?", it asks "what capital proportion does each asset need to contribute the same amount of risk?" If stocks are twice as volatile as bonds, put half as much money in stocks. The portfolio feels equally exposed to stock moves and bond moves—hence the name.

## The mathematics of volatility weighting

Suppose stocks have an annual [volatility](/historical-volatility/) of 15% and bonds 5%. A naive equal-dollar split means stocks control the risk. To equalize, you'd invest inversely: put 5 units in stocks for every 15 units in bonds. The actual ratio depends on their correlation; if they move together (correlation near 1), the math is straightforward; if they offset (correlation near 0), you need heavier rebalancing.

Most risk parity [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) use more sophisticated weighting. They compute the [volatility](/historical-volatility/) of each asset class, then divide target risk equally. If you want each asset contributing, say, 10% of total portfolio risk, and stocks are 15% volatile, you allocate less to stocks than to lower-volatility assets. The formula scales capital inversely to volatility.

## Leverage is almost always required

Here's the catch: if you weight purely by inverse volatility, the bond portion typically swallows 60–80% of your capital, leaving equities underfunded. A 100% dollar portfolio might split only 20% stocks, 80% bonds. That feels conservative and low-returning.

Risk parity funds typically use [leverage](/leverage-ratio-forex/) to solve this. They might hold 20% stocks and 40% bonds in cash, then borrow 40% more to buy additional bonds, alternatives, or both. The leverage dial sits between 1.5× and 2.5× gross notional. This boosts expected returns without—in theory—increasing risk concentration, because the extra capital still goes to lower-volatility buckets.

## Asset classes beyond stocks and bonds

Pure 60/40 isn't rich enough for true risk parity. Most implementations include three to five buckets: developed-market equities, emerging-market equities, government and corporate bonds, commodities, and sometimes [real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/) or [alternative assets](/hedge-fund/). Each gets assigned a volatility target and a risk budget.

The beauty is rebalancing discipline. As stocks boom and volatility drops, the fund sells stocks and buys bonds to re-equalize risk. As bonds rally, it trims the bond allocation. This forces a countercyclical discipline: buying what's cheap and selling what's expensive in relative-risk terms.

## When the strategy excels—and when it breaks

Risk parity thrived in the 2010s. Markets were range-bound; correlations remained moderate; leverage was cheap and available. A balanced, risky, volatility-weighted portfolio outpaced a stock-heavy traditional fund without wild swings.

But the strategy has two severe vulnerabilities. First, in a crisis when correlations spike toward 1 (everything falls together), the lower-volatility assets no longer offset stock losses. Bonds might rally, but only modestly. The leverage amplifies losses. During March 2020, some risk parity funds suffered sharp drawdowns despite their theoretical balance.

Second, risk parity is crowded. As more capital chased the same bond and commodity allocations, those assets inflated. The leverage required to boost returns relied on persistently low borrowing costs. A regime shift—higher rates, tighter credit—can unwind the strategy quickly. Rebalancing also trades against price trends in choppy markets, locking in losses.

## Risk parity versus [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) [alpha](/alpha/)

Risk parity is primarily a [beta](/beta/) play: it chases diversified market exposure. Unlike [actively-managed hedge funds](/actively-managed-fund/) that hunt for mispriced securities, risk parity relies on mechanical allocation rules and leverage. The manager's edge lies in volatility forecasting, rebalancing timing, and cost discipline—not security selection.

That distinction matters for fees. A [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) charging 2% and 20% performance [fees](/performance-fee/) promises to beat the market. A risk parity strategy, priced at 0.5–1.5% base plus modest carry, frankly admits it's selling diversification and leverage, not genius. The return target is typically bond yields plus a leverage premium, often 5–8% annually before fees.

## Implementation in modern portfolios

Institutional investors—pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurance companies—favour risk parity because it offers predictable drawdown patterns and avoids the concentration of traditional 60/40. A university endowment or a retirement system can justify leverage when diversification is the primary goal.

Retail access is limited. A few mutual funds and [ETFs](/etf/) approximate risk parity by overweighting bonds and alternatives, but they avoid explicit leverage. True risk parity, with derivatives and borrowed capital, remains mostly the province of [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) and institutional separate accounts. The approach works best for long-term, patient capital that can tolerate the occasional shock and trust in the rebalancing mechanism.
