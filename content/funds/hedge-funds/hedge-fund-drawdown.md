*A **maximum drawdown** is the largest percentage decline from a peak [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) to a subsequent trough in a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/)'s history. If a fund rises to $100 million and then falls to $70 million, its maximum drawdown is 30%. Unlike [volatility](/historical-volatility/) measures, drawdown captures the experience of an [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) who buys at the worst time and holds through the loss.*

## Why drawdown matters

A [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) posting 12% annualized returns over ten years sounds attractive—until you learn the fund fell 40% in 2009 and took five years to recover. An [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) who entered at the peak lost 40% of their capital; the long-term 12% average return is cold comfort when your portfolio halved.

Drawdown is the metric that matters to real money. An [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) evaluates a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) not on average return but on the loss they could experience if they buy at the worst moment. A [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) with 8% average returns and a maximum drawdown of 15% is materially different from one with 10% average returns and a 45% maximum drawdown. The latter requires an [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) to stomach near-bankruptcy to capture the extra 2% return.

## Calculation and measurement

Maximum drawdown is calculated by identifying the highest [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) the [fund](/hedge-fund/) ever reached, then finding the lowest [NAV](/net-asset-value/) after that peak. The percentage decline from peak to trough is the drawdown.

For example, a [fund](/hedge-fund/)'s monthly [NAV](/net-asset-value/) progression might be:

- January: $100 million (peak)
- February: $95 million
- March: $85 million (trough)
- April: $87 million
- May: $110 million (new peak)
- June: $105 million
- July: $80 million (trough)

The maximum drawdown is the largest peak-to-trough decline: from $110 million (May peak) to $80 million (July trough), a 27.3% loss. Note that drawdown measures the worst single decline during the period, not cumulative losses.

## Recovery and drawdown duration

A related metric is recovery time: how long it took for the [fund](/hedge-fund/) to return from trough to peak. A 30% drawdown recovering in three months is less painful than a 30% drawdown that takes two years to recover. Recovery duration also reflects the [fund](/hedge-fund/)'s ability to generate alpha after losses—a struggling [fund](/hedge-fund/) may recover slowly or not at all.

Some [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) impose "hurdle rates" or reset [high-water marks](/hedge-fund-two-and-twenty/) to force closure if drawdown becomes too severe. A [fund](/hedge-fund/)'s [prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) might specify that if [NAV](/net-asset-value/) falls below 50% of peak, [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) are allowed to force a redemption or the fund is dissolved. This protection is rare in modern [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) but was more common during the [2008 financial crisis](/recession/).

## Drawdown versus volatility

Drawdown and [volatility](/historical-volatility/) are related but distinct. [Volatility](/historical-volatility/) ([standard deviation](/hedge-fund-two-and-twenty/) of returns) measures the typical month-to-month or year-to-year wiggle in portfolio value. A [fund](/hedge-fund/) might have 10% annual [volatility](/historical-volatility/), meaning returns swing ±10% around the mean. Drawdown is the worst sustained loss, which may be far larger than typical [volatility](/historical-volatility/).

A [fund](/hedge-fund/) holding illiquid or [concentrated positions](/concentration-risk/) can have low measured [volatility](/historical-volatility/) (if [NAV](/net-asset-value/) is estimated rather than marked to market) but very high maximum drawdown when a liquidity crisis forces a revaluation. Conversely, a high-[volatility](/historical-volatility/) [fund](/hedge-fund/) might bounce around but recover quickly, posting a smaller maximum drawdown than its [volatility](/historical-volatility/) would suggest.

## Drawdown by strategy

Different [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) strategies produce different drawdown profiles. A [long-short equity](/hedge-fund/) fund that shorts [overvalued stocks](/stock/) may limit drawdown because short positions cushion portfolio declines. A [distressed debt](/hedge-fund/) fund may experience high drawdown during market [recessions](/recession/) when distressed [securities](/securities-and-exchange-commission/) crater alongside everything else. A [market-neutral](/hedge-fund/) fund targeting zero [correlation](/historical-volatility/) with equity [indices](/sp-500-index/) should have lower drawdowns, though past performance does not guarantee future results.

Data from the 2008 [crisis](/recession/) revealed that many purportedly "market-neutral" [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) suffered drawdowns of 20–30%, not because the strategy was flawed but because [correlations](/historical-volatility/) broke down under stress: previously uncorrelated assets fell together.

## The 2008 example

The 2008 [financial crisis](/recession/) was a test case for drawdown. Many [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) experienced maximum drawdowns of 30–50%, some approaching 60%. A few notables:

- A well-regarded [long-short fund](/hedge-fund/) fell 40% in 2008 and did not fully recover until 2014.
- Funds holding [mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/) or leveraged [real estate](/residential-real-estate/) fell far harder than [equity](/equity-financing/) hedges.
- A few [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) (most famously, one managed by a former [Goldman Sachs](/goldman-sachs/) partner) collapsed entirely, experiencing a drawdown of 100% (total loss).

These blowups made maximum drawdown a central metric for due diligence. [Investors](/investment-grade-bond/) now demand drawdown history, worst-quarter returns, and recovery times before committing capital to a new [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/).

## Using drawdown in due diligence

A prudent [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) reviewing a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) should ask:

- What is the maximum drawdown over the fund's history?
- Has the fund experienced multi-year drawdowns from which it took years to recover?
- What was the worst single year or quarter?
- How does this [fund](/hedge-fund/)'s drawdown compare to competing [funds](/hedge-fund/) or [indices](/sp-500-index/)?
- What is the recovery time from trough to peak?

These answers reveal both risk tolerance and manager skill. A [fund](/hedge-fund/) with a 20% maximum drawdown shows discipline; a [fund](/hedge-fund/) with a 50%+ drawdown suggests either high leverage, concentrated bets, or poor risk controls. A [fund](/hedge-fund/) that experiences a large drawdown but recovers in a year shows resilience; one that takes five years to recover suggests the manager lacks the ability to generate alpha.

## Forward-looking limitations

Maximum drawdown is a backward-looking metric. Historical drawdown informs [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) expectations but does not predict future drawdown. A [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) with a 25% maximum drawdown over its first five years could experience a 50% drawdown in its sixth year if the market environment or strategy shifts. Conversely, a fund that has never experienced a large drawdown might be unusually lucky or managed by a skilled manager; only time reveals which.

The [SEC](/securities-and-exchange-commission/) now requires [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) to disclose maximum drawdown in certain contexts, but standardization remains incomplete. Some funds report drawdown to the nearest percentage point; others claim it to one decimal. Some measure drawdown monthly; others use daily [NAV](/net-asset-value/). These variations can obscure the true risk profile.
