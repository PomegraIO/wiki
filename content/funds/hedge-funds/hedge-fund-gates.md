*A **redemption gate** is a contractual provision allowing a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) to limit the percentage of assets that [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) can withdraw in any single redemption period, typically expressed as a cap (e.g., 80% of requests processed per quarter). Gates were introduced as a liquidity-management tool to prevent panic withdrawals from forcing rapid asset sales.*

## Origins and logic

Redemption gates became standard practice after the 1998 collapse of [Long-Term Capital Management](/hedge-fund/), when the fund froze withdrawals entirely to avoid a forced asset fire-sale. The model then crystallized during the 2008 [financial crisis](/recession/), when numerous funds that held [mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/) and other illiquid positions faced simultaneous withdrawal requests. Without gates, funds would sell their best assets first to raise cash, destroying returns for [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) who stayed.

A gate works by order of filing: [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) submit redemption requests by a cutoff date, and the fund pays out up to the gate percentage on a pro-rata basis. If the gate is 50% and [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) request $100 million in redemptions, everyone receives 50 cents per dollar requested. The remainder goes into a queue for the next redemption period. This preserves the fund's ability to liquidate assets in an orderly fashion rather than a firesale.

## How gates are implemented

Most [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) [prospectuses](/fund-prospectus/) now include boilerplate language permitting redemption gates, either at the manager's discretion or triggered by certain conditions. A fund might specify that gates activate if redemptions exceed, say, 25% of assets in a single quarter. Alternatively, gates may be permanently in place—every redemption period, the fund pays out only up to a preset percentage.

The gate percentage varies by strategy. A fund holding mostly liquid [equities](/equity-financing/) might set a gate at 80% or even 100% (no gate). A fund specializing in distressed [debt](/debt-financing/) or private deals might cap it at 25%, since the underlying assets cannot be sold quickly. The [prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) typically specifies whether gates are mandatory or discretionary; in practice, discretionary gates are invoked only when redemptions threaten the portfolio.

## The investor experience

Being gated is painful. Suppose an [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) holds $1 million in a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) and requests redemption on the March 31 cutoff. The fund imposes a 60% gate for the second quarter. The [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) receives $600,000 on May 1 and is told the remaining $400,000 will be processed in the third quarter, assuming no further gates are imposed. If the fund continues to gate, the [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) might wait a full year or more to fully redeem.

During that wait, the [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) has no say in how the fund is managed; they hold [shares](/common-stock/) in a vehicle they are exiting and cannot trade their redemption queue position to another [investor](/investment-grade-bond/). The [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) also faces tax complications: they may owe [capital gains taxes](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) on the redemption in the year it was requested, even though cash arrives later. Some [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) view gates as a breach of contract; litigation has occasionally resulted, though courts generally uphold gates if properly disclosed.

## The manager's perspective

From the [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) manager's viewpoint, gates are a tool to preserve the fund's integrity. Without them, redemptions could force the sale of assets at distressed prices, crystallizing losses and undermining the strategy. A gate allows the manager to adhere to the fund's investment thesis—holding onto long-dated illiquid positions—without panic-selling when the [market](/stock-market/) spikes downward and [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) flee.

Gates also act as a self-stabilizing mechanism. A [fund](/hedge-fund/) that imposes a 50% gate on high-redemption quarters effectively signals to remaining [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) that the fund is not unravelling. This can reduce subsequent redemption requests, creating a virtuous cycle. Conversely, announcing a gate can panic [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) who were not planning to redeem, triggering more requests—a perverse but real outcome.

## Legal and regulatory constraints

The [Investment Company Act](/investment-company-act-of-1940/) technically prohibits "gates" for registered [funds](/hedge-fund/) without [SEC](/securities-and-exchange-commission/) relief. Most [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) operate as private partnerships and avoid this constraint through proper disclosure in the [prospectus](/fund-prospectus/). However, the [SEC](/securities-and-exchange-commission/) has scrutinized whether gates are excessive or undisclosed. If a fund fails to disclose a gate or invokes it without proper authority, [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) may have grounds for claims.

Some states have stricter rules on redemption restrictions. New York, which oversees many hedge funds, requires that gates be proportional and disclosed in detail. The [CFTC](/securities-and-exchange-commission/) (Commodity Futures Trading Commission) has also issued guidance on gates for funds holding significant [derivatives](/option/). The net effect is that gates are legal, expected, and widespread—but managers must follow procedural rules and disclose them honestly.

## Gates versus side pockets

Redemption gates and [side pockets](/hedge-fund-side-pocket/) serve related but distinct purposes. A gate delays all redemptions proportionally, preserving asset continuity for everyone. A [side pocket](/hedge-fund-side-pocket/) segregates a specific illiquid holding, freezing redemptions only for the portion exposed to that asset. A fund might use both: a gate to slow overall redemptions and a side pocket to isolate a particularly toxic position.

Gates are also often paired with [lock-up periods](/hedge-fund/), which prevent redemption entirely for a set term (e.g., one year). A new [investor](/investment-grade-bond/) might have a one-year lock-up; after that, they can redeem, subject to gates and quarterly cutoff dates.

## Modern evolution

In recent years, some large [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) have relaxed or eliminated gates, using instead "subscription vehicles" or "continuation funds" to manage illiquid assets. These alternatives allow [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) to exit the fund entirely while a subset of [investors](/investment-grade-bond/) rolls capital into a new vehicle holding the illiquid positions. Gates remain standard, however, for traditional [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) structures, particularly those focused on complex strategies where asset liquidity is genuinely constrained.
