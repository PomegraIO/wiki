*The **guideline transaction method** values a private company by identifying recent [acquisitions](/acquisition/) of similar businesses and applying the multiples those buyers paid to the target company's financial metrics. Where market comps are sparse or stale, deal multiples offer a reality check grounded in actual money exchanged between informed parties.*

## When comps come from deals, not public markets

Public company [valuations](/stock/) rest on continuous market pricing—millions of shares trade daily, creating fresh evidence of what investors will pay. Private companies have no such luxury. The guideline transaction method fills that gap by asking: what did acquirers recently pay for comparable businesses, and what multiple of EBITDA, revenue, or other metrics did that represent?

A buyer offering 8× EBITDA for a marketing agency in 2024 provides a data point. If your target company—similar size, margins, growth rate, customer concentration—generates £3 million in EBITDA, an 8× multiple suggests a valuation around £24 million. The method assumes that comparable market conditions and buyer risk appetite apply to your target too.

This approach works particularly well for roll-up or add-on scenarios, where financial sponsors or strategic buyers are actively consolidating fragmented sectors. It is less reliable in niche markets where deal flow is thin, or when the target is materially different in scale, profitability, or strategic fit.

## Finding the right comparables

Identifying truly comparable transactions demands discipline. "Comparable" doesn't mean "in the same industry"—it means similar size, margin profile, growth trajectory, and customer/product concentration.

A £5 million EBITDA SaaS company with 60% gross margins is not comparable to a £5 million EBITDA business-services firm with 40% margins. Buyers will typically pay 8–12× for the SaaS business and 5–7× for services. Mixing them clouds the signal.

Key screening criteria include transaction size (deals significantly larger or smaller than your target may involve different buyer dynamics), acquisition date (multiples shift with interest rates, credit availability, and sector sentiment), and buyer type. A financial sponsor buying a platform for add-on acquisitions may pay higher multiples than a strategic buyer integrating a single target into an existing operation.

Data sources range from public SEC filings (strategic acquisitions by public companies are disclosed) and specialized M&A databases (Refinitiv, FactSet, Pitchbook) to broker-led processes and industry networking. Dealmakers often have better visibility than published databases, so advisers and investment bankers can be valuable guides to recent market pricing.

## From multiples to value

Once you've assembled a set of comparable transactions, you extract the multiples implied by each deal and calculate a range. A typical analysis might reveal that five similar acquisitions closed at 6.5×, 7.2×, 8.1×, 7.8×, and 6.9× EBITDA. The mean sits around 7.3×, with outliers informing a plausible range of 6.5× to 8.1×.

Apply that range to your target's EBITDA or revenue figure to arrive at a valuation band. If your company generates £2.5 million in EBITDA:

- Conservative (6.5×): £16.25 million
- Mid-point (7.3×): £18.25 million
- Optimistic (8.1×): £20.25 million

The width of the band reflects genuine market uncertainty. Narrower ranges suggest tighter, more predictable buyer behavior; wider ranges often signal structural variation in how different buyer types value businesses in the sector.

## Adjusting for differences

Raw comparables rarely match your target perfectly. Systematic adjustments can narrow the gap.

If comparables were all 2–3× larger in revenue, add a discount for scale (smaller companies typically command lower multiples, as they have less financial cushion and operational redundancy). If the target has superior [margins](/ebitda-margin/) relative to comparables, a modest premium may be justified. High [customer concentration](/concentration-risk/) risk deserves a haircut.

Each adjustment should rest on concrete data, not intuition. An adjustment for a missing key customer, for instance, might benchmark against the revenue impact: lose a customer representing 20% of EBITDA, and a 10–15% valuation cut is defensible. Vague adjustments for "management quality" or "growth potential" are red flags—those factors should already be embedded in the comparable set if you've screened well.

## Limits and overlaps

The guideline transaction method assumes that past buyer behavior predicts future pricing. In reality, acquirer appetite, interest rates, and sentiment shift. A multiple of 8× that held in 2022 may compress to 5.5× by 2024 if credit markets tighten.

Also, buyers overpay sometimes. An outlier deal at 12× may reflect a strategic premium (tax synergies, revenue acceleration, competitive threat) that won't persist. Filtering out obvious outliers—or treating them separately—helps avoid inflated anchors.

For complex cap tables or distressed situations, the guideline transaction method often pairs with the [discounted cash flow](/discounted-cash-flow-valuation/) method. Deal-based multiples set a market sanity check; intrinsic valuation methods fill in firm-specific details.
