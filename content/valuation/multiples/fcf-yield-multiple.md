*The **FCF yield multiple** is [free cash flow](/wiki/free-cash-flow/) (FCF) per share divided by the stock price, expressed as a percentage. It measures the cash return an investor receives for each dollar of stock held, independent of [dividends](/wiki/dividend-yield/) or [buybacks](/wiki/share-buyback/). A stock trading at $100 with $5 per share in FCF has a 5% FCF yield—comparable to a bond coupon.*

<aside class="wiki-infobox">

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Formula** | (Free cash flow per share / Stock price) × 100% |
| **Interpretation** | Cash return as % of stock price |
| **Typical range** | 2%–8% for mature companies, 0.5%–2% for high-growth |
| **Calculation** | Trailing 12-month FCF ÷ shares outstanding ÷ stock price |
| **Inverse** | [Price-to-FCF ratio](/wiki/price-to-free-cash-flow-ratio/) (P/FCF) |
| **Comparison** | Dividend yield, earnings yield, [bond yield](/wiki/bond-yield-spread/) |
| **Advantage** | Harder to manipulate than earnings; reflects actual cash |
| **Limitation** | Excludes [capital expenditure](/wiki/capex-budgeting/) needs and [reinvestment](/wiki/reinvestment-risk/) |

</aside>

## Why FCF yield matters: comparing apples to cash

An investor comparing two stocks—one yielding 3% in [dividends](/wiki/dividend-yield/), another yielding 2% in [dividends](/wiki/dividend-yield/) but earning 5% in FCF yield—needs a unified metric. [Dividend yield](/wiki/dividend-yield/) only captures what the company chooses to pay out. FCF yield captures the underlying cash-generation capacity, regardless of payout policy.

A mature company earning high FCF might retain cash, reinvest in [buybacks](/wiki/share-buyback/), or sit on a [balance sheet](/wiki/balance-sheet/) fortress. The cash is still generated; it still belongs to shareholders. FCF yield quantifies this value.

A growth company might have zero [dividend](/wiki/dividend-yield/) and negative [earnings](/wiki/earnings-per-share/) (due to [R&D](/wiki/intangible-assets/) and reinvestment) but positive FCF. Again, FCF yield captures the cash truth that [earnings yields](/wiki/earnings-yield/) or [dividend yields](/wiki/dividend-yield/) miss.

## Calculating FCF per share and FCF yield

Free cash flow is [operating cash flow](/wiki/cash-flow-statement/) minus [capital expenditures](/wiki/capex-budgeting/):

```
FCF = Operating Cash Flow – CapEx
FCF per share = FCF / Shares Outstanding
FCF Yield = (FCF per share / Stock Price) × 100%
```

For example, suppose a company generated $1B in [operating cash flow](/wiki/cash-flow-statement/) and spent $200M on [CapEx](/wiki/capex-budgeting/). FCF is $800M. With 100M shares outstanding and a stock price of $50:

```
FCF per share = $800M / 100M = $8
FCF Yield = ($8 / $50) × 100% = 16%
```

A 16% FCF yield is high—implying the stock is either very cheap relative to cash generation or the company is not reinvesting enough for growth.

## Interpreting FCF yield in context

**For mature, stable companies:** A 4–6% FCF yield is typical. The company generates steady cash, typically pays some via [dividends](/wiki/dividend-yield/) or [buybacks](/wiki/share-buyback/), and reinvests to maintain competitive position. An 8% FCF yield might signal the market has repriced the company downward due to competitive threats.

**For high-growth companies:** A 1–3% FCF yield is normal. Growth companies burn cash (high [CapEx](/wiki/capex-budgeting/), [R&D](/wiki/intangible-assets/)) to expand, so [operating cash flow](/wiki/cash-flow-statement/) is modest relative to the equity value markets assign. Low FCF yield does not mean the company is a bad investment—only that growth expectations are high.

**For cyclical companies:** FCF yield fluctuates with the business cycle. During downturns, FCF may surge (companies cut [CapEx](/wiki/capex-budgeting/)), inflating FCF yield deceptively. During booms, FCF sinks (high [CapEx](/wiki/capex-budgeting/)) as companies build capacity. Using trailing-12-month or normalized FCF is safer than any single quarter.

## FCF yield vs. other valuation metrics

**vs. [Dividend yield](/wiki/dividend-yield/):** FCF yield is the "full" cash generation; [dividend yield](/wiki/dividend-yield/) is only the portion distributed. A company with 5% FCF yield but only 2% [dividend yield](/wiki/dividend-yield/) is retaining 3% in cash—for reinvestment, [buybacks](/wiki/share-buyback/), or fortress [balance sheet](/wiki/balance-sheet/). This is not a red flag; it is a choice about capital allocation.

**vs. [Earnings yield](/wiki/earnings-yield/):** [Earnings](/wiki/earnings-per-share/) can be manipulated via [accrual accounting](/wiki/accrual-accounting/); cash flow is harder to fake. A company with 8% [earnings yield](/wiki/earnings-yield/) but 2% FCF yield is earning on paper but not in cash. Investors should be wary: the earnings may not materialize.

**vs. [Bond yield](/wiki/bond-yield-spread/):** FCF yield and [bond yield](/wiki/bond-yield-spread/) are comparable—both are cash returns to an investor per dollar of capital deployed. A stock with 6% FCF yield is more attractive than a [corporate bond](/wiki/corporate-bond/) yielding 4%, if the company is stable. But [bonds](/wiki/bond/) have seniority in [bankruptcy](/wiki/liquidation/), so lower [risk](/wiki/market-risk/) warrants a lower yield.

## The capital expenditure trap

FCF yield has a critical limitation: it subtracts [CapEx](/wiki/capex-budgeting/), but does not account for how much [CapEx](/wiki/capex-budgeting/) is truly necessary. A company spending 5% of [revenue](/wiki/revenue-recognition/) on [CapEx](/wiki/capex-budgeting/) to maintain assets might have high FCF yield. But a competitor spending 15% on [CapEx](/wiki/capex-budgeting/) to build market share might have lower FCF yield—yet better long-term prospects.

This is why FCF yield must be analyzed alongside [reinvestment rate](/wiki/reinvestment-risk/) and [growth](/wiki/growth-trajectory-trading/) expectations. A high FCF yield can indicate:

1. The company is undervalued (good opportunity).
2. The company is mature and not investing for growth (fair, if [dividends](/wiki/dividend-yield/) are stable).
3. The company is harvesting [asset](/wiki/asset-backed-security/) base without reinvesting (warning sign—eventually leads to competitive decline).

## FCF yield in screening and valuation

Value investors often screen for stocks with FCF yields above 5–7%, combined with stable or growing FCF. This identifies companies that generate real cash and trade at reasonable [multiples](/wiki/multiples-valuation/). The logic: a 6% FCF yield is like a bond coupon, but with upside from growth and [buybacks](/wiki/share-buyback/).

In [discounted cash flow](/wiki/discounted-cash-flow-valuation/) (DCF) valuations, FCF yield informs the terminal [growth rate](/wiki/growth-option/). A stock with a 5% FCF yield implies the market expects modest [growth](/wiki/growth-trajectory-trading/) beyond FCF. If the company can sustain higher [growth](/wiki/growth-option/) (via [reinvestment](/wiki/reinvestment-risk/)), the stock is undervalued.

## Earnings quality check

FCF yield often reveals earnings quality issues. A company reports $2 per share in [earnings](/wiki/earnings-per-share/), but FCF is only $0.50 per share. This gap suggests:

- Heavy use of non-cash [revenue](/wiki/revenue-recognition/) or [accrual accounting](/wiki/accrual-accounting/) tricks.
- Large [capital expenditures](/wiki/capex-budgeting/) (not yet fully [expensed](/wiki/depreciation/)).
- [Working capital](/wiki/net-working-capital-ratio/) deterioration (receivables growing faster than [revenue](/wiki/revenue-recognition/)).

The 75% gap between reported [earnings](/wiki/earnings-per-share/) and FCF is a red flag. [Cash](/wiki/cash-flow-statement/) is reality; [earnings](/wiki/earnings-per-share/) are assumptions.

## Cyclical businesses and normalized FCF

Cyclical businesses (airlines, oil majors, mines) have FCF yields that swing wildly with the cycle. In downturns, [CapEx](/wiki/capex-budgeting/) is slashed, FCF surges, and FCF yield looks deceptively attractive. In booms, [CapEx](/wiki/capex-budgeting/) is maxed out, FCF falls, and FCF yield appears weak.

Analysts use "normalized" or average FCF yields over a full cycle (3–5 years) to smooth these swings. A cyclical company with a 10-year average FCF yield of 3–4% is better evaluated on that than on a single year's 8% yield (probably a downturn trough).

## Conclusion: a window into true cash economics

FCF yield is one of the most informative valuation metrics because it is tied to cash generation and hard to manipulate. A high FCF yield signals either a bargain (market mispriced the company) or a value trap (cash generation is unsustainable). Combined with analysis of [reinvestment needs](/wiki/capex-budgeting/), [competitive position](/wiki/deep-moat-investing/), and [balance sheet](/wiki/balance-sheet/) strength, FCF yield is a powerful tool for identifying undervalued stocks with real cash generation.
