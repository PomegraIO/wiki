*The **Price-to-Book Multiple** (P/B) is the stock market price per share divided by the [book value](/wiki/book-value-investing/) (net asset value) per share. A P/B of 2.0 means the market will pay $2 for every $1 of net assets on the company's [balance sheet](/wiki/balance-sheet/). It is one of the oldest equity valuation metrics and remains in use because it captures whether a business trades as a premium or discount to its accounting equity—a snapshot especially useful for asset-heavy industries and cyclical companies where earnings volatility obscures true value.*

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Formula** | Price per share ÷ Book value per share |
| **Book value per share** | [Shareholders' equity](/wiki/equity-reit/) ÷ Shares outstanding |
| **Common abbreviation** | P/B ratio; also "Price-to-Tangible Book" |
| **Typical range** | 0.5–4.0 for mature industries; 5.0+ for tech/growth stocks |
| **Inverse metric** | [Price-to-Tangible-Book](/wiki/tangible-book-value-per-share/) (excludes intangibles) |
| **Strengths** | Comparable across industries; less volatile than [P/E](/wiki/price-to-earnings-ratio/); less dependent on profit recognition |
| **Weakness** | Ignores intangible value; reflects book value bias |

</aside>

## How P/B works and why accountants' balance sheets matter

Book value is the equity residual on a [balance sheet](/wiki/balance-sheet/): [total assets](/wiki/asset-turnover-ratio/) minus [liabilities](/wiki/debt-to-equity-ratio/). For a bank, it closely approximates liquidation value. For a manufacturer, it includes land, plant, and equipment at [historical cost](/wiki/historical-cost/) minus [depreciation](/wiki/depreciation/). For a retailer, it includes inventory. For a software company, book value often understates true worth because it excludes [intangible assets](/wiki/intangible-assets/)—the value of the code, brand, and customer relationships rarely captured on the [income statement](/wiki/income-statement/).

A P/B of 1.0 means the market is willing to pay exactly what the company's net assets are worth on paper. A P/B above 1.0 signals the market believes management can deploy those assets to earn returns *above* their [cost of capital](/wiki/cost-of-equity/). A P/B below 1.0 suggests either deep pessimism about future [return on equity](/wiki/return-on-equity/) or a genuine liquidation scenario. During the 2008 financial crisis, many bank stocks traded at 0.5 or lower, as depositors and [credit markets](/wiki/credit-spread/) doubted the true value of [asset-backed securities](/wiki/asset-backed-security/) on bank balance sheets.

## Price-to-Book vs. Price-to-Earnings: when to use each

The [P/E ratio](/wiki/price-to-earnings-ratio/) divides price by *earnings*, making it sensitive to [depreciation](/wiki/depreciation/), [amortization](/wiki/amortization/), and [accounting judgments](/wiki/revenue-recognition-policy/) about when to recognize revenue. P/B divides by *equity*, a flow-independent snapshot of assets minus debts. In industries with:
- **Capital-intensive operations** (banks, utilities, REITs): P/B is often more stable because a large asset base is explicit.
- **Cyclical earnings** (automakers, steel mills): P/B smooths volatility better than P/E because book values don't swoon on temporary margin pressure.
- **Asset sales and write-downs** (insurance companies, distressed funds): P/B adjusts immediately when assets are revalued or sold.

Conversely, P/B falters for intangible-heavy industries (software, pharma, consumer brands) where the [goodwill](/wiki/goodwill/) and [intangible assets](/wiki/intangible-assets/) acquired in past [acquisitions](/wiki/acquisition/) dominate the balance sheet, inflating book value without generating commensurate earnings. In such cases, a seemingly high P/B (5.0+) may still be justified.

## The value trap: low P/B doesn't mean cheap

A stock trading at a P/B of 0.7 looks attractive until you realize the company has been earning negative [returns on equity](/wiki/return-on-equity/) for three years. The low P/B is not a bargain signal; it is the market correctly pricing in that further [capital reduction](/wiki/capital-reduction/) and asset liquidation are coming. Conversely, a company with a P/B of 3.0 may be justifiably priced if it earns a 20% [return on equity](/wiki/return-on-equity/) and reinvests 50% of earnings at that same high rate—a [growth](/wiki/growth-investing/) story.

The relationship between P/B and future returns is weakly positive: low P/B screens tend to outperform on average, but the scatter is wide. [Value traps](/wiki/value-trap-avoidance-fund/) (companies with persistently low P/B and poor fundamentals) have destroyed as much wealth as growth [bubbles](/wiki/bubbles-and-manias/).

## Industry variations and comparability

Financial institutions show wildly different P/B multiples. A profitable, well-capitalized bank may trade at 0.8–1.2 times book because its earnings are predictable and [capital ratios](/wiki/capital-adequacy/) are regulated. A distressed bank in a [credit crisis](/wiki/credit-loss-model/) may trade at 0.3 times book because [loan losses](/wiki/expected-loss-model/) are eroding equity. Insurance companies typically trade at lower P/B (0.7–1.0) because their book value is inflated by [deferred tax assets](/wiki/deferred-tax-asset/) and [intangible premiums](/wiki/acquisition/) from past acquisitions.

REITs, which distribute 90% of [free cash flow](/wiki/free-cash-flow/), often trade at P/B ratios of 1.0–1.5; industrial manufacturers trade at 1.5–3.0. Technology and consumer-discretionary firms regularly trade at 5.0+ because investors expect [return on equity](/wiki/return-on-equity/) far above the [cost of capital](/wiki/weighted-average-cost-of-capital/).

## Computing Price-to-Book: the accounting caveat

Book value is pulled directly from the [balance sheet](/wiki/balance-sheet/), but which balance sheet? The most recent [10-K](/wiki/10-k/) quarter-end, or the latest [10-Q](/wiki/10-k/) mid-quarter filing? Should you use shares *outstanding* or [fully diluted shares](/wiki/fully-diluted-shares/) (to account for [employee stock options](/wiki/employee-stock-options/) and [convertible bonds](/wiki/convertible-bond/))? Most market data providers use trailing quarter-end book value and basic shares outstanding, which is a reasonable standard. But a more conservative analyst might compute P/B using fully diluted shares to ensure the metric is not overstating profitability on a per-share basis.

## P/B and market cycles

During asset bubbles—tech 2000, real estate 2008, crypto 2021—the highest-flying stocks often sport P/B ratios of 10+, as investors price in improbable future earnings growth or sustained capital appreciation unmoored from fundamentals. At market bottoms and in secular bear markets, average P/B ratios compress sharply. The long-term median P/B for the S&P 500 hovers around 1.5; when it rises to 2.5+, market valuations are historically elevated; when it falls below 1.0, genuine bargains and distressed sellers coexist.
