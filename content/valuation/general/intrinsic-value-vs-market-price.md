## What Intrinsic Value Represents

**Intrinsic value** is the theoretically correct price of a stock based on its ability to generate future cash or earnings. A company worth $1 billion in intrinsic value, divided by 100 million shares, implies an intrinsic value per share of $10.

The most rigorous approach is [discounted cash flow (DCF)](/discounted-cash-flow-valuation/) valuation: forecast the company's free cash flows for the next 5–10 years, calculate a terminal value (the company's value beyond the forecast period), and discount both back to present value using a cost of capital (usually the weighted average cost of capital, or WACC). The sum is intrinsic value.

Alternative approaches include earnings-based methods ([price-to-earnings multiples](/price-to-earnings-ratio/), earnings power valuation), [asset-based valuations](/balance-sheet/), and comparables (what similar companies sell for). Each method yields a different estimate, and serious investors use multiple approaches to triangulate.

Intrinsic value is not an objective fact; it is an estimate, and different analysts will reach different estimates based on their assumptions about growth rates, margins, discount rates, and terminal values. A stock's intrinsic value to one analyst might be $50; to another, $75. This disagreement is the source of price discovery and market dynamism.

## Market Price and Market Efficiency

The **market price** is the price at which the stock last traded, or the current bid and ask prices, set by the collective decisions of buyers and sellers in the open market.

According to the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH), the market price reflects all available information and is the "correct" price at any moment. From this view, intrinsic value and market price should converge. The existence of a gap suggests either:
1. Your intrinsic value estimate is wrong.
2. The market hasn't yet incorporated information you have.
3. The market is behaving irrationally in the short term but will correct over time.

Most professional investors reject pure EMH but accept that markets are "mostly efficient"—information is incorporated quickly, but inefficiencies (pockets of mispricing) exist and can be exploited by skilled analysts with a long time horizon.

## Interpreting a Divergence: Undervaluation and Overvaluation

When a stock's market price is below your calculated intrinsic value, the stock is **undervalued**. The gap represents potential upside. If you estimate a stock is worth $100 per share but it trades at $60, the gap is 40%, or 40% of upside.

The magnitude of the gap matters. A 10% gap might reflect normal estimation risk and market noise. A 40% gap suggests either a significant analytical opportunity or a serious error in your valuation. Value investors typically require a margin of safety—a minimum discount to intrinsic value—before buying. If you estimate intrinsic value at $100 but require a 30% margin of safety, you'll buy only if the price falls to $70 or below.

Conversely, when market price is above intrinsic value, the stock is **overvalued**. The gap represents downside risk. If you value a stock at $50 but it trades at $80, the stock is 60% overvalued. Value investors avoid such stocks or short them, depending on conviction and risk appetite.

## Why Gaps Persist: Market Psychology and Information Asymmetry

Intrinsic value divergences don't instantly close. Several factors explain persistence:

**Information asymmetry**: Management, insiders, and sophisticated investors may have better information than the broad market. A stock might be undervalued because the market hasn't yet understood a company's competitive moat or a pending catalyst.

**Sentiment and behavioral biases**: Markets overshoot. During hype cycles (e.g., AI booms, crypto manias), investors extrapolate growth rates to absurd levels, and stocks become wildly overvalued. During downturns, fear causes panic selling, and even sound companies trade below intrinsic value.

**Liquidity constraints**: Illiquid stocks can remain deeply mispriced for extended periods. If a stock is hard to trade (thin order book, few shareholders), information slowly propagates, and arbitrage is difficult. A large investor spotting the mispricing might lack a way to exploit it without moving the price dramatically.

**Time horizons**: The market is short-term oriented. The stock market's dominant participants are day traders, momentum funds, and short-term algorithms. A company with strong long-term fundamentals but near-term earnings headwinds may trade below intrinsic value for years until the headwinds clear.

**Model risk**: Investors using different valuation models (DCF vs. multiples vs. comparables) will disagree on intrinsic value. This disagreement is healthy—it reflects legitimate uncertainty—but it also means that no single "correct" price exists. The market price represents the weighted consensus of all these models.

## The Role of Catalysts and Mean Reversion

A gap between intrinsic value and market price is often interpreted as a temporary mispricing that will close when a **catalyst** arrives. A catalyst might be:
- Earnings beat that validates the fundamental view
- Management change or strategic shift
- Macro shift (interest rates fall, commodity prices rise, a recession ends)
- Analyst upgrade or institutional investor accumulation
- A restructuring, spinoff, or acquisition that unlocks hidden value

Investors buy undervalued stocks betting on catalysts. However, catalysts can take years to arrive, and the stock may drift further away from intrinsic value before snapping back. This is called "catching a falling knife": you value a stock at $100, buy at $60, but it falls to $40 before recovering. The correct valuation doesn't insulate you from near-term price risk.

## Risks of the Intrinsic Value Approach

Relying on intrinsic value as an investment guide carries genuine risks:

**Model risk**: Your DCF model or valuation method is wrong. You forecast 5% perpetual growth, but the company faces disruption and growth turns negative. Your $100 intrinsic value estimate was nonsense.

**Execution risk**: A company might have good fundamentals but poor management, or strong products but eroding competitive position. Intrinsic value assumes stable competitive dynamics; real businesses face disruption.

**Market timing**: You correctly identify an undervalued stock, but the market doesn't care for three years. You earn nothing, while investors in expensive stocks riding momentum earn 50%. Patience is a virtue, but it's also a cost.

**Valuation multiples can expand or contract**: A stock might trade at 15x earnings (a reasonable multiple) but fall to 10x earnings due to rising [interest rates](/interest-rate/) or a shift in investor preferences, even though earnings are stable. The multiple contraction is a real loss, independent of business fundamentals.

## Reconciling Intrinsic Value with Market Price

A mature investor uses intrinsic value as a guide, not a dogma. The process is iterative:
1. Calculate intrinsic value using multiple methods (DCF, multiples, comparables). Recognize the range of uncertainty.
2. Compare to market price. Identify the gap.
3. Ask: Is the gap due to mispricing, or is my estimate wrong? What could I be missing?
4. If convinced of a gap, decide on an appropriate margin of safety.
5. Buy or short only if the risk/reward is favorable and the position size is appropriate to conviction.
6. Monitor: As new information arrives, revisit intrinsic value. If it falls below your purchase price, reassess.

This approach avoids both the naive view that markets are always right (they aren't) and the overconfident view that your valuation model is gospel (it isn't). It respects both fundamental analysis and market humility.
