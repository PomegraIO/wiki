*When a company carries heavy debt, its equity shrinks to a small slice of enterprise value—called **stub equity**—where small changes in the underlying business value can swing the stock by multiples. Valuing this residual requires understanding the debt structure and why traditional equity models break down.*

## Why stub equity matters

When a company finances itself heavily with debt, the equity holders sit at the bottom of the capital structure. A $100 million enterprise—financed with $90 million of debt and $10 million of equity—has equity that represents only 10% of operating value. If enterprise value drops 15%, the equity takes a much larger hit in percentage terms. Conversely, if the business outperforms by the same amount, equity upside can be spectacular. This magnification is why stub equity demands careful valuation discipline: small errors in enterprise assumptions drive disproportionate errors in what shareholders will actually own.

Stub equity typically emerges in three settings: [leveraged buyouts](/leveraged-buyout/) where sponsors load the company with acquisition debt; [reorganizations](/debt-restructuring/) where creditors take equity in exchange for debt forgiveness; and distressed situations where [going concerns](/going-concern/) maintain a thin equity cushion.

## Calculating stub equity value

The standard approach is mechanical: begin with **enterprise value** and work downward.

Enterprise Value (estimated)  
Less: Total Debt Outstanding  
Less: Preferred Equity (if any)  
Less: Other Senior Claims (preferred stock, minority interests, pension obligations)  
Equals: Stub Equity Value  

Suppose a company trades at an [enterprise value](/enterprise-value/) of $500 million. Its debt stack:

- Senior bank loans: $300 million
- Subordinated bonds: $100 million
- Preferred stock: $30 million

Stub equity = $500 − $300 − $100 − $30 = **$70 million**.

If the company has 7 million shares outstanding, the implied stub equity value per share is $10. This is the equity valuation *before* any upside or downside risk from misestimation of the enterprise value itself.

## The leverage amplifier

This is where stub equity becomes treacherous. Enterprise value estimates are never certain. Consider the same company with 10 million shares:

| Scenario | Enterprise Value | Less: Debt & Preferred | Stub Equity | Per Share |
|----------|------------------|------------------------|-------------|-----------|
| Downside | $400M | $430M | Negative | N/A |
| Base case | $500M | $430M | $70M | $7.00 |
| Upside | $600M | $430M | $170M | $17.00 |

A 20% swing in enterprise value (±$100 million) moves stub equity from negative (wiping out shareholders) to nearly 2.5× the base case. This is the leverage amplifier at work.

The ratio that governs sensitivity is:

**% Change in Equity Value = % Change in Enterprise Value × (Enterprise Value ÷ Stub Equity Value)**

In our base case, this multiplier is $500 ÷ $70 = **7.1×**. A 10% improvement in the business lifts equity value by 71%. A 10% deterioration erases it entirely.

## Valuation methods under leverage

Conventional [discounted cash flow](/discounted-cash-flow-valuation/) and [multiples-based approaches](/relative-valuation/) still apply to enterprise value. The difference is the interpretation:

**Free cash flow to enterprise.** Project [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/), discount at the [weighted average cost of capital](/cost-of-equity/) (which reflects the company's debt load), and arrive at enterprise value. Then subtract the capital structure and solve for equity.

**Comparable multiples.** If peers trade at 8× [EBITDA](/ebitda/), apply that to your company's estimated EBITDA. Again, this yields enterprise value; the leverage structure determines equity value.

**Option-theoretic view.** Equity in a leveraged company is economically like a [call option](/call-option/) on enterprise value, struck at the debt amount. As the business improves, equity gains leverage. This lens is especially useful in distressed valuations where the equity sits very deep out of the money—or has near-zero value.

## Sensitivity and stress testing

Because stub equity is so sensitive to enterprise assumptions, [stress testing](/stress-testing/) is mandatory. Map a range of scenarios:

- **Revenue base case, upside, downside** (±15% to 25%)
- **Operating margin variance** (reflecting competitive or cost pressures)
- **[Discount rate](/discount-rate/) sensitivity** (a 1–2 percentage point move in WACC can swing equity by 30–50%)
- **Debt refinancing risk.** If interest rates rise or credit spreads widen, the cost to refinance debt increases, reducing available cash to equity.

A sensitivity table or tornado chart makes these dependencies visible and forces you to identify which variables drive equity value most.

## When equity is worthless (or negative)

If debt and preferred claims exceed enterprise value, stub equity is underwater. In [bankruptcy](/going-concern/), shareholders own nothing; creditors become the new equity holders. In a going concern—a company that has not yet filed—the market may still price equity on recovery hope (option value), but fundamental value is zero.

This scenario is common in [leveraged buyouts](/leveraged-buyout/) that assume high exit multiples or rapid [deleveraging](/debt-restructuring/). If the business stumbles, equity holders see their stake evaporate long before the company misses a debt payment.

## Comparing leverage and equity returns

Leverage amplifies both returns and losses. Two otherwise identical companies—one financed with 30% debt, the other with 70% debt—will show very different [returns on equity](/return-on-equity/) (ROE) for the same operating profit. The heavily leveraged firm will post higher ROE in good years and larger losses in downturns.

Stub equity valuation forces you to think explicitly about this trade-off: the promised return on equity must compensate for the amplified downside risk.
