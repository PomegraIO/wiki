*Bulge bracket banks—the largest global investment banks like [Goldman Sachs](/goldman-sachs/), [JPMorgan Chase](/jpmorgan-chase/), and [Morgan Stanley](/morgan-stanley/)—generate revenue from four main pillars: [trading](/market-maker-trading/), [underwriting](/securities-and-exchange-commission/), advisory (mergers and acquisitions), and [asset management](/actively-managed-fund/). Each division operates differently, carries different risks, and drives different profit margins.*

## Trading: Market-Making and Proprietary Positioning

Trading is often the largest single revenue line, especially in years when markets are volatile or risk appetite is high. Bulge bracket banks earn money in two ways: as [market makers](/market-maker-trading/) and through [proprietary positioning](/hedge-fund/).

As a market maker, the bank stands in the middle of buy and sell orders. They capture the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/)—the difference between the price they pay (bid) and the price they charge (ask). On millions of transactions per day across equities, bonds, currencies, and [derivatives](/derivatives-hedging/), those fractions of a cent aggregate to tens of millions in daily revenue. [Brokers](/broker/) and hedge funds depend on this liquidity, and bulge brackets compete ferociously on execution speed and price.

Proprietary trading is how banks bet their own capital. A bank might hold a long position in [crude oil](/crude-oil/) or short a basket of tech [stocks](/stock/) based on the firm's macro outlook. If the bet wins, the bank pockets the profit. If it loses—and they often do—the bank absorbs the loss. This is riskier and more volatile than market-making, but the upside is uncapped. During rallies, prop trading can be wildly profitable. During crashes, it can wipe out a quarter's earnings.

Trading also includes [fixed-income](/bond/) (bonds, mortgages, [credit](/credit-default-swap/)), currencies, and commodities. A bank might trade corporate [bonds](/corporate-bond/) it recently underwritten, or [mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/) it assembled. The complexity here is high, the margins tight, and the risk concentration real.

The challenge for banks is that pure spread revenue shrinks in calm, low-volatility markets. Traders fight for market share, and commissions compress. That's why banks also pursue proprietary bets—to goose returns when market-making alone isn't enough.

## Underwriting: IPOs, Bonds, and Syndication

Underwriting is the process of buying securities from the issuer and reselling them to public investors. Bulge brackets earn fees from this act of intermediation.

When a company wants to raise equity via an [initial public offering](/initial-public-offering/) (IPO), it hires an underwriter (or syndicate of underwriters). The bank buys all the shares at a fixed price, then sells them to institutional and retail buyers at a higher price. The spread is the underwriting profit. A typical equity underwriting fee is 3–7% of the raise. On a $1 billion IPO, that's $30–70 million split among the lead and co-managing banks. The lead bank (the prestige player) takes the largest cut.

Bulge brackets also underwrite [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/). A company issues debt to refinance existing loans or fund growth. The bank buys the bond issuance and distributes it to [pension funds](/401k-plan/), [insurance](/auto-insurance/) companies, and other [institutional investors](/investment-company-act-of-1940/). Bond underwriting fees are typically 1–2% of the issue size, lower than equity but still material on large deals.

[Mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/) underwriting is another major line. Banks buy mortgages from originators, bundle them into [securities](/securitization/), and sell them to [real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/) (REITs), pension funds, and foreign investors. The profit comes from origination fees, servicing rights, and ancillary [derivatives](/derivatives-hedging/) (interest-rate [swaps](/swap/)).

Underwriting is recurring revenue but lumpy. It depends on [capital markets](/capital-flows/) activity. During booms, companies raise capital furiously, and underwriting fees surge. During recessions, IPO windows close and [debt](/debt-financing/) issuance plummets. A bank that earned $5 billion from underwriting in 2021 (peak SPAC and low-rate mania) might earn $2 billion in a quiet market. This volatility is why banks diversify across advisory and asset management.

## Advisory: Mergers, Acquisitions, and Restructuring

Advisory is the most profitable business on a per-dollar-of-balance-sheet basis. A bank earns a fee (typically 0.5–1.5% of deal value, paid by the buyer, seller, or both) for advising on [mergers](/merger/) and [acquisitions](/acquisition/).

When Apple acquires a software company for $10 billion, it pays its advisor (maybe [Goldman Sachs](/goldman-sachs/)) $50–150 million to structure the deal, negotiate terms, run an auction, and handle [due diligence](/due-diligence/). The advisor doesn't buy or sell anything; it provides expertise, credibility, and access to the other side.

Large [leveraged buyouts](/leveraged-buyout/) (LBOs) also generate huge advisory fees. A [private equity](/private-equity-fund/) firm acquiring a company for $50 billion might pay its financial advisor $100–200 million. The bank also makes money on the debt side, arranging [credit facilities](/debt-financing/) and underwriting the debt portion of the deal.

Restructuring and bankruptcy work is another advisory revenue stream. When a company needs to [refinance](/refinancing-risk/) distressed debt or reorganize its liabilities, banks advise on alternatives. These fees can be substantial—tens of millions on a complex restructuring.

Advisory is relatively stable (at least compared to trading) because it's driven by longer-term business strategy and [market cycles](/market-cycle/), not daily volatility. It also requires deep relationships with corporate CFOs and private equity sponsors—something bulge brackets cultivate across decades.

## Asset Management: Fees on Trillions

Most bulge brackets operate large [asset management](/actively-managed-fund/) divisions, managing client money in [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/), [ETFs](/etf/), [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/), and separate accounts. The revenue is a percentage of [assets under management](/net-asset-value/) (AUM).

A typical [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) on an actively managed [equity fund](/equity-etf/) is 0.5–1.5% annually. On a $100 billion portfolio, that's $500 million to $1.5 billion per year in revenue—with high operating margins because the incremental cost of managing another dollar is near zero.

Bulge bracket asset managers also earn performance fees on hedge funds and private equity funds. These are typically "2 and 20"—2% of AUM annually, plus 20% of profits above a threshold (the "[carried interest](/carried-interest-compensation/)"). The math is generous: a $10 billion hedge fund earning 15% annually ($1.5 billion profit) generates $200 million in performance fees plus $200 million in base fees. That's $400 million of leverage on a few hundred portfolio managers.

Asset management is the steadiest revenue pillar because it's recurring, less cyclical, and not directly exposed to [market timing](/market-timing/). Even in down markets, fees roll in.

## Prime Brokerage and Ancillary Revenue

Prime brokerage is how bulge brackets finance [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) and [leveraged](/leverage-ratio-forex/) traders. The bank lends money (leverage), provides settlement and custody, and offers analytics. The fees are charges for borrowing, the [spread](/bid-ask-spread/) on securities lending, and account management fees. It's profitable but highly cyclical—it crashes when hedge funds delever or blow up.

Smaller revenue streams include [treasury](/treasury-bill/) services, [foreign exchange](/currency-risk/), [commodities](/crude-oil/) trading, and clearing (acting as an intermediary between buyers and sellers).

## Profitability and Cyclicality

Bulge bracket banks are enormously profitable in boom years—[JPMorgan Chase](/jpmorgan-chase/) and [Goldman Sachs](/goldman-sachs/) routinely post net margins above 20%. But they're cyclical. When [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) widen, volatility soars, and [M&A](/merger/) volumes collapse, revenues can halve in a single year. Staff costs remain sticky (you can't lay off all bankers overnight), so profitability swings wildly.

The largest earnings volatility comes from trading (especially [proprietary trading](/hedge-fund/) and [derivatives](/derivatives-hedging/)). Asset management and advisory are steadier. That's why bulge brackets have diversified; a bank reliant on trading alone would face revenue cliffs. Banks that balance all four divisions weather cycles better.
