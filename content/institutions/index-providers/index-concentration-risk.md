*An **index concentration risk** arises when a cap-weighted index becomes dominated by a small number of very large constituents, so that movements in a handful of stocks drive the entire index's return. A diversification tool in name becomes a bet on five mega-cap companies—the opposite of risk reduction. This risk has grown acute as a few technology giants have ballooned to represent 25–35% of major market indexes.*

## Why cap-weighting creates concentration

Most major market indexes use [market-capitalization](/market-capitalization/) weighting: each security is weighted by its market cap as a share of total market cap. This means the largest companies automatically have the largest index weights.

There is nothing wrong with this methodology—it reflects market reality and reduces [rebalancing](/asset-allocation/) costs. But it creates a structural vulnerability: as markets concentrate into fewer hands, so do the indexes that measure them. If five tech giants grow to represent a third of the market, they represent a third of any cap-weighted index tracking that market.

For an investor using an [index fund](/index-fund/) as a [diversification](/diversification/) tool, this is a problem. The fund is advertised as broad market exposure but delivers outsized bets on Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Alphabet, and Amazon—the "Magnificent Seven" in recent years. A single catastrophic event at one of these companies ripples through the entire index.

## Measuring concentration: the Herfindahl index

Financial analysts use the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) to quantify concentration. The HHI is the sum of squared weights; an equal-weighted index of 500 stocks has HHI = 1/500 = 0.002; a single-stock index has HHI = 1.0. The S&P 500's HHI has climbed from around 0.01 in the 1990s to 0.035–0.04 in recent years, reflecting rising mega-cap dominance.

Higher HHI means fewer stocks drive returns. Investors can compare HHI across indexes and over time to gauge how much concentration risk they are accepting.

## Real-world examples of concentration surges

In 2023–2024, the so-called "Magnificent Seven"—Microsoft, Apple, Nvidia, Google, Amazon, Tesla, and Meta—collectively drove nearly all S&P 500 gains. These seven stocks represented roughly 30% of the index by weight. An investor holding a plain vanilla S&P 500 [ETF](/etf/) was effectively betting on artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and digital advertising concentration.

This concentration lasted several years, creating a regime where the index and a handful of mega-cap tech stocks were nearly perfect correlated. Traditional [portfolio](/asset-allocation/) diversification—holding bonds, mid-caps, and commodities—often failed to hedge S&P 500 exposure because the index itself *was* the mega-cap tech bet.

## Concentration risk differs from [systemic risk](/systemic-risk/)

Concentration risk is micro: a single index is over-weighted to a few names. [Systemic risk](/systemic-risk/) is macro: an entire financial system is fragile because too many market participants hold the same bets. However, concentration in major market indexes can *create* systemic risk if a shock to the concentrated holdings forces massive liquidations across millions of funds holding the same index.

## How concentration shifts over time

Concentration is not constant. It tends to be highest during bull markets when winners grow fastest, and it can diminish if leadership rotates to new sectors. In the 1990s, the S&P 500 became dangerously concentrated in telecommunications and technology before the 2000 bubble burst. The concentration eased in the 2000s with broadening participation across healthcare, finance, and industrials. By 2007, concentration had dropped. Then 2008 hit, and a few large banks that everyone had relied on to *not* fail created the crisis.

## Solutions: weighting alternatives

Investors uncomfortable with cap-weighting concentration can choose alternatives:

- **Equal weighting**: Every stock gets the same weight, regardless of market cap. This requires constant rebalancing and incurs higher costs but eliminates concentration bias. Results are better when leadership is diversified.
- **Fundamental weighting**: Stocks are weighted by accounting metrics—earnings, cash flow, book value—rather than market price. This tends to tilt toward less expensive (often smaller) names and can reduce mega-cap concentration.
- **Diversification weighting**: Rules-based approaches that explicitly cap the weight any single constituent can have.

Each alternative carries its own costs and biases. Equal weighting [rebalances](/asset-allocation/) more aggressively, selling winners (higher costs, lower tax efficiency). Fundamental weighting tilts toward value and smaller caps, delivering different return profiles from the broad market.

## The dilemma for passive investors

A [passive investor](/index-fund/) accepting cap-weighting agrees to hold the market as-is. If the market concentrates, the index concentrates. Trying to reduce concentration through alternative weighting schemes introduces [tracking error](/alpha/) and style drift—the fund no longer holds the market but a modified version of it.

The alternative is accepting concentration risk as the cost of broad-market passive exposure. Or accepting that true market-matching diversification declines when the market itself concentrates, and using factors like value, momentum, or quality to hedge.
