*The **Moscow Exchange** (MOEX) is Russia's primary bourse and one of the world's largest [FX trading](/currency-risk/) venues. Unlike most exchanges, MOEX is vertically integrated—it operates not only equities and derivatives markets but also a clearinghouse and significant money-market trading, consolidating nearly all Russian financial market infrastructure under one operator.*

## Vertical integration and market scope

MOEX's defining structural feature is its absorption of exchange, clearing, and settlement functions. Most major exchanges outsource clearing to independent operators: the [NYSE](/new-york-stock-exchange/) uses the DTCC; European exchanges typically use Euroclear or Clearstream. MOEX operates its own clearing division (National Clearing Centre), handling settlement, guarantees, and counterparty risk management internally.

This vertical integration reduces operational friction and allows MOEX to coordinate pricing, margins, and liquidity management across segments with minimal intermediary cost. It also creates concentrated systemic risk: any operational failure at MOEX's clearing layer affects equities, derivatives, FX, and money markets simultaneously. Regulators and the Bank of Russia oversee this arrangement closely.

MOEX operates four primary market segments: the main equities board (RTS Standard), the alternative board for growth firms (Innovatsiya), a government securities market (OFZ), and foreign currency trading (which has grown to be one of the world's largest FX venues by notional volume).

## Equities and the RTS index

The **RTS Index** is MOEX's primary benchmark, a [market-capitalisation](/market-capitalization/)-weighted index of the largest and most liquid Russian stocks. The index comprises roughly 50 companies, though weighting is heavily skewed toward state-owned or state-controlled enterprises (Gazprom, Rosneft, Sberbank, Lukoil, Magnet retail, and telecommunications firms).

State ownership and control is endemic in the Russian market. Many listed firms are majority-owned or substantially controlled by the state or state-connected oligarchs, creating governance concerns and regulatory uncertainty for international investors. While some Russian firms operate with transparent accounting and boards, others face allegations of related-party transactions, opaque ownership, and exclusion of minority shareholders from economic upside.

Listing standards on MOEX are lighter than in developed markets. Information asymmetries between insiders and outsiders are significant, and enforcement of minority shareholder protections has been inconsistent. These governance weaknesses have historically suppressed valuations and limited international institutional participation.

## FX trading and the ruble

Remarkably, MOEX is one of the world's largest venues for FX trading by notional volume. The ruble (USD/RUB and EUR/RUB pair) trades extensively on MOEX's FX segment, driven by Russian corporates hedging commodity revenues, oil and gas companies managing export proceeds, and international banks trading the ruble for speculation and inventory management.

The ruble is volatile and highly correlated with oil prices (Russia's largest export), geopolitical risk, and central-bank policy. MOEX's FX volume reflects the maturity of Russian corporate FX hedging and the significant daily flows generated by commodity exporters converting foreign earnings into rubles for domestic spending.

During sanctions and periods of geopolitical tension, the ruble has experienced sharp devaluation, and MOEX's FX market has transmitted these shocks to global FX venues. The exchange has occasionally implemented temporary trading halts or restrictions during extreme volatility, a measure that underscores the concentrated importance of MOEX to Russian financial stability.

## Money markets and government debt

MOEX hosts trading in Russian government securities (OFZ, or federal loan bonds, in Russian) and interbank lending. The money market is essential infrastructure: Russian banks use MOEX to borrow and lend reserves, manage liquidity, and hedge interest-rate risk.

Trading in OFZ is substantial and has grown as Russia's central bank and government use monetary policy and fiscal spending to manage the economy. International investors historically held Russian government debt, but sanctions post-2014 and especially post-2022 have reduced foreign participation, making the market more domestically focused and exposing it to domestic liquidity shocks.

## Derivatives and commodities

MOEX's derivatives segment lists futures and options on the RTS index, individual stocks, the ruble, interest rates, and commodity indices (particularly oil and natural gas, given Russia's resource-dependent economy). Commodity futures are heavily used by energy companies and utilities to manage price exposure.

The exchange also lists contracts on global commodities: crude oil and Brent futures reference global pricing but allow Russian exporters and traders to hedge local positions without accessing overseas venues.

## International access and sanctions impact

Until 2022, MOEX was accessible to international investors through established brokers and clearing relationships. International funds held Russian equities as an emerging-market allocation, and global banks traded the ruble and Russian government debt actively.

The 2022 invasion of Ukraine and subsequent international sanctions severed most links between MOEX and global financial markets. International brokers halted client access to Russian equities, and major settlement infrastructures (Euroclear, Clearstream) closed Russian accounts. This isolation dramatically reduced foreign participation and liquidity for Russian assets, confining MOEX largely to domestic investors.

The sanctions also affected MOEX's operations: international IT providers withdrew services, some data feed vendors ceased operations, and MOEX faced operational challenges managing settlements while excluded from global clearing systems. The exchange adapted by strengthening domestic clearing and settlement relationships, but the global disconnection has persisted, making MOEX increasingly a closed market.

## Governance and regulation

The Bank of Russia and the Federal Financial Markets Service oversee MOEX. The exchange operates under Russian securities law, which has evolved toward stricter capital requirements and risk management but remains less prescriptive than SEC or FCA regulations. Enforcement priorities reflect the state's economic and geopolitical interests: financial stability is prioritized, but minority investor protection and transparent governance enforcement are inconsistent.

MOEX's status as a critical financial infrastructure gives the Russian government strong incentives to ensure its stability and operational continuity, particularly during periods of external financial stress or sanctions.
