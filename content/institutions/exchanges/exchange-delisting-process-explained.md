*A **stock exchange delisting** is the removal of a company's shares from public trading on a regulated exchange. Delisting can be forced by the exchange due to non-compliance with listing standards (financial tests, governance rules, or disclosure requirements) or can be initiated by the company itself when it decides to go private. Once delisted, shares can still trade, but only in smaller, less liquid [over-the-counter](/over-the-counter-market/) markets.*

## Mandatory Delisting: The Financial Triggers

Every stock exchange publishes listing standards that all [public companies](/public-company/) must follow. When a company breaches these standards, the exchange sends a notice and usually grants a grace period to cure the problem. If the problem is not remedied, the exchange moves toward delisting.

The most common reasons for forced delisting are:

**Minimum closing price.** The NASDAQ and NYSE both impose minimum stock price thresholds. If a stock closes below $1 for 30 consecutive trading days, the exchange issues a warning. The company has 90 calendar days to close at $1.00 or above for 10 consecutive trading days. If it fails, delisting proceedings begin. Some exchanges have graduated thresholds; a stock above $0.50 might get additional extensions, while stocks below $0.50 face faster proceedings.

**Market capitalization floors.** Companies must maintain a minimum [market value](/market-capitalization/). For the NASDAQ, this is typically $35 million (though there are multiple tests and the exact threshold depends on the listing tier). For the NYSE, minimums are higher. If a company's market cap falls below the threshold for 30 consecutive trading days, it enters the cure period.

**Financial test failures.** The exchanges administer quantitative tests tied to shareholder equity, net income, and revenue. If a company fails any of these for two consecutive years, delisting is triggered unless it cures within the grace period (usually 60–90 days). These tests prevent "shell companies" — entities with no real business — from occupying exchange slots.

**Governance breaches.** If a company fails to maintain an independent audit committee, an independent [board of directors](/board-of-directors/), or fails annual shareholder votes on executive compensation, the exchange can initiate delisting. These rules enforce [corporate governance](/board-of-directors/) standards across all listed companies.

**Disclosure failures.** If a company stops filing required financial statements ([10-K](/10-k/), quarterly filings) with the [SEC](/securities-and-exchange-commission/), the exchange issues a notice and typically gives 60 days to file. Non-compliance triggers delisting. This is a rare but serious breach.

## The Delisting Notice and Cure Period

When a company breaches a standard, the exchange does not immediately remove the listing. Instead:

1. **Notice is issued.** The exchange notifies the company in writing of the specific violation and the cure requirement.

2. **Grace period opens.** Depending on the violation, the company typically has 30 to 180 days to cure. For price-based thresholds, grace periods are often strictly defined (e.g., 30 days above $1.00). For financial metrics, they can be longer and may account for the next reporting cycle.

3. **Company can request a hearing.** The company can formally object, request a hearing before the exchange's listing panel, and present a remediation plan. If the company has a credible path to compliance (e.g., a pending merger at a premium price, imminent profitability, or a capital raise), the panel may grant additional time.

4. **Delisting notice (if cure fails).** If the company does not cure within the grace period and the panel denies an appeal, the exchange issues a "Notification of Removal from Listing" with an effective date, typically 10 trading days later.

5. **Final delisting.** On the effective date, the company's shares cease trading on the exchange and are moved to the over-the-counter market.

## Voluntary Delisting: Going Private

A company can choose to delist its shares, usually as part of a going-private transaction. This happens when:

- A [leveraged buyout](/leveraged-buyout/) firm buys the company and takes it off the market.
- An activist investor or [management team](/board-of-directors/) wins shareholder approval for a privatization.
- The company is acquired by another firm that consolidates it as a subsidiary.

In a voluntary delisting, the company files a Form 25 (in the U.S.) notifying the SEC of its intent. It typically offers to buy out remaining [public shareholders](/public-company/) at a negotiated price, approved by a vote. Once the buyout closes and shareholders are paid, the company stops operating as a publicly traded [public company](/public-company/) and its shares are delisted at an agreed-upon date.

## What Happens to Shareholders After Delisting

When a stock is delisted from a major exchange, shareholders do not automatically lose their shares; the shares continue to exist and represent the same ownership claim. However, trading conditions change dramatically:

**Over-the-counter trading.** The stock moves to the OTC market, trading on platforms like the OTC Pink Sheets or the OTC Bulletin Board (now part of OTC Markets Group). These venues have minimal regulatory oversight compared to exchanges, wider [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/), and much lower volume.

**Reduced liquidity.** An investor who owned 1,000 shares of a delisted company might find it extremely difficult to sell. There may be few buyers at any price, and any sale will likely occur at a significant discount to the pre-delisting price.

**Broker restrictions.** Many brokers restrict or forbid their clients from trading over-the-counter securities without explicit approval, citing the higher risk and lower regulation. Some brokers will not execute OTC trades at all, effectively freezing client positions.

**Dividend and voting rights persist.** If the company continues to pay dividends, shareholders still receive them (though the company may reduce or suspend dividends as part of a distressed restructuring). Shareholders still have voting rights on shareholder matters, though shareholder meetings become less relevant if the company is in bankruptcy or the subject of [debt restructuring](/debt-restructuring/).

**Forced sales.** In the worst cases, if a company enters [bankruptcy](/liquidation/) and the shares are deemed worthless, shareholders may be wiped out entirely. If the company is acquired or restructured, shareholders may lose their claims if they are junior to debt holders.

## Delisting Mechanics Across Major Exchanges

**NASDAQ rules**: Stocks must maintain a minimum closing price of $1.00. The grace period is 90 calendar days. Companies can request a hearing and, if approved, may get an additional 180 days. Financial thresholds include minimum shareholder equity and net income tests applied over rolling periods.

**NYSE rules**: Similar $1.00 minimum price test, but grace periods and test thresholds differ. The NYSE allows for expedited delisting in cases of fraud or extreme non-compliance.

**TSX (Toronto Stock Exchange)**: Canadian companies face minimum shareholder equity, minimum market capitalization, and minimum trading volume standards. Grace periods vary by standard and whether the company is a development-stage company.

Each exchange also maintains listing tiers (e.g., NASDAQ Global Select, NASDAQ Capital Market) with different thresholds, allowing smaller or riskier companies to list under stricter monitoring.

## Why Companies Face Delisting Risk

Delisting is rare for large, healthy companies but common for:

- **Distressed or insolvent firms**: When a company's financial condition deteriorates, its stock price falls, triggering price-based delisting thresholds.
- **Shell companies or failed startups**: Companies that never achieved profitability or revenue often fall below financial tests.
- **Companies losing market confidence**: A fraud scandal, executive departure, or competitive failure can tank the stock price and trigger delisting within months.
- **Private equity deals**: A sound company may be delisted voluntarily as part of a going-private transaction.

The delisting process forces discipline on listed companies. The threat of losing exchange access incentivizes compliance with governance and disclosure rules, which protects [public shareholders](/public-company/) and the broader market.
