Kelly Services operates one of the largest staffing and workforce solutions networks in the United States and internationally. The company makes its money by placing workers with employers — both temporary assignments and permanent placements — taking a cut from the fees employers pay or the markup on worker wages. It is regulated by labor laws in every jurisdiction it serves, which both protects it (competitors face the same rules) and constrains it (regulations can shift the cost structure).

## The staffing industry's core business

Kelly Services' work is straightforward on the surface: a company needs workers, Kelly finds and hires them, places them at the client, and earns money from the transaction. For temporary workers, Kelly typically charges the employer a percentage of the worker's wage — often 20 to 30 percent markup depending on the role and market. For permanent placements, the company earns a one-time fee, often tied to the hired worker's first-year salary. The cash moves fast: workers are paid from Kelly's payroll, and employers reimburse Kelly, creating a churn of money through the business daily.

The company operates across multiple segments. Its core Americas segment handles the bulk of placements — light industrial, administrative, technical roles, and nursing — in the United States, Canada, and Latin America. A separate EMEA (Europe, Middle East, Africa) segment runs similar operations internationally. A smaller professional services and staffing has grown through acquisition and organic expansion.

## What makes staffing a durable business

Staffing works because employers will always need flexibility. Seasonal spikes, unexpected departures, temporary projects, and economic uncertainty all create demand for workers who can be hired and released without the commitment of permanent employment. For workers, it offers a path into employment when permanent jobs aren't readily available. Both sides prefer working with an established staffing firm rather than hiring randomly or managing payroll themselves.

But the industry is low-margin and labor-intensive. Placing a worker requires recruiting, screening, onboarding, and ongoing payroll administration. The company holds inventory in the form of workers between assignments. If a worker is unproductive, injured, or quits, Kelly absorbs the loss. Larger clients have leverage to negotiate lower markups. And in a strong labor market, good workers can find direct permanent employment, tightening the pool Kelly can access. In a weak labor market, employers cut their use of staffing, hurting volume.

The regulatory environment shapes everything. Staffing companies are classified as employers and must comply with payroll taxes, benefits rules (where applicable), and labor law — including rules that vary by state and country around wages, hours, overtime, worker classification, and benefits eligibility. These regulations raise the compliance cost but also create a moat: smaller competitors struggle with the overhead, while Kelly's scale lets it spread the cost.

## Scale and diversification as a hedge

Kelly has weathered decades of economic cycles by operating at substantial scale and diversifying both by geography and by segment. The United States remains the largest market, but international operations provide some hedge. The different segments — industrial, administrative, technical, professional — respond differently to economic cycles, so a downturn in one is sometimes offset by steadiness in another.

The company has invested in technology to improve matching and reduce friction: online platforms where workers log availability and employers request workers, automated scheduling, and data analytics to forecast demand and supply. These tools help Kelly operate more efficiently than a purely labor-intensive staffing model would allow, though the competitive advantage of any single tool is typically short-lived because rivals adopt similar systems.

## The permanent headwinds and how Kelly earns anyway

Staffing is a business where the customer (the employer) is always shopping for a better price. Large employers can threaten to build internal recruitment functions or use competing staffing firms to pressure Kelly down. Remote work has increased geographic competition: a worker in one state can now fill a role in another, fragmenting local monopolies. Automation in certain roles has reduced demand for temporary workers in some categories, particularly in data entry and basic administrative work.

Yet Kelly remains viable because the core value proposition — handling the complexity of hiring, payroll, compliance, and worker management — is real, and most employers prefer outsourcing it to doing it themselves. The company's age (founded 1946) and brand recognition give it an advantage in winning client relationships, though brand loyalty is limited when a competitor offers better economics.

## Tracking Kelly as an investment

Anyone evaluating Kelly should review its annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0000055135) for the breakdown of revenue by segment and geography, margins by segment, and commentary on pricing trends and cost pressures. Key metrics include the revenue-per-worker-per-year (indicating pricing and productivity), the gross margin trend (showing whether Kelly is winning or losing price wars), and turnover among large clients (a sign of competitive weakness or strength).

Staffing companies are also highly sensitive to the labor market. Watch the unemployment rate and average wages: when unemployment falls and wage inflation rises, employers hire permanent staff directly, hurting temporary-staffing demand and increasing the wages Kelly must pay to attract workers, squeezing margins. Conversely, in a downturn, temporary staffing demand can spike, but pricing pressure intensifies. The quarterly earnings calls reveal which segments are growing and where management sees strength and weakness.
