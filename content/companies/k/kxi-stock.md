*The **iShares Global Consumer Staples ETF** (ticker KXI) is a fund that holds the global makers of food, beverages, household products, and personal-care items — companies whose products people buy regardless of economic cycle. It is a passive, diversified exposure to the consumer staples sector across developed markets, structured to emphasize dividend-paying companies and common enough that it can be held as a core sleeve in many portfolios.*

## What the fund holds and why

KXI tracks an index of consumer-staples companies from developed markets — the United States, Europe, Japan, Australia, and other high-income countries. The holdings are the familiar multinational giants: makers of packaged food, beverages, household cleaners, personal hygiene, and supermarket staples. These are companies whose products sit on shelves worldwide and whose brands carry decades or generations of consumer habit.

The sector's defining economic property is low correlation to the [business cycle](/business-cycle/). When [recessions](/recession/) arrive, people still buy laundry detergent, toothpaste, and baked beans. They may buy less steak and fewer cosmetics, but the basic unit volumes remain stable. This stability is why consumer staples has long been treated as a defensive sector — a place to park capital when the outlook turns uncertain or when a portfolio needs steadying.

The fund itself is cap-weighted, meaning the largest companies in the index carry the largest weights. It holds dozens of positions and is broad enough that no single company dominates, making it a true sector exposure rather than a bet on a few names. The large multinational players — which operate in dozens of countries and have the scale to distribute globally — make up the bulk of the portfolio.

## Dividend and income focus

One feature that surfaces in KXI's name and structure is emphasis on dividend-paying stocks. Consumer staples, as a sector, has historically offered higher [current yield](/current-yield/) than the broader [stock market](/stock-market/). Because these are mature, cash-generative businesses with stable profit streams, they often return capital to shareholders as [dividends](/dividend/) rather than reinvesting it for rapid growth. A consumer-staples company is unlikely to double its earnings in three years, so investors in the space implicitly expect to be paid along the way.

This income profile attracts a particular type of investor — retirees seeking current cash, income-focused funds, and dividend-growth strategies. It also means the fund's total return comes partly from price appreciation and partly from the quarterly dividends that accumulate in the account. The yield is qualitatively higher than the broader market, though it varies with economic cycle and [interest rates](/interest-rate/).

## Geographic diversification and currency risk

The fund's "global" mandate means holdings span North America, Europe, and Asia-Pacific. This geographic spread is both a strength and a source of complexity. It provides exposure to staples demand in multiple developed economies and reduces concentration in any single country's fortunes. It also means the fund carries currency exposure: holdings in [Japanese yen](/japanese-yen/), euros, pounds sterling, and other developed-market currencies create daily fluctuations relative to a US-dollar holder. When the dollar strengthens, foreign holdings are worth less in dollar terms; when it weakens, they are worth more. This currency movement is real and can meaningfully affect returns.

## Costs, liquidity, and how to trade

As an iShares product, KXI benefits from the scale and operational efficiency of [BlackRock](/blk-stock/), one of the largest fund operators globally. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is qualitatively low, typical of broad, passive, developed-market [sector funds](/sector-fund/). The fund trades on a major exchange with high daily volume, making it simple to buy and sell in any account that can hold equities.

The most important thing to know about the fund's structure is that it is a plain vanilla exchange-traded fund — not leveraged, not inverse. It aims to track the index as closely as possible, moving up or down broadly in line with the consumer-staples sector. There are no special mechanics, no daily rebalancing bets, no derivatives woven into the structure.

## What makes this fund different from picking individual stocks

A reader might ask: why buy a fund of consumer-staples companies when I could simply own the three or four large ones I know best? The answer is [diversification](/diversification/) and simplification. Even within consumer staples, there are meaningful differences in exposure. Some companies are more exposed to North American volume, others to emerging-market growth (which matters for staples, despite the sector's label). Some have stronger brands in beverages, others in household products. Some are facing currency headwinds in regions where they have factories. By holding all of them in one fund, an investor gets the sector exposure without the bet on whether [Procter & Gamble](/pg-stock/) will outperform [Unilever](/ul-stock/), or whether Nestlé's emerging-market exposure will prove a boon or a liability.

The fund also eliminates the need to monitor individual holdings or rebalance between them. That hands-off quality is useful for investors who want the sector's characteristics without the ongoing work of stock-picking.

## Real risks and limits

Consumer staples may be defensive, but the sector is not risk-free. [Inflation](/inflation/) — especially in input costs like agricultural commodities, packaging, and energy — can squeeze margins. If inflation outpaces pricing power, staples companies may see profitability shrink. Interest-rate rises make the [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) look less attractive relative to [bond](/bond/) yields, which can pressure the share prices. Meanwhile, the sector has limited growth — a mature market where volume expansion is slow — so returns ultimately depend on dividend yield and valuation compression or expansion. In a market where growth stocks are outperforming, a staples fund will lag.

Currency moves can also create drag for a US investor: if the dollar strengthens sharply, the overseas holdings become less valuable in dollar terms, offsetting any local-currency gains. And like all [index funds](/index-fund/), KXI cannot outperform its index by definition; it can only track it, mingling a small amount of performance drag against the actual index due to costs and cash drag.

## How to research and think about this fund

The core research is the fund's holdings list and the underlying index methodology. BlackRock publishes both freely; you can see exactly which companies the fund holds and in what proportions. From there, the relevant questions are:

- What is the fund's current yield, and how does it compare to bond yields and inflation? If bonds are paying more than the dividend, the income case is weaker.
- What is the fund's exposure to specific geographic markets and [currency risk](/currency-risk/)?
- How is the sector itself positioned within the broader market — cheap or expensive on traditional metrics?

For any single company question, the fund's factsheet lists the top holdings. The [fund prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) and [annual report](/10-k/) are public and spell out the index methodology and costs. A fund like KXI is often best thought of not as a standalone position but as a complement to growth-oriented holdings — a stabilizing presence that provides cash and stability while equity-market volatility surrounds it elsewhere in a portfolio.
