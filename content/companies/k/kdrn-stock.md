*The **Kingsbarn Tactical [Bond ETF](/bond-etf/)** (KDRN) is an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) giving its managers discretion to allocate across different segments of the [bond](/bond/) market — government bonds, corporate debt, international bonds, high-yield securities — based on their assessment of relative value and economic outlook. No fixed index; the portfolio shifts.*

## The active bond management premise

Most bond ETFs are passive or index-tracking. They hold a fixed basket mirroring a published bond [index, rebalancing](/index-rebalancing/) mechanically as bonds mature or new issues enter the index. This keeps costs low and ensures transparency — the holdings are known in advance.

KDRN takes a different approach. The fund's managers actively decide how much to allocate to each bond type and market segment. When they believe government bonds are overvalued, they reduce that exposure. When corporate spreads — the extra yield paid for corporate debt above risk-free bonds — look cheap relative to [credit risk](/credit-risk/), they increase corporate holdings. If they see opportunity in international bonds or emerging-market debt, they can add exposure. The portfolio is dynamic, not static.

This flexibility comes with a tradeoff: higher fees than passive bond funds, and the risk that active decisions underperform or lag the market. The promise is that skillful timing and stock-picking in the bond market can deliver better risk-adjusted returns than simply buying and holding an index.

## The tactical lever

The "tactical" in the name points to shorter-term decision-making rather than a buy-and-hold philosophy. Tactical managers respond to changing valuations — they notice when a particular bond segment has become expensive or cheap and rotate in or out accordingly. They assess economic signals — if a [recession](/recession/) looms, they might reduce corporate credit exposure and add government bonds. If [inflation](/inflation/) appears to be breaking, they might position differently for interest-rate moves.

A tactical approach requires frequent monitoring and active trading. The manager is rebalancing based on judgment calls about economic cycles, [credit spreads](/credit-spread/), and relative value across bond markets. Some managers are skilled at this; others mistakenly trade too much, incurring costs that eat into returns, or make calls that turn out wrong. Tactical funds are more dependent on manager skill than passive funds, where the mechanics handle themselves.

## What KDRN typically owns

The holdings are bonds — debt securities issued by governments, corporations, and international borrowers. The fund does not hold stocks. Because the managers have discretion, the composition shifts, but a typical allocation might span U.S. [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) (long and short-dated), investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/), international government bonds, and emerging-market debt. The fund may also hold cash equivalents or money-market instruments when the managers see value drained from bonds overall.

The holdings are marked to market daily, meaning the share price floats with the value of the underlying bonds. Unlike individual bonds, which you can hold to maturity and collect [par value](/par-value/), an ETF share price will fluctuate based on [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and credit conditions. That is the nature of owning fund shares rather than the bonds directly.

## Interest-rate and credit risk

Bonds are sensitive to interest-rate moves. When rates rise, bond prices fall — a bondholder locked into an older, lower rate faces capital loss if forced to sell. The longer the maturity, the larger the move. KDRN's duration — its sensitivity to rate moves — depends on what the managers are holding at any moment. A portfolio heavy in long-dated bonds carries high duration and volatility. One concentrated in short bonds or floating-rate debt carries low duration and is more stable.

Credit risk is also present. The fund holds corporate and high-yield bonds, which carry the risk that an issuer defaults or weakens financially, causing the bond's value to drop. The managers are betting they can navigate this risk — owning credit-risky bonds when they are attractively priced, reducing exposure when credit appears vulnerable. In broad credit crises, all corporate bonds struggle; a tactical approach does not eliminate that risk, only rotates timing.

## The expense ratio and manager skill

Active bond management is not cheap. KDRN's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) will be meaningfully higher than a passive bond ETF — perhaps two to four times higher — because of the salaries of credit analysts, traders, and portfolio managers. The fund makes sense only if the added performance covers that extra cost. Over long periods, most active fixed-income managers underperform their benchmark before fees, and many do not beat it afterward. So the burden of proof is on the managers to demonstrate skill worth the cost.

Track the fund's return relative to a broad bond index — a benchmark like the Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index — to see whether active management has added value. Compare it to both passive alternatives and other active bond funds. Performance can vary sharply by manager and by market environment, so look at returns over multiple years and different rate-cycle periods.

## Trading and liquidity

The fund trades throughout the day on an exchange at a price set by buyers and sellers. The spreads — the gap between bid and ask prices — depend on the volume of trading in KDRN and the underlying bond market's liquidity. Bonds are less liquid than stocks; a large redemption from the fund could force managers to sell less-liquid bonds at unfavorable prices, a cost borne by remaining shareholders.

## Where this fund fits

KDRN appeals to bond investors who believe active management can exploit pricing inefficiencies in the bond market and are willing to pay for that skill. It is also useful for investors who want bond exposure but prefer the flexibility of an actively rebalancing strategy over the fixed nature of an index. Some investors use tactical bond funds as a substitute for a portion of their core fixed-income allocation, expecting the active rotation to generate excess returns.

The fund is not for investors who believe markets are efficient or who want the predictability and low costs of index investing. It is also not for those expecting sharply rising rates — a tactical manager cannot protect against that, only navigate it more or less skillfully.

Evaluating KDRN means reviewing historical performance — both absolute returns and returns relative to relevant benchmarks — over long periods. Check whether the manager has outperformed consistently or whether outperformance is spottier. Look at turnover, the frequency with which the fund trades, to understand whether the tactical approach is delivering better results than the costs it incurs. Read the fund's communications and reports to understand the philosophy and reasoning behind current allocations. And do not confuse past performance — which cannot be guaranteed — with evidence that future returns will surpass a passive alternative.
