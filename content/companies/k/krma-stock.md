*The **Global X Conscious Companies ETF** (KRMA) selects equities based on environmental, social, and governance standards, offering investors a vehicle for allocating capital to companies that meet sustainability and ethical benchmarks.*

## What it tracks and why it matters

KRMA follows an index of companies screened for ESG characteristics — firms that meet or exceed thresholds on carbon emissions relative to peers, labour practices, board diversity, executive compensation, and other governance metrics. The fund removes outright exclusions: tobacco manufacturers, weapons makers, coal producers. What remains is a diversified basket that still carries significant technology and financial services exposure, though weighted toward companies with stronger ESG profiles than the Russell 1000 or S&P 500.

The underlying index is not static. Constituent companies are re-screened periodically; those that slip on ESG measures can be removed. A company that was acceptable on emissions five years ago might fall below threshold today if it has not improved. That dynamic element means KRMA's composition drifts over time in response to corporate behaviour, not just price movement.

## The investor logic

ESG-driven investment rests on two premises. The first is that environmental and governance risks eventually become financial risks — a company with weak climate strategy or poor management oversight faces regulatory, reputational, or operational headwinds that will depress returns. The second is that conscious consumers and employees increasingly direct their wallets and careers toward ethical firms, creating a virtuous loop where good conduct drives performance.

Neither premise is uniformly true. The academic literature is mixed: some studies show ESG portfolios deliver competitive or superior risk-adjusted returns; others show they lag. Much depends on time horizon, market cycle, and how ESG is measured. KRMA's design hedges these debates by offering plain exposure to companies that score well on published ESG criteria, letting investors form their own view on whether that screening adds value over time.

## Structure and scope

KRMA is a plain vanilla ETF, not leveraged or inverse. It holds a basket of roughly 400–500 companies across all sectors, with a cap on the weight of any single holding. It is not a thematic fund focused on clean energy or renewable technology; rather, it is a broad-market barbell that filters for ESG quality within existing industries. A financial services company with strong diversity and climate disclosures might sit in KRMA alongside a tech firm with low carbon intensity. An industrials company with robust labour practices and transparent supply chains can coexist with a consumer discretionary firm that scores well on governance.

The result is a fund that looks like the broad market to a first glance but with measurable differences in composition: lower exposure to fossil fuels and defence, often a slight tilt toward larger, more established companies that have resources to invest in ESG infrastructure, and some sector over- or underweights driven by the screening process.

## Costs and trading

KRMA carries a modest [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) typical of diversified [equity ETFs](/equity-etf/) from a major issuer. The fund trades with ample liquidity on a major exchange; [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) are tight. Daily volume is sufficient that a retail investor or small institution can establish or exit a position without market impact.

## Risks and limitations

ESG screening does not eliminate traditional [market risk](/market-risk/). KRMA has held significant weight in technology stocks at various points in its history; when that sector corrects sharply, KRMA moves in lock-step. The fund also carries the usual equity risks: sector concentration, interest-rate sensitivity, and the broad market risk that equities face in a [recession](/recession/).

A secondary consideration is ESG definition creep. Different vendors use different ESG metrics; KRMA's index is built on one set of criteria, and those criteria shift as academic and investor consensus evolves. A company that is considered "conscious" by today's scoring may not qualify in five years if ESG standards tighten. This is not a flaw — it reflects genuine progress on sustainability standards — but it means KRMA is not a static choice.

## How to research KRMA

Review the prospectus and the index methodology on Global X's website to understand exactly which ESG criteria drive inclusion and exclusion. Check the fund's top holdings and sector allocation, then compare to a broad-market fund like the SPY or VOO to see where the screening has shifted the composition. Watch the fund's ESG rankings and note whether the underlying index has adjusted companies in or out; high turnover could signal that ESG standards are tightening or that constituents are falling short. Track the fund's performance relative to an unscreened broad-market index; that spread tells you whether the ESG filter has been a return drag or a modest tailwind in any given period.
