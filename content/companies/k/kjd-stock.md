> Designed for days or weeks, not months or years. Every day of sideways movement bleeds capital.

KJD is a [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) tracking twice the daily return of JD.com, the Chinese e-commerce and logistics company listed on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/). On days JD.com rises 1 percent, KJD aims to rise 2 percent. On days it falls 1 percent, KJD falls 2 percent. The leverage is achieved through a portfolio of the underlying stock plus financial derivatives — options, futures, or swaps — that the fund rebalances daily to maintain the 2x multiplier. That daily rebalancing is the critical detail: leverage is not static but reset every trading day. This structure, while simple to describe, creates a hidden erosion of value called volatility decay that quietly destroys long-term returns even when the underlying stock goes nowhere.

Volatility decay emerges from the mathematics of leveraged daily returns. Consider a stylized example: JD.com opens at 100, rises to 101 (a 1 percent gain) on day one, then falls back to 100 (a 0.99 percent loss) on day two. The stock finishes two days unchanged. But KJD, leveraged 2x, rises 2 percent on day one (ending at approximately 102), then falls 1.98 percent on day two, leaving it at roughly 99.98. Despite the underlying stock being flat, KJD is underwater. This erosion accelerates as volatility increases. A stock that swings wildly up and down with identical average returns experiences far more decay than one that climbs steadily. Because percentage losses compound, the 2x multiplier magnifies both directions unequally: gains and losses are not symmetric at 2x.

JD.com, as a large-cap Chinese tech and e-commerce company, carries moderate to high daily volatility, especially during periods of market stress or China-specific regulatory announcements. Over weeks and months of typical daily swings, decay becomes material. A study of historical leveraged ETF performance shows that even in [flat markets](/flat-market/), they can trail their stated 2x target return by several percentage points annually, depending on realized volatility. In sideways or choppy markets, decay is particularly punishing.

KJD is explicitly designed for short-term tactical trading, typically days or weeks. A trader who expects JD.com to rise in the near term might use KJD to double the upside exposure with less capital at risk than buying an outright 2x position using margin. The fund's tight [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) and substantial daily volume enable intraday trading. But holding KJD as a long-term investment is a mistake. Decay will erode returns silently over months and years, regardless of whether JD.com stock itself rises. Many retail investors buy leveraged ETFs and forget to exit, holding them through multiple market cycles and suffering compounding damage from decay.

The fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is moderate, reflecting the cost of daily rebalancing and the derivatives used to track the leverage target. KraneShares charges a transparent fee, embedded in the fund's price. This fee is the cost of liquidity and the hedging mechanism; it is unavoidable.

The risks are multidimensional. Most obviously, leverage magnifies losses. A 10 percent fall in JD.com stock produces a 20 percent loss in KJD. A 50 percent decline obliterates the fund entirely. Beyond directional risk, volatility decay — the quiet erosion described above — compounds in choppy markets. Additionally, JD.com is a Chinese company, not a U.S. corporation. It faces geopolitical risk (U.S.–China tensions, potential delistings), China-specific regulatory action (antitrust, data security, e-commerce restrictions), currency fluctuations, and the political risk of ownership concentration or state intervention. KJD amplifies all of these risks by a factor of two. In periods of severe stress on Chinese tech stocks, JD.com can plunge sharply, and KJD will fall twice as fast. Several times in recent years, U.S. legislators have proposed or threatened delisting Chinese companies, causing JD.com stock to spike or plunge intraday based on regulatory headlines. KJD would move twice as sharply.

The fund is wholly unsuitable for passive investors, retirement accounts, or anyone who cannot afford a 50 percent loss on their position. The prospectus carries clear warnings about daily reset and volatility decay, but many retail investors overlook or misunderstand them, only realizing too late that the fund's value has eroded despite the underlying stock holding steady or even rising modestly.
