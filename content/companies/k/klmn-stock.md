*The **Invesco MSCI North America Climate ETF (KLMN)** is a thematic equity fund that holds U.S. and Canadian companies selected for relatively low carbon footprints and strong environmental governance. It attempts to give investors North American [stock market](/stock-market/) exposure while filtering out the highest-emission businesses.*

## What "climate investing" actually means in this ETF

Climate investing has become a catch-all term for everything from divesting from fossil fuels entirely to holding companies that are slowly reducing emissions. KLMN sits in the middle of that spectrum: it does not exclude oil, gas, and coal producers entirely, but it weights toward companies with lower emissions per unit of revenue, stronger climate-risk disclosures, and governance practices that suggest a serious commitment to reducing their carbon footprint.

The fund tracks the MSCI North America Climate Change Index, which is constructed using MSCI's proprietary climate and environmental data. The methodology scores companies on carbon intensity (emissions relative to revenue), exposure to climate risks, and whether they have set credible net-zero or emissions-reduction targets. It then systematically overweights lower-emission companies and underweights higher-emission ones within the universe of large and mid-cap North American equities.

This is not a hard exclusion — it is a scoring and weighting approach. An oil producer with genuinely low-cost extraction and credible decarbonization plans might still appear in KLMN, just at a lower weight than, say, a renewable-energy infrastructure company. The result is a portfolio that skews markedly greener than the S&P 500, but one that still maintains [diversification](/diversification/) across sectors and retains exposure to energy, materials, and other traditionally carbon-intensive industries.

## The construction: MSCI's climate methodology

MSCI, a data provider owned by [S&P Global](/spgi-stock/), maintains detailed environmental, social, and governance ratings on tens of thousands of companies. For KLMN's index, MSCI's climate methodology focuses on several dimensions. Carbon intensity (tonnes of emissions per million dollars of revenue) is a primary one; companies with lower ratios score higher. Exposure to climate risks — whether a company operates in flood-prone areas, relies on water-intensive operations, or is exposed to renewable-energy transitions — is another. A third is management quality: whether the company has committed to science-based decarbonization targets, whether it discloses emissions thoroughly, and whether it invests in clean technology.

The index construction then applies these scores to weight companies within a cap-constrained framework. Large caps (like [Microsoft](/msft-stock/) or [Apple](/aapl-stock/)) can compose a certain maximum of the index even if their climate scores are excellent, to prevent concentration. Sector diversification is also preserved, so the fund does not tilt so far toward clean-energy and tech that it becomes a sector bet rather than a market bet.

This approach has a trade-off: it captures much of the diversification of a broad North American equity index while tilting toward lower-carbon and more climate-conscious companies. But it is not a pure "clean energy" or "ESG" fund. It is a broad-market North American equity fund with a climate filter applied.

## How KLMN differs from the broader market

In normal market conditions, KLMN will look somewhat different from the S&P 500 or a total-market index. Energy stocks — oil, gas, and coal producers — will be underweight; renewable-energy infrastructure, energy-efficient industrials, and clean-tech companies will be overweight. Finance and materials companies with strong environmental practices will be at full or higher weight; those with poor climate practices might be underweight.

Over the past decade, this tilt has meant that KLMN likely outperformed during years when clean energy, tech, and climate-conscious companies led markets, and underperformed during years when energy and traditional industrials rallied. The relationship is not deterministic — other factors, like valuations and [interest rates](/interest-rate/), matter enormously — but the thematic tilt is real and will be visible over any multi-year period.

## Investors and motivations

KLMN appeals to several categories of investor. Fiduciaries and institutions with mandates to integrate climate considerations into their equity portfolios use it as a core holding, replacing a portion of their broad-market index allocation with one that has the same scope but a climate lens. Individual investors who believe climate change poses material financial risks to companies and want to avoid the highest-emission businesses use KLMN to achieve that without sacrificing diversification. And some investors simply believe that lower-carbon companies will outperform over the long term as regulations tighten, investor [capital flows](/capital-flows/), and customers and employees increasingly demand sustainable practices — for them, KLMN is a conviction bet, not just a risk-management tool.

Investors skeptical of climate thematic tilts or those betting that traditional energy will outperform should probably stick with a plain broad-market [index fund](/index-fund/).

## Expense ratio and flows

KLMN's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is higher than a simple S&P 500 or total-market index fund but lower than most actively managed equity funds. The cost covers the licensing of MSCI's climate data and index methodology, fund administration, and Invesco's management. It is qualitatively moderate for an ETF with a specialty tilt.

Like most [thematic ETFs](/thematic-etf/), KLMN's asset base has grown significantly as climate-related investing has become mainstream, which has improved liquidity and likely narrowed the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) over time.

## Risks and limitations

The climate filter is only as good as MSCI's data and methodology, which evolve. If MSCI's carbon-accounting standards shift, what counts as "low-carbon" can change, which could alter the fund's composition and performance. Additionally, companies can change: a low-carbon score is not a guarantee of future environmental leadership, and corporate scandals or policy shifts can upend climate credentials overnight.

The North America focus means limited geographic diversification — KLMN has no European, Asian, or emerging-market exposure. An investor seeking global climate-conscious equity exposure would need a different fund.

Finally, and critically, a climate-filtered North American equity index is still an equity fund and still subject to stock-market risk. In a broad market downturn, KLMN will fall, climate credentials notwithstanding. The filter improves the risk profile relative to traditional oil-and-gas heaviness, but it does not eliminate equity-market volatility.

## Researching KLMN

Review Invesco's fact sheet and prospectus to understand the current holdings, the exact methodology MSCI uses to score climate performance, and the fund's expense ratio. Look at KLMN's year-to-date and longer-term performance versus the S&P 500 or a broad-market index to see how the climate tilt has traded off. Check the sector breakdown — is the fund truly still diversified, or is it effectively a tech fund with some clean energy? And if you are motivated by climate risk mitigation, read MSCI's detailed climate-methodology documentation to determine whether their approach matches your own views on what constitutes material environmental risk. Some investors will find the approach too permissive (because it still includes carbon-intensive companies); others will find it too strict (because it underweights energy companies during energy-supply crises). Matching the fund's philosophy to your own beliefs is essential.
