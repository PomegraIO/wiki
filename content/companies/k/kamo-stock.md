*Kensington Credit Opportunities ETF (KAMO) is a fixed-income fund that gives investors exposure to a diversified basket of credit [bonds](/bond/) — corporate debt issued by companies of varying credit quality — with the goal of generating income above what [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) offer while managing the risks of default and interest-rate movement.*

## Origins and strategy

Kensington Capital Partners launched KAMO to give individual investors direct access to a professionally managed portfolio of credit opportunities. The fund sits in the landscape of actively managed [fixed-income ETFs](/fixed-income-etf/) — a newer category that blurs the line between traditional index-tracking exchange-traded funds and actively managed [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/). KAMO does not simply hold a static index of bonds; instead, the Kensington team actively selects which credit instruments to own, adjusting the portfolio as market conditions and [credit cycles](/credit-cycle/) shift.

The fund's core thesis is that credit markets offer yield-hungry investors an opportunity to earn returns above the [risk-free rate](/risk-free-rate/) by holding debt issued by companies whose risk has been priced fairly or even mispriced by markets. Unlike equity investors, who hold a claim on a company's future earnings, credit investors hold a contractual obligation to be paid back — principal plus interest — at stated dates. That priority in the capital structure is the reason bonds typically deliver lower returns than stocks, but it is also why they are the natural home for investors who prefer income over the speculative upside that equity prices offer.

## What KAMO actually holds

The fund constructs a diversified mix of [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) across the credit-quality spectrum. This includes [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/) — those issued by companies with minimal default risk — and [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/), sometimes called [junk bonds](/junk-bond/), issued by companies with weaker [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/) or higher leverage. The specific allocation shifts as market conditions change. When credit conditions are benign and spreads are tight, the fund might hold more investment-grade bonds and accept lower yields. When the economy weakens and spreads widen, the same portfolio manager might move into higher-quality names or adjust duration.

KAMO's approach is sector-agnostic by design. Rather than betting on energy stocks or technology, as an equity fund would, KAMO instead owns debt across multiple industries — energy companies, technology firms, consumer goods, health care, and financial institutions all appear on its holdings list. This [diversification](/diversification/) lowers the risk that a single sector downturn will severely damage the portfolio.

The fund also manages interest-rate duration — the sensitivity of bond prices to changes in Treasury yields. If rates rise, bond prices fall, and funds with longer duration (higher sensitivity) suffer larger losses. KAMO's managers adjust the fund's duration stance based on their outlook for the interest-rate environment. In a falling-rate environment they might stretch duration to capture price appreciation. When rates are rising they might shorten it to insulate the portfolio from further losses.

## Income, yield, and trading

KAMO trades on [stock exchanges](/stock-exchange/) alongside equities, which means investors can buy and sell shares at market prices throughout the day — a convenience index-tracking bond mutual funds do not offer. The fund holds a basket of bonds that themselves trade infrequently (corporate bonds are far less liquid than stocks), so the ETF structure allows the fund issuer to create and redeem shares at [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), absorbing retail demand without disrupting the underlying bond holdings.

The fund yields income in two forms. Interest payments from the bonds held in the portfolio are passed through to shareholders, either reinvested or paid out depending on the investor's choice. Capital gains or losses may also arise if the fund sells bonds at prices different from their purchase price or if bond prices appreciate as yields fall.

## Risks and the credit cycle

KAMO's returns are tied intimately to credit conditions — the willingness of lenders to extend credit and the ability of borrowers to service debt. In a strong economy with low [default rates](/default-rate/), the fund's high-yield bonds may outperform, because the extra yield investors demanded for holding them proves unnecessary. In a [recession](/recession/) or economic shock, the opposite happens: defaults rise, high-yield spreads widen, and the fund's value can fall sharply. This means KAMO is procyclical — it performs best when the economy is healthy and worst when it weakens, precisely when many investors most need their portfolio to hold value.

[Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) is the second major threat. If the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises rates and Treasury yields climb, the bonds KAMO holds fall in price, because buyers would rather own newly issued bonds paying higher rates. The longer the average maturity of KAMO's holdings, the more it will suffer from rising rates. Conversely, falling rates boost bond prices, which is why [bond ETFs](/bond-etf/) have historically offered diversification value in equity-heavy portfolios — they rally when stocks fall and the Fed cuts rates in response.

Credit selection risk — the chance that the fund's managers misjudge individual bond credits or sector shifts — is also present. An [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) that bets heavily on a particular issuer or sector can outperform peers if that bet is correct, but can also underperform if it is wrong.

## Who holds KAMO and why

The fund appeals to income-seeking investors who want exposure to the higher yields credit bonds offer but lack the expertise or scale to build a diversified portfolio on their own. It is also a tool for diversifying within the fixed-income portion of a broader portfolio — an investor might pair Treasury-heavy holdings with KAMO to add credit exposure without owning equities.

## Researching KAMO

To understand this fund, start with Kensington's fact sheet and prospectus, which detail the fund's investment objective, holdings, [performance, fees](/performance-fee/), and risks. The fund's holdings should list the bonds it owns, updated regularly. Pay attention to the average credit quality (the percentage in investment-grade versus high-yield), the average maturity, and the yield-to-maturity of the overall portfolio. Watch how the fund performs relative to peers — other credit-focused ETFs and actively managed bond funds — over multiple [market cycles](/market-cycle/). Performance in rising-rate environments and in recessions reveals how well the management team navigates risk. Finally, compare the fund's expenses to alternatives; actively managed bond funds charge higher fees than passive index funds, and those fees directly reduce returns, so they must be earned through outperformance for the investor to come out ahead.
