*The **Harbor AlphaEdge Small Cap Earners ETF (EBIT)** is a rules-based equity fund that selects and weights U.S. small-cap companies by their profitability, rather than by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) or equal weight. It combines small-cap exposure with a quality screen: only businesses that are earning money participate.*

## The Opportunity in Small-Cap Profitability

Small-cap stocks are generally riskier and less liquid than large caps, but they offer higher growth potential and lower valuations. The problem is that small caps also include many unprofitable or marginally profitable businesses, which adds [idiosyncratic risk](/idiosyncratic-risk/) without compensating return. EBIT takes a different approach: it defines the universe as all small-cap companies with positive earnings, then weights them by the magnitude of those earnings.

The logic is straightforward: a small company generating $10 million in profit is meaningfully different from one generating $1 million. An earnings-weighted index gives more portfolio weight to the stronger earner, concentrating on profitable compounders rather than speculative stories.

## How the Index Works

The index begins with all companies in the Russell 2000, the broad small-cap benchmark. It applies a profitability filter: a company must have positive earnings in the most recent [fiscal year](/fiscal-year-definition/) and the preceding quarter to qualify. That screen immediately eliminates many cyclical, struggling, and loss-making names—roughly 20–30% of the Russell 2000 in a typical year.

The remaining companies are then weighted by earnings (specifically, net income). This creates a quality tilt: businesses with stronger bottom-line performance represent larger portfolio positions. Rebalancing occurs quarterly, capturing earnings changes and removing companies that fall out of profitability.

## Why Small Caps and Why This Way?

Small-cap value has underperformed large-cap growth stocks for much of the past decade, but it offers higher long-term expected returns for investors willing to accept volatility. Weighting by earnings instead of market cap biases the fund toward profitable, self-sustaining businesses—the kinds that tend to compound wealth over years rather than boom-bust cycles.

The trade-off is concentration. A profitability screen and earnings weighting naturally narrow the opportunity set. The fund holds fewer names than the full Russell 2000 and assigns larger weights to the most profitable names, amplifying both upside and downside.

## What This Is Not

EBIT is not a large-cap quality fund disguised as small-cap. It is not a [growth fund](/growth-fund/); many profitable small caps are mature, slow-growers. It is not a deep-value strategy; profitable companies trade at reasonable valuations, not distressed ones. And it is not a volatility play: profitability screens out the most speculative small caps, reducing risk relative to the broad Russell 2000.

## Costs and Liquidity

The 0.29% [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is competitive for a specialized small-cap strategy. The fund is young, launched in July 2024, so assets are still modest. This means the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) may be wider than larger peers during volatile trading, and the fund may have limited historical performance data to evaluate. For investors comfortable with that youth, the rational index design and low cost are attractive.

## How to Research EBIT

Start with the fund's prospectus to understand the earnings criteria and weighting methodology. Compare EBIT's returns and characteristics to the Russell 2000 and to other small-cap value or quality alternatives like SLYV or IWN over whatever historical period is available. Check the composition: are the top 10 holdings household names or lesser-known compounders? Examine turnover to understand how often the earnings screen boots out and brings in companies; high turnover increases trading costs and tax inefficiency.

Test your conviction in the thesis: do you believe profitable small-cap companies outperform unprofitable ones over a multi-year horizon? And are you comfortable with small-cap volatility? If yes, this is a focused way to express that view. If you prefer broad small-cap exposure without profitability screening, the Russell 2000 itself remains the simplest choice.
