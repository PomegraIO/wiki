*The **ProShares Short MSCI EAFE** (ticker EFZ) is an inverse exchange-traded fund—a financial instrument designed to move in the opposite direction of the MSCI EAFE index, which tracks large-cap developed-market stocks outside North America. It is a tactical tool, not an investment vehicle, and works through daily rebalancing mechanics that make it unsuitable for buy-and-hold investors.*

## Structure and mechanics

EFZ achieves its inverse exposure through derivatives — primarily swap contracts and futures — that effectively short the underlying MSCI EAFE index. When the index falls one percent, EFZ rises approximately one percent. When the index gains one percent, EFZ declines one percent. The fund is designed to reset daily, meaning it rebalances its derivative positions at the end of each trading session to maintain exact -1x exposure for the following day.

This daily-reset structure is crucial. It means EFZ works for tactical moves lasting hours or days, but compounds unpredictably over longer periods. If an index rises 10 percent in Month One and falls 10 percent in Month Two (returning to the starting point), an inverse fund with daily rebalancing will not return to the starting point — it will have lost money due to the mathematics of compounding and reinvestment at different prices. Retail investors who buy EFZ intending to hold it for a year often discover they have lost money even if the underlying index was flat, because daily reset decay accumulates.

## Who uses it and why

EFZ exists primarily for [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/), professional traders, and sophisticated investors who want to reduce their exposure to developed-market equities for a short period without selling their actual holdings. A portfolio manager who is concerned about European or Japanese weakness for the next two weeks can take a short position in EFZ, hedging her developed-market exposure, then close the position once the concern passes. The fund also attracts tactical traders betting on a near-term selloff in developed markets — investors making a conscious short-term directional bet rather than a long-term allocation.

Some individual investors buy EFZ for protection during market turmoil, as a small hedge against a larger long position in developed-market stocks. This makes sense only if the [holding period](/holding-period/) is measured in weeks; holding it through a long [bull market](/bull-market/) will erode the position steadily.

## Costs and the expense ratio

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) for EFZ is higher than for a traditional passive [index fund](/index-fund/) tracking EAFE, typically in the range of 0.4 to 0.5 percent annually. This reflects the cost of maintaining derivative positions and the daily rebalancing. The fund is actively managed in the sense that the ProShares team is constantly buying and selling futures and swaps to maintain the daily -1x target, unlike a buy-and-hold index fund. For a trade lasting days, that friction is often acceptable; for a position held for years, it compounds into significant drag.

Trading costs matter too. EFZ trades on US exchanges with tight spreads (the bid-ask gap is usually small), so executing a trade is generally cheap. But holding costs mount quickly if the fund is held for extended periods.

## The real risk: volatility decay

The signature danger of EFZ is volatility decay. In a choppy market with large daily swings, the index might move up and down frequently without net directional change, yet EFZ will decline steadily because the daily resets lock in losses on down days and fail to capture full gains on up days. This mathematical drag is not a malfunction — it is inherent to how the fund works. An inverse fund with daily rebalancing will always underperform a perfect inverse of the index over multi-day periods.

There is also [basis risk](/basis-risk/): the derivatives that drive EFZ might not track the underlying index perfectly, particularly if the derivatives market becomes dislocated or illiquid. And there is leverage risk — the fund is effectively leveraged short, meaning a sharp, sustained rally in the index can produce outsized losses.

## How traders monitor EFZ

Users treat EFZ as a tactical position, not an investment. A trader might set an entry price, a profit target, and a stop-loss level — much like a short stock position. Some use it to hedge a portion of a developed-market allocation for a defined period. The fund's daily [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) (NAV) — what the underlying holdings are worth — is published constantly, and discrepancies between the trading price and NAV occasionally appear, creating opportunities for arbitrage (though institutional traders, not retail investors, typically exploit these).

Monitoring the fund means watching the underlying MSCI EAFE index hourly or daily, tracking the fund's price moves minute by minute if executing a tactical trade, and understanding the composition of the index — which stocks and sectors it holds and how a particular news event (earnings, [central bank](/central-bank/) action, geopolitical news) will likely affect them. EFZ is a tool for the alert trader, not a set-and-forget holding.
