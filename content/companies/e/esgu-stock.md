*The iShares ESG Aware MSCI USA ETF (ESGU) applies environmental and governance filters to broad U.S. stock-market exposure, giving investors a way to own a diversified slice of American corporations while tilting away from fossil-fuel extractors, poor-governance companies, and firms facing material social controversies.*

## How the screening shapes the portfolio

ESGU starts with the universe of large and mid-cap U.S. stocks—the backbone of the American economy—then systematically screens out or underweights companies that score poorly on ESG factors. The index excludes fossil-fuel producers, underweights companies with weak board independence, reduces exposure to firms with active labor controversies, and favors companies with strong environmental management practices.

The practical effect is that ESGU looks much like the S&P 500 at first glance. Technology, healthcare, and financials dominate, and mega-cap names like [Microsoft](/msft-stock/), [Apple](/aapl-stock/), JPMorgan, and [Nvidia](/nvda-stock/) occupy large positions. But closer inspection reveals that ESGU carries less exposure to energy companies than a market-cap-weighted index would, slightly favours companies with independent boards, and avoids a handful of firms involved in major controversies. For someone used to owning a plain total-market US index, the transition to ESGU would feel familiar—same universe, same trading patterns, slightly different composition at the edges.

MSCI, the [index provider](/index-provider/), computes ESG scores for each company based on environmental practices, labour relations, governance structures, and product impact. These scores are mechanistic and measurable—not subjective judgements by fund managers, but systematic application of published criteria. A company's ESG score either meets the threshold for inclusion or underweighting, or it does not.

## The consequences of fossil-fuel reduction

The most visible impact of ESGU's screening is the reduced weight on energy companies. A standard market-cap-weighted index might allocate 3-5 percent to energy sector stocks, including oil majors, coal producers, and utility companies. ESGU's fossil-fuel exclusion reduces that exposure considerably, though utilities and renewable-energy companies remain represented.

This exclusion creates two opposite risks. In periods when oil prices spike and energy stocks surge, ESGU's underweight will drag returns. A major geopolitical disruption or supply crisis favours holders of unscreened broad indexes, which capture the energy rally. Conversely, in periods when energy companies face regulatory pressure, stranded-asset write-downs, or structural decline due to energy-transition trends, ESGU's avoided exposure becomes an advantage.

The exclusion also means ESGU holds slightly more capital-goods, technology, and healthcare stocks than a plain index. These sectors have benefited from the broad market trends of recent decades—digital transformation, automation, and aging-population healthcare demand. Overweighting them by default could be fortuitous or could set up underperformance if those trends reverse.

## Performance and the value question

A recurring question for ESG-screened funds is whether the screening adds value or costs performance. The evidence is mixed. Some studies find that ESG-favoring companies outperform over time, either because better governance predicts better management or because ESG leaders attract a growing pool of capital. Other studies find no persistent advantage. Most research suggests the performance difference, if any, is small compared to the volatility of the broad [stock market](/stock-market/).

ESGU's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is low but modestly higher than the plainest US equity index ETFs, reflecting the work of applying ESG screens. Over decades, this cost differential is material, though the fee itself is not dramatic.

## Risks and suitability

ESGU is a US stock fund. It rises and falls with American corporate earnings, [interest rates](/interest-rate/), and investor sentiment. A broad [recession](/recession/), a rate shock, or a sustained [bear market](/bear-market/) in US equities hits ESGU the same way it hits any large-cap US fund. The ESG screen does not protect against [market risk](/market-risk/); it redistributes exposure within the market.

The screening does reduce certain concentrations: you avoid fossil-fuel company blowups, you miss corruption scandals at poorly governed firms, and you sidestep controversies that sometimes drag single-company stocks down. But you also miss any outperformance those excluded companies might deliver. This is a values-alignment trade, not a risk-mitigation strategy.

ESGU appeals to long-term holders who want US equity exposure aligned with environmental and governance preferences. It is neither cheaper nor inherently better than unscreened broad indexes; it is a choice to apply ESG filters to a familiar benchmark.

## How to research and monitor ESGU

Begin with the prospectus and factsheet detailing which ESG metrics drive inclusion and exclusion. Review the fund's top 20 holdings and sector breakdown, comparing them directly against a plain [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/). The differences reveal where the ESG screen has concentrated or lightened exposure. Pay special attention to the energy sector—compare ESGU's weight there to a standard index to quantify the fossil-fuel exclusion impact.

Compare ESGU's total return to an unscreened US large-cap index over rolling 3-, 5-, and 10-year periods. Short-term differences are noise; longer periods reveal whether ESG screening has been a benefit, a cost, or immaterial. Monitor changes to MSCI's ESG methodology in the prospectus; index changes can occasionally affect performance, though usually marginally. Finally, consider whether the ESG approach aligns with your own values and investment philosophy—that alignment is often worth more to long-term holders than small performance differentials.
