The Harbor Osmosis Emerging Markets Resource Efficient ETF pursues a specific thesis in high-growth but turbulent markets. Rather than owning all emerging-market equities, EFFE selects companies from developing economies that have demonstrated efficient use of resources — water, energy, materials — relative to their output. The fund holds manufacturers, utilities, financials, and others across emerging geographies, but only those that score well on environmental resource-efficiency metrics.

The index that EFFE tracks applies quantitative screens to the broad emerging-market opportunity set. Companies are evaluated on measures like carbon intensity (greenhouse-gas emissions per unit of economic output), water use per unit of production, waste management, and material efficiency. The fund then holds a portfolio weighted to this emerging-markets subset, concentrating on firms that use fewer inputs to produce their output or that have made investments in cleaner processes.

The logic is twofold. First, emerging economies are growing faster than developed ones and offer higher equity returns over long periods — that is the basic emerging-market story. But rapid growth often means rapid resource consumption and pollution. Companies in emerging markets that manage that growth efficiently — by choice or because they operate in jurisdictions with stricter standards — face lower regulatory risk and may be better positioned for a world of carbon pricing or resource scarcity. Second, efficiency often correlates with better management and capital discipline. Firms that wring more output from fewer inputs tend to be operationally superior, a characteristic that compounds into stock returns.

Harbor Capital Advisors, the fund's sponsor, is part of [Affiliated Managers](/amg-stock/) Group and specializes in factor-based and thematic investing. EFFE is a straightforward ETF — no leverage, no inverse mechanics — that holds actual equities and trades with the volume and simplicity of any emerging-market fund. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is in the range of broad emerging-market ETFs, though slightly higher than the cheapest plain-vanilla emerging-market vehicles, reflecting the resource-efficiency screening process.

The risks are substantial. Emerging markets themselves are volatile — driven by commodity cycles, [capital flows](/capital-flows/), political uncertainty, and currency swings. A crisis in China, a [central bank](/central-bank/) rate shock in India, or a political surprise in Brazil can whipsaw emerging-market funds. EFFE adds an overlay: by selecting for efficiency, the fund may exclude fast-growing but inefficient manufacturers, or young tech companies that have not yet optimized. In periods when emerging-market growth depends on those less-efficient players, EFFE lags. Currency exposure is automatic and meaningful — emerging-market currencies are far more volatile than developed-world ones, and they often weaken sharply in crisis. A portfolio in EFFE faces both equity-market swings and currency swings, and they often amplify each other.

The efficiency screen also depends on the data. Resource-efficiency metrics are imperfect — companies report inconsistently across jurisdictions, greenwashing exists, and methodology changes can shift which companies qualify. An update to carbon-accounting standards or a change to how water use is measured can reshuffle the portfolio.

Researching EFFE starts with the prospectus, which details the screening methodology and the list of metrics used to rank resource efficiency. The fact sheet shows the current holdings, geographic breakdown, and sector makeup, revealing what emerging economies and industries are over- or underweighted. Comparing EFFE's returns to a plain emerging-market index (such as the [MSCI Emerging Markets Index](/msci-emerging-markets-index/)) over various time periods shows whether the efficiency filter has paid off or become a drag. Volatility and [drawdown analysis](/drawdown-analysis/) reveals whether EFFE swings more or less than the market, and whether it holds up or crumbles in emerging-market crises.
