*EQUITY BANCSHARES INC (EQBK), a Colorado-chartered bank holding company, is a regional community bank whose deposits and loan portfolio reflect the economic health and real estate cycles of the Mountain West. Reading EQBK's 10-K requires focus on loan composition, provisions for credit losses, and deposit stickiness in competitive markets.*

## Opening the 10-K: Loan Portfolio Breakdown

EQBK's asset side begins with the loan portfolio, which is disclosed in a detailed schedule in the 10-K notes. Look for the breakdown by type: [commercial real estate](/commercial-real-estate/), commercial and industrial (C&I), consumer, agriculture, and owner-occupied real estate. Each category carries different loss rates. Owner-occupied real estate (OOER) secured by the borrower's home or business is less volatile than [acquisition](/acquisition/) and development (AD) loans. If EQBK has concentrated its portfolio in construction lending, it has higher cyclical risk. If it is tilted toward OOER and consumer, it is more defensive. The loan portfolio breakdown is not a buried fact—it is Table 1 or Table 2 in the notes to consolidated financial statements, and it is the key to understanding credit risk.

## Loan Loss Reserves and the Allowance for Credit Losses (ACL)

Banks set aside reserves for expected credit losses, called the Allowance for Credit Losses (ACL) under current accounting standards. EQBK's ACL is disclosed as a deduction from gross loans in the balance sheet, and the 10-K's notes explain the methodology. Key questions: Is the ACL rising or falling relative to total loans? Is the company taking larger provisions (loan loss expense) than peers, signaling deteriorating credit quality, or smaller provisions, signaling confidence? Look at "[provision for credit losses](/provision-for-credit-losses/)" in the [income statement](/income-statement/) across the prior three years. Rising provisions combined with falling loan loss coverage ratios (ACL / loans) is a red flag; declining provisions with stable coverage suggests stable credit conditions. The MD&A will explain the economic assumptions driving the ACL—[unemployment rate](/unemployment-rate/) expectations, real estate price trends. If EQBK raised its unemployment or home price assumptions, that's a credit tightening signal.

## Delinquency and Nonaccrual Loans: Early Warning

The 10-K discloses loans 30, 60, and 90+ days past due (delinquencies) and loans on which interest accrual has stopped (nonaccrual loans). These are your early warning signals. If delinquencies are rising trend-over-trend, borrowers are under stress. If nonaccrual loans are growing, the bank is moving toward charge-offs. EQBK will report these in both absolute dollars and as a percentage of total loans. A [delinquency](/delinquency/) ratio of 0.5% is typical for banks in good times; 1–2% signals stress. The notes will also show charged-off loans (loans written off as uncollectible) by category. A sudden increase in charge-offs in the real estate category indicates distressed borrowers and portfolio deterioration.

## Net Interest Margin and the Deposit Franchise

Banks earn money primarily from the spread between the [interest rate](/interest-rate/) they pay on deposits and the rate they charge on loans. This spread is called the net interest margin (NIM). EQBK's NIM is disclosed in management's discussion and appears in the [consolidated statements](/consolidated-statements/) of income. A healthy regional bank typically runs a NIM of 3–4.5%; if EQBK's NIM is compressing (falling), it signals either deposit competition (forced to pay higher rates to retain deposits) or falling loan yields (rates on new loans are lower than maturing loans). This matters because a shrinking NIM eventually forces difficult choices: cut expenses, grow volume, or sacrifice profitability. The 10-K will explain the composition of deposits by type: transaction accounts (checking, savings), money market, and time deposits (CDs). Transaction accounts are stickier (less price-sensitive); CDs are rate-chasers. If EQBK's CD rates spiked in recent quarters, management is fighting to retain deposits—a sign of competitive pressure or deposit flight risk.

## Interest Rate Risk and Asset-Liability Management

Banks are exposed to interest rate risk: if rates rise, the value of their loan portfolio (made at older, lower rates) declines in economic terms, while the cost of deposits may rise. EQBK manages this through the shape of its balance sheet. If it has more fixed-rate loans than deposits, rising rates hurt. If it has floating-rate loans and deposits, rising rates help. The 10-K includes a table showing the repricing schedule of assets and liabilities—which loans reset in the next 12 months, which in 2–5 years. A bank with a large ladder of assets and liabilities repricing together is hedged; one with a cliff is exposed. EQBK's management discussion will explain how it sizes its duration mismatch deliberately. The key is whether rate risk is managed intentionally or is a byproduct of the loan and deposit mix.

## Profitability Decomposition: Spread, Volume, and Operating Leverage

EQBK's net income flows from three drivers: (1) the spread on loans and deposits (NIM times earning assets); (2) non-interest income (fees, trading gains, [loan origination fees](/loan-origination-fees/)); and (3) operating expenses. To read the 10-K intelligently, decompose three years of net income into these parts. If net income is growing, ask: is it because NIM is widening (revenue strength) or because expenses are falling (cost discipline)? Is the company making larger gains on securities sales (one-time), or is operating income steady? The income statement shows these clearly: net interest income (NIM × earning assets), provision for credit losses (subtracted as a loss), non-interest income (fees and gains), and operating expenses. EQBK's operating leverage—the degree to which revenue growth flows to the bottom line—hinges on whether it can grow volume without proportional cost growth. A regional bank with flat loan growth and flat expenses but falling NIM is in trouble. One with rising loan volume at flat [expense ratios](/expense-ratio/) is improving.

## The Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) Ratio and Regulatory Capital

EQBK, as a bank holding company above a certain size, reports regulatory capital ratios: Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1), [Tier 1 capital](/tier-1-capital/), and total capital. These appear in the 10-K's risk section and are expressed as percentages of risk-weighted assets. Regulators set minimum thresholds; EQBK must maintain CET1 above 4.5% (plus buffers for well-capitalized institutions). If EQBK's ratio is tight, it has limited cushion to absorb losses or pay dividends. If it is strong (8%+), the bank is overcapitalized and could return capital to shareholders. This ratio is a constraint on strategy. A bank below minimum feels regulatory pressure; one well above it can grow or repay shareholders.

## Dividends and Capital Return Discipline

Many regional banks return capital via dividends and buybacks, constrained by regulatory approval and capital ratios. EQBK's [cash flow statement](/cash-flow-statement/) shows dividends paid and shares repurchased. Look for consistency: does the company raise the dividend annually, signaling confidence, or freeze it during uncertain periods? Buyback programs announced in press releases may or may not be executed in full. The 10-K will note any capital return programs and whether they are subject to regulatory approval. A bank that suspends or cuts dividends is signaling deteriorating capital position or credit quality.
