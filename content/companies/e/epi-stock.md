*The **WisdomTree India Earnings Fund** — trading as EPI on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) — is an exchange-traded fund that captures India's publicly listed equity market through an unusual lens: rather than equal-weighting all companies or weighting them by market value, it favors the companies that actually pay [dividends](/dividend/) to shareholders, and sizes those positions based on how much dividend each company pays.*

## Why India matters to global investors, and why dividend-weighting

India has emerged as one of the world's most compelling long-term growth stories. With a population exceeding 1.4 billion, a young workforce, rising digital adoption, and economic growth rates that consistently outpace developed markets, India offers investors exposure to a large developing economy where rising incomes and consumer spending are driving corporate profits. EPI is designed to tap that growth specifically through the lens of earnings — companies that are profitable enough and confident enough in their futures to return cash to shareholders as dividends.

The dividend-weighting approach is WisdomTree's signature methodological twist. In a traditional market-cap-weighted index like those used by most broad emerging-markets ETFs, the largest companies by total stock-market value get the largest weightings, which can mean overweighting unprofitable high-growth companies and underweighting mature, cash-generative ones. In EPI's dividend-weighted approach, a company's weight in the fund is determined by the dollar amount of dividends it pays, not by its [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/). This has two effects: it tends to tilt the portfolio toward established, profitable businesses with returning cash flows rather than growth-at-any-cost stories, and it creates a small yield boost because the fund is mechanically overweighting the higher-dividend payers.

## How the dividend-weighted index works in practice

EPI selects the largest and mid-cap Indian companies and then rebalances their weights annually based on the dividends they paid over the prior year. A bank or utility that paid $100 million in dividends will occupy a larger slice of the fund than a software company of similar market cap that paid $10 million in dividends, all else equal. As companies change their dividend policies — cutting dividends, raising them, or initiating them for the first time — their weightings in the fund shift accordingly, which drives some turnover and transaction costs but nowhere near as much as an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) would incur.

This approach naturally biases EPI toward sectors that pay dividends: Indian financials, utilities, consumer staples, and industrial companies tend to be steady dividend payers, while India's fast-growing tech and software companies often retain earnings rather than distributing them. For investors seeking classic Indian growth — tech, business-process outsourcing, digital disruption — EPI is not the optimal vehicle; it will likely underweight those sectors. For investors seeking profitable Indian businesses returning cash, the methodology is a natural fit.

## India-specific risks and why they matter

India presents unique opportunities but also distinct risks. [Currency risk](/currency-risk/) is fundamental: most Indian stocks trade in rupees, and the rupee regularly appreciates or depreciates against the dollar in ways that can amplify or dampen returns for a dollar-based investor. A 10 percent decline in the rupee turns a positive-return year into a flat one for an unhedged investor, even if the stocks themselves perform well.

Political and regulatory uncertainty is another persistent feature of emerging-market India. Policy shifts on taxation, foreign investor restrictions, or capital controls can rapidly affect the attractiveness of Indian equities. The Indian government's role in the economy is also more interventionist than in developed markets, meaning decisions about [interest rates](/interest-rate/), [inflation](/inflation/) targets, and infrastructure spending have outsized effects on investor returns.

Market liquidity in individual Indian stocks is often thinner than in developed markets, which can widen the spreads investors encounter during times of stress. And [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) is relevant: India's economy and [stock market](/stock-market/) are dominated by a relatively small number of large business conglomerates and banks, which means even a diversified fund like EPI has meaningful exposure to the decisions and fortunes of a handful of enormous companies.

The dividend-weighting approach itself introduces a specific risk: if the companies in the fund sharply cut their dividends during an economic downturn (as sometimes happens), the fund's composition will shift substantially, potentially locking in losses for investors who bought before the cuts. The approach assumes dividends are stable or growing, an assumption that does not always hold.

## Trading and costs

EPI trades on the NASDAQ like any other US-listed ETF, giving US investors the ability to buy and sell during regular market hours without relying on Indian market hours. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) for the fund is relatively low, reflecting WisdomTree's indexing methodology, though it is slightly higher than a purely passive broad market index would be because of the annual rebalancing required to maintain dividend weights.

The [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) — the cost of buying and selling shares — varies with market conditions. In normal times the spread is tight, but during periods of high volatility or when risk appetite is low, the spread can widen. Indian economic or political shocks can trigger temporary disconnects between the fund's price and the value of its underlying holdings, creating temporary arbitrage opportunities or risks for traders.

## How to study EPI as an investment

Investors interested in EPI should start with WisdomTree's documentation of the fund's index methodology, which lays out the exact rules for selecting companies and weighting them by dividends. The fund's fact sheet provides the current holdings, [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/), expense ratio, and key metrics. The WisdomTree website also publishes the index components and their weights, which is useful for understanding the fund's current exposures.

Understanding India's macroeconomic backdrop is also essential. Track the Indian [central bank's](/central-bank/) policy on interest rates and inflation, the government's fiscal position, and any major regulatory changes affecting foreign investors or corporate taxation. Currency movements in the rupee matter separately from the stock performance itself, so monitoring rupee strength or weakness is important for projecting returns in dollar terms.

Finally, because EPI is tilted toward dividend-payers rather than growth, an investor should compare it to other India-focused ETFs that may use different index methodologies — some market-cap-weighted, some equally weighted — to decide which approach best matches their investment thesis and risk tolerance.
