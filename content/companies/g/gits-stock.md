*Global Interactive Technologies, Inc., ticker **GITS**, operates in the competitive and capital-hungry digital-platform space. Like many technology firms below mega-cap scale, GITS faces the ongoing puzzle of funding growth: whether to raise capital through debt (which requires stable cash flows and creditworthiness it may lack as a smaller player) or through equity (which dilutes ownership and requires convincing investors of long-term returns). The company's financial structure reflects this growth-versus-ownership tension.*

## Growth Capital and the Cost of Dilution

GITS, as a mid-cap or smaller technology company, likely relies primarily on equity raises to fund expansion. Debt financing is expensive for firms with unproven and volatile profitability; banks and bond investors demand either substantial [collateral](/balance-sheet/) or a track record of [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) and stable earnings. GITS, if still in a scaling phase, may lack both. Equity raises—through [common stock](/common-stock/) issuance or convertible securities—become the default path. Each equity round dilutes existing shareholders, transferring ownership percentage to new investors. A founder or early investor holding 10 million shares may drop to 8 million effective ownership after a secondary offering, even if share count remains constant in absolute terms.

The math is unforgiving. If GITS raises $10 million at a $50 million pre-money valuation, the company admits new equity equal to 16.7% of the post-round market cap. Original shareholders see their ownership stake shrink correspondingly. If the company later raises at a lower valuation (a "down round"), earlier investors face even steeper dilution and mark-to-market losses on paper. This creates pressure on management to time capital raises carefully and to hit growth milestones that justify a rising valuation path.

## Warrant Overhang and Convertible Complexity

Smaller technology firms, especially those with OTC listings and limited institutional following, often issue [warrants](/stock/) or convertible instruments to sweeten capital raises. A warrant grants the holder the right to buy common shares at a fixed price in the future—a call option on the company's stock. Convertible debt or preferred shares allow lenders or investors to convert their claim into equity under specified conditions. Both reduce [cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) available to the company (if warrants are exercised, diluting existing shareholders; if convertibles convert, same effect) and can create a ceiling on stock price appreciation. If GITS issued 1 million warrants at a $10 strike price and the stock rises toward $12, warrant holders have incentive to exercise, flooding the market with new shares and suppressing the stock further.

These instruments appear in the [10-K](/10-k/) footnotes and dilution calculations. Sophisticated investors and analysts compute "fully diluted" share count—shares outstanding plus shares issuable from all options, warrants, and convertibles—to understand the true [earnings per share](/earnings-per-share/) and [return on equity](/return-on-equity/). A company showing strong earnings per common share can appear weak on a fully diluted basis if warrant and option overhang is severe.

## Burn Rate and Cash Runway

Technology companies scaling to profitability often operate with negative cash flow—they spend on engineering, marketing, and infrastructure faster than they collect revenue. This "burn rate" is critical. If GITS has $5 million in cash and burns $1 million per quarter, it has five quarters of runway before the bank account is empty. Management must plan capital raises to land before cash is depleted, or the company faces a steep discounted raise or operational crisis. Detailed [10-K](/10-k/) filings include cash flow statements showing cash used in operations; regular monitoring of this metric tells investors whether the company is approaching a funding deadline.

Burn rate also disciplines product and go-to-market strategy. A high burn may be justified if revenue is growing exponentially and unit economics are improving—each dollar spent today yields multiple dollars of revenue tomorrow. A flat or declining revenue with high burn is a red flag, signaling the company is not finding a sustainable market or is executing poorly.

## Revenue Model and Profitability Path

GITS's capital structure depends partly on its revenue model. If the company sells enterprise software on long-term contracts with upfront payments, cash flow can eventually match or exceed earnings, reducing financing pressure. If it operates an advertising platform or takes commissions, revenue is thinner and more volatile. A subscription or SaaS model (Software-as-a-Service) generates recurring revenue with predictable churn, attractive to investors and conducive to higher valuations and debt financing. A transactional or project-based model creates unpredictability, pushing the company toward equity capital markets.

GITS's path to profitability shapes its capital structure choices. If management credibly projects operating profitability within three years, investors are more willing to support equity raises and lenders more willing to extend credit. If profitability is perpetually five years away, equity dilutes more sharply and debt becomes inaccessible, leaving the company vulnerable to market downturns and funding droughts.

## Institutional Ownership and Shareholder Rights

OTC-traded smaller technology firms often have limited institutional ownership—[mutual funds](/mutual-fund/), [index funds](/index-fund/), and institutional [ETFs](/etf/) may avoid them due to size, liquidity, or regulatory constraints. This creates a more fragmented shareholder base, often including retail investors, former employees, and insiders. Shareholder governance is looser; control thresholds are lower; hostile takeovers or activist campaigns are less common but sometimes occur.

Without strong institutional backing, GITS has less leverage in capital raises. A venture capital firm or growth equity investor can demand board seats, preferential terms, and control provisions. The company's founding shareholders may be forced to accept significant dilution and loss of control in exchange for growth capital. The [10-K](/10-k/) discloses beneficial ownership of shares exceeding 5%, revealing the power structure and any concentration risk if a single shareholder or affiliated group could block important decisions.

## Debt Capacity and Credit Risk

If GITS eventually achieves consistent profitability and strong free cash flow, debt becomes viable and attractive. Bank lines of credit and term loans carry lower cost of capital than equity and do not dilute ownership. However, until that transition occurs, GITS likely has minimal debt. Lenders demand collateral or cash flow coverage; an unprofitable software company has neither. Any debt GITS carries is probably convertible (with a warrant or conversion feature), a liability structure that blurs the line between debt and equity but allows the company to raise capital when straight debt is unavailable.

A convertible note that GITS issued might convert to equity if the company reaches a $200 million valuation in five years, or if a new equity round occurs at $100 million or higher. The note holder accepts a lower interest rate (say, 5% annually) because conversion upside compensates for lower current yield. This structure buys the company time but defers the dilution question; equity dilution arrives when conversion is triggered.

## Strategic Exit and Shareholder Return

GITS's capital structure is shaped partly by exit expectations. If management and board anticipate an acquisition by a larger technology firm, the company can afford higher burn rates and prolonged pre-profitability because the acquirer will value the product, user base, and team. If an IPO to a larger exchange is the goal, institutional investors will demand clearer profitability and governance. If the founders expect to harvest value through dividends and gradual exits, the capital structure must eventually yield consistent cash returns—requiring a shift to profitability and capital discipline that may constrain growth.

The [10-K](/10-k/) often hints at strategy through disclosure of related-party transactions, management compensation structure, and whether the company has filed any registration statements for future offerings. Clues to capital strategy also appear in the balance sheet trends and cash flow trajectory over several years.
