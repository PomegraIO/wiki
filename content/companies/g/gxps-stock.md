*Consumer staples—the companies that sell groceries, soap, diapers, cigarettes, and beer—are the ballast of equity portfolios. They are rarely exciting, but they are persistently profitable and they pay [dividends](/dividend/) that feel generous when measured against their modest price-to-earnings multiples. **Global X PureCap MSCI Consumer Staples ETF (GXPS)** tracks a subset of those companies using a distinctive approach: instead of the market-cap weighting that dominates most indices, it applies "pure capitalization" weighting, a method that adjusts how stocks are included to avoid the artificial tilt toward tiny illiquid names that can distort traditional cap weighting.*

## What the index holds and why

The MSCI USA PureCap Consumer Staples Index identifies publicly traded companies in the consumer staples sector—food, beverage, household products, personal care, and tobacco—then weights them by a modified form of [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/). The index is not a passthrough to the largest staples names; it is a curated list of around 70 to 100 companies that meet liquidity and capitalization thresholds set by MSCI. Within that universe, pure capitalization weighting means the fund holds each company in proportion to its market value, but with a floor and ceiling that prevent the largest names from dominating and the smallest from vanishing.

This weighting scheme replicates how a portfolio manager might construct a staples exposure manually—holding the majors like [Procter & Gamble](/pg-stock/), Mondelez, and Nestlé, but avoiding the massive over-concentration in the single largest company that can happen in a free-floating market-cap index. It also avoids the tiny-cap creep that pure market-cap indexing can introduce, where a company with a billion-dollar market cap can drift into or out of the index based on a daily price move.

Consumer staples are defensive: when the economy slows, people still buy toilet paper, breakfast cereal, and shampoo. During [recessions](/recession/), staples stocks often outperform discretionary stocks because their earnings are more predictable. They also tend to have pricing power (a brand of soap or soft drink can raise prices when input costs rise) and strong [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/), which they return to shareholders as dividends.

## The pure capitalization structure

Pure capitalization weighting differs subtly from the simple market-cap weighting used in most broad indices. In a standard market-cap index, a company's weight is determined purely by its stock price times the number of shares outstanding. Over time, if a company's stock soars, it gets bigger; if the company shrinks, it gets smaller.

Pure capitalization introduces brackets: the index will have a maximum weight for any single company (often around 3–5% of the index) and may have minimum weights for companies below a certain size. This prevents the mega-cap dominance problem (where the index would be 20% Procter & Gamble alone) and the micro-cap inclusion problem (where illiquid tiny companies can sneak in and out and cause tracking error). For investors in GXPS, this means slightly better [diversification](/diversification/) than a pure market-cap staples index and a more stable portfolio that does not lurch toward concentration or liquidity crises.

## Dividend exposure and income orientation

Staples companies are mature, profitable, and capital-efficient; they generate cash faster than they can reinvest it usefully. Rather than plough excess profits into R&D or [acquisitions](/acquisition/), they return it to shareholders as dividends. This makes GXPS a magnet for income-oriented investors—those in retirement, those seeking regular cash flow, or those who reinvest dividends for compounding.

The [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) on the index typically ranges from 2.5% to 4%, which is more generous than the overall [stock market](/stock-market/) but less than high-yield strategies. The dividends are generally qualified (eligible for favorable tax treatment in the United States) because they come from large, established corporations, not from real-estate investment trusts or other special structures with different tax rules.

For long-term holders, dividend income is a material part of total return over decades, even though it is often overlooked in discussions of cap gains.

## Trading and liquidity considerations

GXPS trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) exchange and benefits from Global X's distribution network and the overall liquidity in ETF markets. Because the underlying index is made up of large, well-traded companies (Procter & Gamble, Colgate-Palmolive, Mondelez), the ETF itself tends to be liquid, with tight [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and high trading volume. Investors can buy and sell shares intraday at prices close to the underlying index value.

The fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) is calculated daily based on the close of trading in the companies held, and the ETF's market price stays tightly aligned with that value because [authorized participants](/authorized-participant/) can create or redeem shares in bulk.

## Risks and sensitivities

Staples stocks are defensive, but they are not risk-free. The sector is sensitive to consumer confidence: if unemployment spikes or confidence collapses, even staples companies see demand softness. Currency movements affect companies with international operations (most large staples firms do). Regulatory risks exist in tobacco and food safety. Commodity price [inflation](/inflation/) (cocoa, palm oil, [natural gas](/natural-gas/)) can squeeze margins if companies cannot raise prices fast enough.

The pure capitalization structure, while reducing extreme concentration, also means GXPS will not capture any outperformance if the smallest staples companies—those just above the index's capitalization floor—deliver outsized gains. In a [bull market](/bull-market/) for small-cap staples, a pure market-cap competitor might outperform GXPS slightly.

Dividend risk is often overlooked: if a major holding cuts its dividend due to financial stress (or changes in payout policy), the yield investors expect from the fund will fall. Dividend cuts are rare in staples but not impossible, especially if [interest rates](/interest-rate/) or capital requirements shift.

## How to research GXPS

Start with the fund's prospectus and the MSCI USA PureCap Consumer Staples Index methodology, which explain the weighting scheme and the criteria for inclusion. The fund's fact sheet details the current top holdings, the dividend yield, the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), and the tracking error (how closely the ETF follows its index). 

Monitor the sector's fundamentals through company earnings reports for the largest holdings (a Procter & Gamble or Nestlé earnings miss will move the entire fund). Watch for major dividend announcements, cuts, or increases in the holdings. Track inflation and interest-rate expectations, because rising rates can reduce the appeal of dividend-paying staples stocks relative to [bonds](/bond/).

The fund's [annual report](/10-k/) discloses any corporate actions in the holdings, [proxy voting](/proxy-voting/), and performance against the index. Because GXPS is a passive ETF tracking a specific index, it is best understood as a defined exposure tool: it gives you the pure-cap-weighted staples sector, nothing more or less, and a researcher should focus on whether that sector's valuation and dividend yield match their investment goals.
