## What is GEME and what markets does it actually cover?

GEME is an exchange-traded fund holding equities of companies headquartered in emerging-market economies, primarily Latin America, Eastern Europe, and developing Asia — including China and India but excluding Japan. A team of analysts selects roughly 50 to 100 stocks they expect will outperform based on fundamental research and company assessment. Unlike a passive emerging-markets index fund, which holds hundreds of stocks weighted by their market capitalisation, GEME concentrates on a smaller list it believes offers superior risk-adjusted returns. The portfolio is genuinely diverse: a large Mexican consumer company sitting alongside a Polish bank, an Indian technology firm, a Brazilian industrial exporter, and a Vietnamese business-services provider — each operating in economies with starkly different growth rates, political stability, governance maturity, and foreign-exchange regimes.

## Why would active management have an edge in emerging markets?

The thesis for active emerging-market managers rests on information asymmetry and market inefficiency. In the United States and Western Europe, thousands of analysts cover thousands of stocks, news travels instantly through electronic channels, and asset prices reflect publicly available information rapidly. In many emerging markets, analyst coverage remains sparse, regulatory filings are less standardised, local-language information is difficult for foreign investors to access, and prices often lag fundamental corporate change by months. A skilled manager with on-the-ground research capability or strong local expertise can theoretically find overlooked bargains and avoid catastrophic errors — companies with hidden related-party transactions, undisclosed leverage, excessive customer concentration, or deteriorating market position. This edge, in principle, justifies the higher fees.

The reality is more complicated. Emerging markets bring structural headwinds that frequently overwhelm any skill in individual stock-picking. Currency movements are severe: a stock that rises 20 percent in its home currency loses 10 percent in dollar terms if that currency depreciates sharply. Political risk — sudden expropriation, asset seizures, capital controls, aggressive tax changes, or shifts in leadership that reverse prior policy — can destroy value overnight and affects all holdings in a country simultaneously. Liquidity in emerging-market equity markets is thin relative to developed markets, making large trades costly and exits difficult during market panics. These structural obstacles mean that even managers with genuine stock-picking skill frequently fail to beat passive emerging-market indices after accounting for fees and currency drag over full cycles.

## What does GEME cost and what are the main risks?

The fund's expense ratio is approximately 0.65–0.85 percent annually, substantially higher than passive emerging-markets funds, which typically cost 0.10–0.35 percent. This premium is charged for the manager's research, analysis, and stock-selection process. History shows, however, that active emerging-market managers do not consistently beat passive alternatives over rolling ten-year periods. The fee therefore becomes a drag on returns regardless of the manager's actual skill level.

Currency risk is the largest structural hazard embedded in GEME. The fund holds stocks denominated in Brazilian reais, Polish zloty, Indian rupees, Thai baht, Mexican pesos, and other emerging-market currencies. When the US dollar strengthens — which often happens when global risk appetite retreats — those currencies weaken in tandem, and returns to a US-dollar-based investor decline even if the underlying stocks rise. GEME can purchase hedging instruments to protect against unfavourable currency moves, but hedging itself costs money and reduces potential upside during strong dollar-weakness periods. With GEME unhedged, currency swings amplify or diminish stock returns in ways unrelated to company performance.

Concentration risk is significant. With only 50–100 holdings, GEME is far more concentrated than a broad emerging-markets index fund holding thousands of stocks. If the manager's top conviction picks underperform — if chosen stocks hit a sector downturn or a geopolitical shock disrupts a region — losses compound across the portfolio. A passive broad-EM fund distributes risk across far more holdings and offers stability at the cost of accepting market returns.

Liquidity in emerging-market equity markets is frequently thinner than in developed markets. A large trade in an emerging-market stock can move prices against the buyer, and during market panics — precisely when emerging markets suffer severe sell-offs — it becomes difficult to exit positions at reasonable prices. GEME itself trades with adequate liquidity on an exchange, but the underlying holdings can be far less liquid, creating a gap between the ease of trading the ETF's shares and the liquidity available in the actual stocks underneath.

## What about political risk and governance problems?

Emerging markets, by definition, have less mature, less stable political institutions and legal systems than developed economies. Expropriation of assets, capital controls, sudden policy reversals, civil unrest, or sovereign default are genuine hazards. A fundamentally sound company can become uninvestable overnight if the government seizes assets, freezes capital flows, or imposes punitive tax changes. GEME's managers work to assess and avoid the worst cases through research and due diligence, but no analytical framework eliminates this risk entirely. It is a structural price of admission to emerging-market equity investing, whether active or passive.

## How should an investor research and evaluate GEME?

Examine the actual top 10 holdings and identify which countries they operate in. Geographic concentration is material: if 40 percent or more of assets are concentrated in China, that represents a large bet on China's economic trajectory and political stability. Compare GEME's rolling three-, five-, and ten-year returns against a passive emerging-markets benchmark to determine whether active management has generated value or simply charged fees while matching or underperforming passive alternatives. Explicitly track the currency impact: if GEME is unhedged, understand that US dollar strength acts as a structural headwind and US dollar weakness provides a tailwind, independent of actual stock-market performance. Read the portfolio manager's quarterly commentary to understand the strategy: Is the fund executing a long-term country rotation (overweighting India while underweighting Mexico)? Is it a sector bet (technology versus financial services)? Or is it pure bottom-up stock-picking with no geographic or sector tilt? Check the fund's annual turnover rate — higher turnover signals frequent holdings replacement and implies elevated trading costs.

Emerging markets can deliver strong returns over full market cycles lasting five years or longer, but those periods will include sharp drawdowns — including years with 30 percent or larger losses. If such volatility would force you to sell at a loss during panic periods, emerging-market equities in any form are inappropriate for your portfolio.
