*Gibson Energy Inc/ADR (GBNXF), trading over-the-counter, represents shares in a Canadian energy company that operates midstream infrastructure—pipelines, storage terminals, and blending facilities—connecting [crude oil](/crude-oil/) producers to refiners and consumers. The company operates as an [American Depositary Receipt](/adr/), allowing US shareholders to hold Canadian stock through a US-traded vehicle. Its unit economics are driven by the volume of crude and refined products moved through its infrastructure and the margin earned per unit of volume.*

## The Unit: Barrels Moved and Margin Per Barrel

Gibson Energy transports and stores crude oil, refined products, and specialty liquids. The unit of business is the barrel—or, more precisely, the barrel-day or the barrel-mile, depending on the service. A company pays Gibson to transport 10,000 barrels of crude oil through its pipeline network from a production area to a market hub. Gibson charges per barrel transported, perhaps $2 to $4 per barrel depending on distance and market conditions. If Gibson moves 10,000 barrels, it generates $20,000 to $40,000 in revenue from that single shipment.

The profitability of that transaction depends on the cost to operate and maintain the pipeline segment. A pipeline that costs $3 million annually to operate (labor, energy, maintenance, inspections) and moves 1 million barrels per year generates $3 per barrel in operating cost. If Gibson charges $3.50 per barrel, the margin is $0.50 per barrel—tight but profitable at scale. If the pipeline moves only 500,000 barrels annually (half capacity), the operating cost per barrel rises to $6, and the business becomes unprofitable.

## Throughput and Capacity Utilization

Gibson Energy's infrastructure has fixed capacity—a pipeline can move X barrels per day, a storage tank can hold Y barrels. As long as the company operates at or near full capacity, unit economics are favorable because fixed costs are spread across maximum volume. If utilization drops—because upstream production falls, or a customer switches to a competitor's pipeline, or a major refinery shuts down—the per-unit cost rises and margins compress.

Throughput is therefore the principal driver of profitability. Gibson benefits from growing crude production in the regions it serves, rising refinery runs, and growing demand for blended products. Conversely, Gibson faces headwinds from production declines, export competition (crude shipped by tanker from other regions), or shifts in transportation patterns (new competing pipelines divert flow).

## Multi-Product Economics

Gibson operates multiple business segments: crude oil pipelines and terminals, refined product pipelines, specialty storage (biodiesel, heavy fuel oils), and marine transportation. Each segment has different unit economics. A crude pipeline moving stable volumes of West Texas crude at $2.50 per barrel has different risk and return than a specialty storage facility charging $2 per barrel-month for strategic storage of biodiesel—a product that is more volatile in demand.

By diversifying across these segments, Gibson stabilizes its overall throughput and revenue base. A decline in crude demand might be offset by stronger refined-product transportation or increased demand for specialty storage. The mix of segments determines the company's overall margin and its resilience to sector shocks.

## Contracted Revenue and Spot Exposure

Some of Gibson's revenue is contracted: a customer commits to shipping X barrels per quarter at Y dollars per barrel for the next three years. This provides revenue certainty and locks in margin. Other revenue is spot or index-based: Gibson charges what the market will bear on a day, and the margin depends on the spread between what competitors charge and Gibson's cost.

Contracted revenue is lower-risk but less upside. Spot revenue is higher-risk but offers upside if Gibson's costs remain fixed and market prices rise (tightening the spread). Gibson's profitability trajectory depends on how much of its portfolio is contracted versus spot, and how aggressive it is in bidding for spot volume.

## Investment in Capacity and Expansion

To grow throughput, Gibson must invest in new pipelines, terminals, and transportation assets. A new pipeline that costs $100 million to build can move 500,000 barrels per year at $2 per barrel margin, generating $1 million in annual profit. The return on investment is 1 percent annually—a very low return if the company has a cost of capital ([cost of debt](/cost-of-debt/) and equity) of 6 to 8 percent.

For expansion to be justified, either the volume must be higher, the margin must be higher, or the useful life of the asset must be much longer than the simple one-year calculation above suggests. Gibson's long-lived infrastructure assets (pipelines, tanks) have useful lives of 20 to 40 years, so the unit economics must be evaluated over the full lifecycle. A pipeline earning 1 percent annually over 30 years generates a 30 percent total return, which, when discounted and compounded, can justify the capital outlay.

## Environmental and Regulatory Costs

Gibson's infrastructure is subject to environmental and safety regulations that mandate inspections, maintenance, emergency response capacity, and spill prevention. These costs are fixed and rising: new environmental rules increase operating costs per unit. If Gibson cannot pass these costs to customers through higher tariffs, margins compress.

Additionally, regulatory delays in permitting new capacity can slow Gibson's growth. A pipeline project approved in concept but delayed by three years in permitting loses three years of operating revenue—a significant drag on the unit economics of that project.

## Capital Intensity and Debt Dependency

Midstream companies like Gibson are highly capital-intensive: they must regularly invest in maintaining and expanding infrastructure. To fund this, they rely heavily on debt. A company with $2 billion in assets might carry $1.2 billion in debt, financing growth and capital maintenance through borrowing and [dividends](/dividend/) paid from operating cash flow.

The unit economics therefore include the cost of debt: higher [interest rates](/interest-rate/) reduce the margin available for dividends or growth. In a period of rising rates, Gibson's profitability per unit transported may be unchanged, but the cost of its debt increases, and the return to shareholders compresses unless [operating margins](/operating-margin/) expand to compensate.

## Commodity and Currency Exposure

Gibson operates in [Canadian dollars](/canadian-dollar/) but has some exposure to [US dollar](/us-dollar/) denominated costs (regulatory compliance, parts procurement) and to commodity prices (crude, refined products, specialty liquids). A weakening Canadian dollar makes imported costs more expensive in local currency terms. A sharp decline in crude prices can reduce shipper demand for transportation (if margins in their own business fall, they defer expansion that would require pipeline volumes).

The unit economics of Gibson's business are therefore exposed to macroeconomic and currency headwinds that are beyond the company's control.
