*[Graphex Group Ltd](/grfxf-stock/) (GRFXF) is a natural graphite mining and processing company with operations and assets primarily in North America. The [public company](/public-company/) model exposes investors to commodity-price volatility and the execution risk inherent in mining development. Filing documents (CIK 1816723) reveal the company's reserve base, capital requirements, and the margin structure that connects ore grades and processing costs to profitability.*

## Mining operations and reserve life

Start with the **proven and probable reserves** statement in the 10-K. The reserve audit (often prepared by a third-party mining engineer and attached as an exhibit) quantifies in-ground graphite in millions of metric tons at a specified cutoff grade. The economics depend critically on:
- **Grade (% graphite in ore)**. Higher-grade ore commands lower processing costs and higher margins.
- **Mining method**: Open-pit is cheaper than underground but leaves less of the resource extractable. Underground costs more but may have higher recovery rates.
- **Reserve life** (reserve tonnage ÷ annual production). Mines with 5-year reserve lives face constant replacement risk; 20-year lives offer durability.
- **Cutoff grade assumptions**. At $500/ton graphite, a lower cutoff becomes economic; at $200/ton, only premium ore matters. This sensitivity is material to guidance and re-life calculations.

## Processing, pricing, and margin structure

Graphite's value lies not in the raw ore but in **beneficiation** (concentration and purification). Graphex's filings should detail:
- **Processing steps**: Crushing, flotation, roasting, purification. Each step is capital-intensive; the 10-K [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) will show [depreciation](/depreciation/) of flotation cells and roasting furnaces.
- **Product mix and pricing**: Graphite is sold as natural flake, microflake, spheroidal (for battery anodes), or specialty powders. These grades command different prices. A shift toward battery-grade graphite (via spheroidization) has higher capex and margins but also higher execution risk.
- **Contractual pricing** vs. **spot-market exposure**. If Graphex sells 80% under long-term contracts and 20% at spot, a commodity downturn hits only the tail. If 100% is spot, volatility cascades to the bottom line.
- **Operating leverage**. If fixed costs (mine equipment depreciation, processing plant overhead) are $50M annually and variable costs are $300/ton, then volume swings hit [operating margin](/operating-margin/) hard.

Check the MD&A for **processing recovery rates** (% of graphite in ore actually recovered as sellable product). If the company boasts a 98% recovery rate, verify this in the operational notes; ghost recoveries inflate reserve economics.

## Capital expenditure and development risk

Graphite mines are infrastructure-heavy. The capex required to bring a new deposit into production, expand existing capacity, or upgrade processing is material. In the 10-K, look for:
- **Sustaining capex**: Annual spending to keep existing operations running. This flows into operating costs and cash-flow calculations.
- **Growth capex**: Expansion of reserves or processing capacity. This is lumpy, requires financing, and carries execution and market-timing risk.
- **Funded status of development projects**: Are new deposits approved and funded, or are they still seeking permits and financing?

A company with $500M in capex ahead on a $150M [market cap](/market-capitalization/) is dependent on external financing or joint partners. The terms of any equity raises, debt covenants, or joint-venture arrangements will cascade into [share dilution](/share-dilution/) or operational constraints.

## Commodity-price exposure and contract mix

Natural graphite is a [commodity](/stock/), priced in the spot market but with long-term contract variations. The 10-K should disclose:
- **Average selling price (ASP)** by product line and geography.
- **Contract coverage**: % of tonnage under fixed-price, inflation-linked, or spot contracts for the next 12, 24, or 36 months.
- **Customer concentration**: Does one customer (e.g., a battery manufacturer or auto-maker) represent >20% of revenue? This creates negotiating leverage in the buyer's favor.

Battery-anode graphite (spheroidized, high-purity) commands a premium; if Graphex markets itself as a battery-supply incumbent but sells mostly into traditional industrial applications, the narrative is misleading.

## Permitting and regulatory standing

Mining operations operate under a web of environmental, permitting, and taxation rules. Material disclosures include:
- **Permits and licenses**: Mining permits, water discharge permits, air quality permits. Are any up for renewal? Are there outstanding violations or closure orders?
- **Reclamation and bonding**: Mining regulations require bonding or trust funds for eventual site reclamation. Large bonding obligations reduce reported net income and cash available to shareholders.
- **Royalties and government take**: Mining usually involves royalties to governments or landowners (% of gross revenue) and potentially windfall-profit taxes in commodity upswings. A royalty of 3% is not the same as 10%.

## Peer and end-market dynamics

Graphex competes with Syrah (now majority-owned by Panasonic), other natural graphite miners, and to some extent synthetic graphite. The competitive position hinges on:
- **Cost curve**: Is Graphex in the lowest-cost quartile? Lowest-cost producers survive downturns and dictate pricing in booms.
- **Product differentiation**: Battery-anode graphite can command premiums; commodity flake cannot.
- **Offtake agreements**: Signed deals with battery makers or auto suppliers reduce spot-price risk.

## For desk research

Begin with the reserve audit and operating history table (usually in Item 1A). Extract cumulative production, ore mined, and grade; this shows whether the mine is becoming harder to work. Then read the commodity-price sensitivity in MD&A and model two scenarios: graphite at $400/ton and $800/ton. The [enterprise value](/enterprise-value/) that justifies the stock price becomes visible once you know the cost structure and reserve life.
