## The history: Treasury indexing evolves

Treasury bonds have been the safest asset in the world for nearly two centuries, backed by the full faith and credit of the US government. For most of that history, investors simply bought Treasury bonds directly from brokers or held them to maturity. In the 1980s and 1990s, as index investing became mainstream, fund sponsors began creating Treasury bond ETFs and mutual funds to make it easier for retail investors to own a diversified basket of Treasuries without the friction of buying individual bonds.

Early Treasury ETFs typically tracked a single maturity or a narrow slice of the curve — a fund that held only ten-year notes, for example, or only bonds maturing between five and ten years. This allowed investors to place a specific bet on where they expected interest rates to move. But most investors wanted simpler exposure: just own the Treasury market and let the bonds mature over time.

Invesco introduced GOVI as an equal-weight approach to the Treasury spectrum. Rather than weight holdings by market capitalization (which would put most of the money in long-dated bonds, since they trade in larger dollar volumes), GOVI divides its portfolio into equal buckets across the maturity range from short-term to thirty-year bonds. Every maturity segment gets roughly the same dollar allocation.

## How equal weighting changes the picture

This equal-weight methodology is the funds defining characteristic. In a traditional Treasury index weighted by market cap, longer-dated bonds carry much higher weights because they have longer durations and larger outstanding amounts. A market-cap-weighted Treasury fund might be 40 percent long-dated bonds, 30 percent intermediate, and 30 percent short-term.

GOVI distributes capital more evenly across the maturity spectrum. This means the fund is less sensitive to moves in the long end of the bond curve (where market-cap-weighted funds are most exposed) and more sensitive to moves across the full curve. When short-term rates fall while long-term rates stay flat, GOVI captures gains across the whole range instead of missing some of them. When the curve flattens (short and long rates converge), GOVI benefits differently than a cap-weighted fund would.

The equal-weight approach is a subtle but meaningful bet on how Treasury yields will move. Investors who think the Fed will eventually cut rates across the entire curve benefit from equal-weighting, because all maturities will likely rise together. Investors who expect only certain parts of the curve to move might prefer a more targeted Treasury fund.

## The portfolio and rebalancing

GOVI holds actual US Treasury securities — not futures or derivatives, but the real bonds issued by the US Treasury. The fund managers buy Treasury bonds at various maturities, equal-weight them across the curve, and rebalance periodically as yields change and bonds mature.

As bonds in the fund approach maturity, they are either allowed to mature (the cash is reinvested further out the curve) or are sold and replaced. This ongoing turnover is one reason even a Treasury fund has a non-zero expense ratio — it is not free to buy and sell Treasuries, and the fund managers earn a spread between the buying and selling prices.

The funds net effective duration (a measure of how much the funds price will move if yields change by one percentage point) is somewhere in the middle of the curve — higher than a pure short-term bond fund, lower than a fund holding only thirty-year bonds. This middle-ground positioning is intentional: GOVI is meant to give you broad Treasury exposure without tilting hard toward any one maturity segment.

## Costs and how it trades

GOVI trades on the NASDAQ like any ETF, with tight bid-ask spreads given the high trading volume. You can buy and sell shares at market prices during market hours without difficulty.

The expense ratio is very low, as Treasury ETFs go. Treasury bonds are liquid, well-understood instruments, and managing a Treasury fund is straightforward — buy Treasuries at various maturities, hold them, rebalance occasionally. The fund sponsor is not running a complex strategy or making high-conviction bets, just managing a portfolio that mirrors a published index.

## The interest-rate risk

GOVIs primary risk is interest-rate risk. When interest rates rise, the market value of existing bonds falls (because new bonds issued at higher rates become more attractive). When interest rates fall, existing bond prices rise. GOVIs price will rise or fall with the Treasury market.

The magnitude of that move depends on the funds duration. A fund with a five-year duration will lose approximately 5 percent of its value if interest rates rise one percentage point. GOVIs equal-weight construction gives it a duration somewhere in the middle of the curve — likely between five and seven years, depending on the current maturity distribution. That is more duration exposure than a short-term Treasury fund but less than a long-duration fund.

For most investors, this is not a bug but a feature. Treasury bonds are meant to stabilize a portfolio during stock market volatility. They perform that role best when their prices move less than stocks do (lower volatility) but still move in a non-correlated direction. GOVIs moderate duration achieves that balance.

## Who GOVI is for

GOVI is suited for conservative investors, retirees, and anyone seeking to own US Treasury bonds without the complexity of buying individual bonds or choosing a single maturity. It is the core holding for investors who want the safety and stability of US government debt in an easy-to-trade package.

It is not suited for investors making tactical bets on where interest rates will move or trying to time the Treasury market. If you have strong convictions about the future path of the Fed or the economy, a more focused Treasury fund will amplify your bet.

## How to research and evaluate

Read Invescos fact sheet for GOVI, which lays out the current maturity distribution and the funds duration. Compare GOVIs duration to other Treasury ETFs to understand where it sits on the curve. Track GOVIs performance during periods of rising and falling interest rates to get a sense of its price sensitivity.

Watch the Treasury yield curve itself. GOVI performs well when the curve is stable or rising (rates rising together across maturities) and suffers most when long-term rates rise sharply while short-term rates fall (the curve inverts or flattens).
