*The **[Goldman Sachs](/goldman-sachs/) Access [Municipal Bond](/municipal-bond/) ETF** (GMUN) is a [passively managed fund](/passively-managed-fund/) holding a broad cross-section of investment-grade municipal [bonds](/bond/) — debt instruments issued by US state and local governments. The "Access" in its name reflects Goldman Sachs' positioning of the fund as a straightforward, low-cost vehicle for retail investors to tap the tax-exempt income that muni bonds offer.*

| Attribute | Details |
|---|---|
| **What it holds** | Investment-grade municipal bonds from US states, cities, special districts, and authorities |
| **Number of holdings** | 2,500+ individual bonds |
| **Credit quality** | Investment grade only (Moody's Baa3 or higher, or S&P BBB- or higher equivalent) |
| **Maturity structure** | Intermediate to long (mix of bonds with 5–30+ year maturities) |
| **Geography** | All 50 US states and hundreds of municipal issuers |
| **Income frequency** | Monthly distributions (typically tax-exempt) |
| **Expense ratio** | Low, reflecting passive indexing |
| **Tax status** | Interest exempt from federal income tax; often exempt from state income tax for residents |

## How it works in practice

GMUN tracks an index of municipal bonds available in the [secondary market](/secondary-market/), selecting those meeting the investment-grade threshold. Unlike an actively managed muni fund, it does not attempt to pick outperformers or dodge credit downgrades; instead, it holds a representative sample of the muni market, rebalancing periodically to maintain its index methodology.

The fund buys bonds in the secondary market — bonds already issued and circulating among investors — rather than at new issuance. This is important because new-issue munis are sold through syndicates with negotiated concessions, while secondary-market trading is often at tighter prices, making it more accessible for large purchases. An ETF's scale allows it to buy efficiently in the secondary market.

The bonds underlying GMUN finance everyday infrastructure: water systems, schools, libraries, highways, hospitals, public housing, and sewer authorities. The issuer repays principal at maturity (often 10–30 years in the future) and sends [coupon payments](/coupon-payment/) (often semi-annually) to bondholders. GMUN consolidates these thousands of individual coupon streams into monthly distributions to shareholders.

## Why munis matter for certain investors

A US muni bond's interest is exempt from federal income tax. Many states also exempt in-state munis from state income tax for residents. This tax shelter makes munis economically attractive for high-income earners subject to steep marginal tax rates. A 5% muni yield is equivalent to roughly a 7–8% taxable yield for a top-bracket taxpayer, depending on state taxes. For investors in low tax brackets, the advantage disappears and munis become unattractive — they should own taxable bonds instead.

The downside of the tax exemption is that the muni market is less transparent and less widely followed than the Treasury or [corporate bond](/corporate-bond/) markets. Defaults do happen, though rarely among investment-grade issuers. A diversified muni fund like GMUN spreads this risk across thousands of issuers.

## The interest-rate and credit dimensions

GMUN's bond holdings carry two main risks. The first is [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/): when the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises rates, existing bond prices fall (and vice versa). A holder buying GMUN must be prepared for the possibility of mark-to-market losses in a rising-rate environment. For a buy-and-hold investor planning to keep the fund for years, this volatility is temporary — bonds repay at par at maturity. For someone who might need the cash in five years, interest-rate risk is material.

The second is [credit risk](/credit-risk/): an issuer may struggle financially and cut coupon payments or default on principal. GMUN's investment-grade mandate screens out the worst credits, but it does not eliminate credit risk entirely. A [recession](/recession/) that spreads fiscal stress across municipalities could pressure even investment-grade issuers. In 2020, the pandemic initially triggered a flight from even high-quality munis, creating significant short-term price declines before credit stabilized.

[Currency risk](/currency-risk/) is not an issue since all bonds are denominated in [US dollars](/us-dollar/) and issued by US entities.

## Comparing GMUN to alternatives

GMUN is not the only muni ETF. There are taxable-bond ETFs (which pay higher yields but offer no tax exemption), Treasury ETFs (which are safer but offer lower yields and no tax advantage), and other muni funds of varying credit quality and maturity. An investor should compare GMUN's yield to peer muni ETFs and to taxable alternatives, calculate the breakeven tax rate (at what marginal rate does the muni yield become equivalent to a taxable yield), and then honestly assess whether they actually face that tax rate.

State tax considerations matter too. A New York resident may benefit substantially from New York-specific muni ETFs, which offer exemption from both federal and state income tax on NY bonds. GMUN's national [diversification](/diversification/) does not capture this double exemption for in-state holdings, so a resident of a high-tax state should compare GMUN to state-specific alternatives.

## Liquidity and practical considerations

GMUN trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) throughout the day with reasonable [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/), making it easy to buy or sell at transparent prices. The fund's creation and redemption mechanism helps keep the ETF's market price close to its underlying [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), so arbitrage opportunities are limited but price slippage is minimal.

The fund's monthly distributions are convenient for income-focused investors and can be reinvested automatically. For taxable investors, the tax character of the distributions (generally exempt from federal income tax) is the core reason to own the fund.

## Research and decision-making

Before buying GMUN, check the [current yield](/current-yield/) relative to your state of residence's income-tax rate. A muni yielding 4% is worth roughly 6% if you face a 33% marginal federal tax rate, but less if you face 22%. Also examine the fund's [effective duration](/effective-duration/) (a measure of interest-rate sensitivity); a longer duration means bigger price swings when rates move. The fact sheet discloses both. Compare GMUN to other national muni ETFs on duration, yield, and [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/). If you live in a high-tax state, compare it to state-specific alternatives. And honestly assess whether you plan to hold the fund through interest-rate swings or whether you might need the cash sooner; that timeline drives how much interest-rate risk you should accept.
