*The Genter Capital Taxable Quality Intermediate ETF (GENT) invests in [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/) issued by the US government, government agencies, and corporations, with a focus on intermediate-maturity securities.*

## What does GENT hold?

GENT's portfolio is split between several [bond](/bond/) types. US Treasuries and Treasury-backed securities (bonds issued by the government itself, backed by the full faith and credit of the United States) form a foundation—these are the safest bonds available, offering minimal [credit risk](/credit-risk/) but typically lower yield than corporate alternatives. Mortgage-backed securities, issued by government-sponsored enterprises like [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/) and [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/), are also typically present; these carry slightly higher yield than pure Treasuries in exchange for [prepayment risk](/prepayment-risk/) (the ability of homeowners to refinance and pay off the bonds early). Investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) issued by companies with strong [credit ratings](/credit-rating/) round out the mix—banks, insurers, manufacturers, technology firms, and utilities issue bonds to fund operations and refinance debt, and GENT holds the highest-quality of these corporate issuances.

The intermediate maturity focus means bonds in the portfolio typically mature between five and ten years from the fund's rebalancing date. This timeframe balances yield against price stability; longer-duration bonds (those maturing in 20 or 30 years) offer higher yield but swing far more wildly when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) change, while shorter bonds offer less volatility but minimal income. An investor buying GENT expects to hold a stable price and collect consistent interest, with exposure to intermediate [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/)—meaningful but not extreme.

## How does quality screening work?

GENT applies an investment-grade filter, meaning all holdings must have credit ratings in the top four tiers from agencies like Moody's or S&P. Investment-grade bonds are considered safe enough for conservative investors; speculative-grade or [junk bonds](/junk-bond/) are excluded. This quality discipline significantly narrows the universe of possible holdings. Within the investment-grade category, GENT may also apply quantitative filters—for example, excluding bonds from issuers with deteriorating earnings, high leverage, or other signs of financial stress. The goal is to hold bonds from entities with strong cash generation and low default risk, so investors can collect interest reliably without constant fear of principal loss.

## What about income and distributions?

GENT distributes its interest income—both from Treasury coupons and corporate bond interest—to shareholders as regular [dividend](/dividend/) payments, typically monthly. These distributions are subject to federal income tax and, in many cases, state and local income tax. The tax treatment varies: interest on US [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) is exempt from state and local tax but subject to federal tax; interest on corporate bonds and mortgage-backed securities is fully taxable. For investors in high tax brackets, this full tax liability is a meaningful drag on returns compared to tax-exempt [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/). For investors in lower brackets or those investing through tax-advantaged accounts (IRAs, 401ks, etc.), the tax drag is irrelevant, and GENT's yield and safety are all that matter.

## What drives GENT's price?

The primary driver is interest-rate movement. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises rates or [inflation expectations](/inflation-expectations/) rise, bond prices fall—investors demand higher yields on new bonds, and older bonds with lower coupons become less attractive. GENT's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) declines as the bonds it holds lose market value. Conversely, when rates fall, bond prices rise and GENT's value increases. With intermediate duration (typically five to seven years), a one-percentage-point rise in rates causes roughly a five- to seven-percent decline in GENT's market price. This price volatility is real and can be significant, especially over short time horizons.

Secondary is credit risk: if an issuer in GENT's portfolio faces financial deterioration, its bonds fall in value before any default occurs. Corporate bonds are particularly sensitive to credit news. A major bank or industrial company reporting weak earnings, losing a key customer, or facing regulatory pressure can see its bond prices drop as investors reprrice the risk. GENT's investment-grade filter lowers this risk materially, but it does not eliminate it.

## Who is GENT for?

GENT suits investors who want fixed-income exposure outside tax-advantaged accounts and who have no use for tax-exempt municipal bonds (perhaps they live in a low-tax jurisdiction, are in a low tax bracket, or expect their income to be low). It also suits retirement accounts where tax efficiency does not matter because all income is ultimately taxed the same way when withdrawn. The intermediate maturity makes it appropriate for investors with a five-to-ten-year time horizon; those needing money sooner should consider shorter-duration funds, and those with longer horizons might accept longer-duration bonds for higher yield.

## What are the real risks?

**Interest-rate risk** is paramount. A sustained rise in rates will pressure GENT's price. If you buy GENT and rates rise sharply, you will face an unrealized loss until rates fall again or maturity arrives. **Credit risk**, while minimized by the investment-grade filter, persists—a [recession](/recession/) or adverse [credit event](/credit-event-sovereign/) can trigger downgrades and price declines in corporate-bond holdings. **[Reinvestment risk](/reinvestment-risk/)** occurs when bonds mature or interest income is received; if rates are lower than they were when GENT was purchased, new investment opportunities will be less attractive. **[Inflation risk](/inflation-risk/)** is silent but real: if [inflation](/inflation/) erodes the purchasing power of GENT's fixed interest payments, the real return is lower than the nominal interest rate suggests.

## How to research GENT before investing?

Start with the prospectus and a recent factsheet to understand the current allocation: what percentage is Treasuries, mortgage-backed securities, and corporate bonds? Look at the weighted-average maturity and duration to confirm they align with intermediate positioning. Review the top 10 holdings to see which corporations and issuers dominate. Examine the fund's yield—is it materially above what Treasury bonds offer, and does that premium adequately compensate for corporate credit risk? Check the expense ratio and compare it to similar taxable-bond funds; GENT's costs should be competitive. Finally, consider your own interest-rate outlook: if you expect rates to rise, GENT's intermediate duration may decline in price; if you expect rates to fall, GENT could appreciate. If uncertain, GENT's intermediate positioning provides reasonable balance.
