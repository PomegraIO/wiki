*The **[Goldman Sachs](/goldman-sachs/) Future Tech Leaders [Equity ETF](/equity-etf/)** (ticker GTEK) is an actively managed stock fund that holds a concentrated portfolio of companies Goldman Sachs' analysts believe are positioned to lead transformative technology trends — artificial intelligence, cloud infrastructure, semiconductor innovation, and related fields.*

## Goldman's thesis on future technology

GTEK does not track a pre-set index. Instead, Goldman Sachs' equity research and portfolio management teams choose which companies they believe are best positioned to capture the next generation of technology innovation. The fund's core premise is that certain sectors — artificial intelligence, cloud computing, semiconductor design and manufacturing, quantum computing, and adjacent infrastructure — will drive outsized returns over the next decade, and that Goldman's analysts can identify which companies within those sectors will win.

The fund is therefore a vehicle for a specific investment thesis. Unlike a broad technology [index fund](/index-fund/) that holds every large-cap tech company weighted by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/), GTEK overweights companies in growth-phase tech segments and underweights or excludes mature tech companies that Goldman judges as lacking future upside. This makes it narrower, more concentrated, and riskier than an index fund — but also positioned to capture larger gains if Goldman's thesis proves correct.

## The concentration play

GTEK typically holds 30 to 50 stocks, far fewer than a passive broad-market technology fund. The fund's largest positions often include established mega-cap technology companies with significant AI or cloud divisions, alongside smaller or mid-cap pure-plays in semiconductors, software infrastructure, and related fields. The concentration means no single holding dominates (the largest position might be 3–5% of assets), but a handful of names can drive the fund's performance meaningfully — if those stocks fall, the fund falls harder than a diversified index would.

This concentrated approach reflects a bet that Goldman's team can identify which technology leaders will outpace the broader market. On a strong year for technology stocks, this concentration amplifies upside; on a weak year, it amplifies downside. The fund is not for buy-and-forget investors; it is for those who believe in both technology as a sector and in Goldman Sachs' ability to pick winners.

## What "future tech" means in practice

Goldman's definition of future-tech companies includes several overlapping categories. First, companies building the infrastructure for artificial intelligence — chip designers such as those optimizing processors for AI workloads, cloud platforms offering AI services, and software providers that enable AI adoption. Second, companies in semiconductor manufacturing and design, because chips are the foundation of every technology advancement and a persistent bottleneck. Third, pure-software companies that solve critical problems in cloud computing, cybersecurity, and data infrastructure. Fourth, companies entering adjacent spaces like quantum computing and edge computing that may become mainstream over the next decade.

The fund filters for companies with strong competitive positions, defensible technology, recurring revenue models where possible, and management teams Goldman's analysts trust. It excludes or avoids companies in mature, commoditized segments — say, PCs or traditional networking equipment — even if they are large, because Goldman judges them unlikely to compound wealth at the rate the fund's target market demands.

## Who this fund targets

GTEK is designed for growth-oriented investors with a multi-year time horizon who believe technology will be a outperforming sector and who trust Goldman's research capability. It is not a stable-income holding; shareholders should expect high volatility. It appeals to investors building a growth-heavy portfolio who want technology exposure with a more concentrated, activist-research angle than a passive technology index would provide.

The fund is also relevant for tactical technology rotations. An investor might hold a broad technology index fund for stable core tech exposure, and then use GTEK to overweight specific technology themes Goldman is bullish on for a defined period. Institutional investors sometimes use it to gain thematic exposure to AI or semiconductor trends without building a custom stock portfolio.

## Costs and performance tracking

GTEK's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is higher than a passive technology index fund, typically around 0.60% to 0.70% per year. This reflects the cost of active management — research, portfolio management, trading — and is reasonable for an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/), though it means the fund must outperform the technology index by at least that margin just to match it. Goldman publishes the fund's performance history regularly; investors should compare it to a broad tech index (like the Nasdaq-100 or technology sector of the S&P 500) to evaluate whether Goldman's stock-picking has justified the fee.

The fund trades on [NYSE Arca](/nyse-arca/) and typically has moderate liquidity, though it is not as heavily traded as the largest passive index ETFs.

## Risks and the concentration trap

The primary risk is concentration. If Goldman's thesis on which technology companies will lead turns out wrong — or if the chosen leaders fall out of favor due to regulation, competition, or innovation shifts — the fund's concentrated bets amplify the losses. An investor holding a broad tech index might see a 10% drawdown during a sell-off; GTEK could easily fall 20% or more if the specific companies Goldman favored underperform.

A second risk is style risk. The fund is tilted toward growth-oriented, higher-valuation companies in emerging technology segments. In years when the market rewards stable cash-flow businesses over growth, GTEK underperforms. Conversely, in years when growth is the place to be, it can significantly outperform. An investor needs to understand this cyclicality.

Third is the risk of technological disruption itself. Goldman's thesis about which technology will matter is intelligent, but technology's path is fundamentally unpredictable. A startup or an unexpected shift in industry standards could disrupt the leaders the fund holds. The fund is not sheltered from that risk; it is directly exposed to it, which is part of why the upside potential is high and the downside risk is real.

## How a reader would research GTEK

Start with Goldman Sachs' fund materials, including the prospectus, fact sheet, and any white papers on the fund's investment thesis and the technology trends it tracks. These explain what problems Goldman's team thinks technology will solve and why they think specific companies will win.

Review the fund's holdings list and sector breakdown regularly. What fraction is in semiconductors versus software versus infrastructure? Which specific companies are the largest positions? Cross-reference those against news about those companies' growth, competition, and regulatory pressures.

Compare GTEK's performance to a broad technology index fund (passive) and to its own stated benchmark if it has one. Over a five-year period, has Goldman's active stock-picking generated returns above the index after fees? If not, a passive fund might be the cheaper alternative.

Finally, consider the fit in a portfolio. GTEK is a satellite position, not a core holding — an overweight to a thesis, not a diversified equity core. Pair it with broader holdings if you want a balanced exposure.
