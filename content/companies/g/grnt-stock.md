*The business of finding, drilling for, and producing [crude oil](/crude-oil/) and [natural gas](/natural-gas/) in the United States unfolds entirely within a regulatory system so intricate that no operator can move a drilling rig or sell a barrel without satisfying federal, state, and in some cases local authorities. **Granite Ridge Resources, Inc.** (GRNT), an independent exploration and production company, does not merely comply with this system—the regulatory regime defines what the company can drill, where, when, and under what environmental and financial conditions.*

## The Permitting Gauntlet

Before Granite Ridge can drill a single well, it must navigate a multi-stage permitting process. On federal lands (public lands managed by the Bureau of Land Management or on the Outer Continental Shelf), the company must submit detailed Applications for Permit to Drill (APD), including geological surveys, environmental assessments, and operational plans. The BLM reviews these applications against the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), examining potential impacts on water, wildlife, air quality, and cultural resources. This review can take six months to two years. State regulators (Texas, Oklahoma, Colorado, New Mexico, depending on Granite Ridge's operating footprint) layer additional permitting atop the federal process. Texas's Railroad Commission, for instance, evaluates whether a well conforms to Rule 8 spacing standards, confirms the company has a right to operate, and may require bonding to cover potential cleanup obligations.

State permits also address groundwater protection and hydraulic fracturing disclosure. Many states now require companies to disclose the chemicals used in fracking fluids to a public database (FracFocus, operated jointly by state regulators and industry). These disclosures are mandatory, not voluntary, and failures to disclose can result in well shut-ins or fines. This is not compliance theater; it is a direct operational constraint. A regulator can deny or delay a permit if the company cannot demonstrate adequate water-management practices or if local opposition reaches sufficient volume.

## Environmental Bonding and Financial Assurance

Regulators do not take Granite Ridge's promises to restore wells and sites at face value. Instead, the company must post a [bond](/bond/) or other financial assurance—collateral held by the state to guarantee that wells will be properly plugged and abandoned and that the land will be reclaimed. Bond amounts vary by state and by the depth and risk profile of the well, but a single deep well might require a $50,000 to $500,000 bond. Companies operating across a state with hundreds of wells can accumulate bonding obligations in the tens of millions of dollars. This is capital that cannot be invested in exploration or production; it sits with the state, earning the state (not the company) any interest accrued. If Granite Ridge were to become insolvent or abandon its obligations, the state would use the bond to fund cleanup.

Because states recognize that bonding alone does not always cover the full cost of plugging and reclamation (wells can be expensive to clean up), some states are moving toward plugging-fund requirements—mechanisms where operators set aside reserves specifically for future decommissioning. These reserves accrue on the company's [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) as a liability, reducing reported earnings and constraining the capital available for [dividends](/dividend/) or reinvestment.

## Lease Obligations and Royalty Structures

If Granite Ridge operates on private land, it must hold a lease from the landowner. Leases are contracts, but they are also subject to state common law, and many states have implied covenants that require operators to conduct operations in a reasonably prudent manner and to develop leases in a timely manner. A regulator or a shrewd landowner can challenge Granite Ridge's actions if the company shuts in a well, redirects capital elsewhere, or delays development unreasonably. If operating on federal lands, Granite Ridge must bid for a lease through the BLM's competitive process, meet minimum bids and rental payments, and pay a percentage of revenue (typically 12.5%) to the federal government as a royalty. These lease terms are not negotiated; they are set by regulation.

## Disclosure and Climate Risk Reporting

The [Securities and Exchange Commission](/securities-and-exchange-commission/) now requires oil and gas companies to disclose certain climate and environmental risks in their annual reports and [10-k](/10-k/) filings. Granite Ridge must address questions such as how it manages emissions, whether it faces physical climate risks (hurricanes, flooding, drought), and what regulatory risks it faces from carbon taxes or emissions limits. The SEC has clarified that companies must disclose material climate-related impacts with the same rigor they would for any other business risk. This means Granite Ridge cannot hide behind generalities; it must quantify and explain its exposure to climate regulation, carbon pricing, and physical climate impacts on its operations.

For an independent producer, this can be uncomfortable. The company's entire value proposition rests on extracting hydrocarbons—assets that climate policy explicitly aims to phase out. Regulators and investors increasingly view stranded assets (reserves that become uneconomic due to climate policy) as material financial risks. Granite Ridge must disclose these risks clearly, even if doing so undermines its ability to raise capital or attract investors seeking to divest from fossil fuels.

## Regulatory Changes and Strategic Uncertainty

One of Granite Ridge's deepest strategic risks is that regulatory regimes can change rapidly. The Biden administration, for instance, placed a temporary moratorium on new federal oil and gas lease sales, later challenged in court. State governments impose emissions standards or methane regulations that raise operating costs. Countries that purchase U.S. oil (particularly European nations) are raising tariffs or import restrictions on high-carbon petroleum. Granite Ridge cannot predict decades ahead which regulations will stick and which will be repealed or weakened. This uncertainty depresses long-term capital investment in exploration; a company that cannot forecast its regulatory environment beyond a few years will not commit billions to finding and developing reserves that may be stranded by the time they become productive.

## Operational Discipline Under Scrutiny

Because the regulatory environment is so visible and often contentious, Granite Ridge's operations are subject to both official and unofficial scrutiny. Environmental groups monitor the company's drilling activity, file administrative complaints with regulators, and challenge permits in court. These lawsuits do not always succeed, but they delay projects, add legal costs, and create reputational pressures. A spill or safety incident can trigger not just state investigations but federal investigations by the Department of Interior, lawsuits, criminal referrals, and massive remediation costs. Granite Ridge must maintain scrupulous safety and environmental practices not because they are profitable (they often are not in the short term) but because regulators and the public demand them.

This discipline extends to workforce practices. Regulators and investors increasingly scrutinize whether oil and gas companies employ fairly, respect indigenous lands, and community stakeholders. Non-compliance can result in permit denials or withdrawal of social license—the informal permission from communities and stakeholders to operate—a loss that can be as damaging as a regulatory fine.

The bottom line: Granite Ridge's business plan is not simply to find oil and drill it profitably. The company's business plan is to find oil, design drilling and operational strategies that can survive permitting review, post adequate financial assurances, disclose material risks honestly, and execute operations in a way that satisfies federal and state regulators. Regulatory compliance is not a cost center; it is a core competency. Companies that treat it as an afterthought do not survive.
