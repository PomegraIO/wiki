The **FT Vest U.S. Equity Moderate [Buffer ETF](/buffer-etf/) - August** (GAUG) is a [structured product](/structured-product/) that wraps a US equity portfolio in a [collar strategy](/collar-strategy/) — holding the broad market while simultaneously selling [call options](/call-option/) out of the money and buying [put options](/put-option/) to create a "cushion" against large losses.

## How the buffer strategy works

The fund holds a diversified portfolio of US large-cap stocks — typically tracking something like the S&P 500 or Russell 1000. Simultaneously, it enters an options collar: it sells short calls above the current market price to collect premiums, and uses those proceeds to buy [protective puts](/protective-put/) below the market. The puts floor the downside — if the market drops more than a specified percentage (around 15%), the put gains offset the portfolio loss. The short calls cap the upside — if the market surges, the gains are capped around a certain level (often 12–16% above the entry point).

The result is a bounded return: modest losses are absorbed by the portfolio, but large declines are cushioned; gains are capped. This trade-off appeals to investors who want equity exposure but cannot stomach a 30% or 40% drawdown — the buffer absorbs some of the pain at the cost of forgoing some of the upside in strong [bull markets](/bull-market/).

## The rolling reset and August mechanics

The collar position does not last forever. Every 12 months, around August, the fund closes the old puts and calls and writes a new collar at new market prices with a new strike structure. This "August reset" is the reason for the fund's ticker suffix. When markets have risen, the new collar is struck at a higher level; when they have fallen, at a lower level. This resetting mechanism means the buffer protection is ongoing, month to month, rather than a one-time shield that expires and leaves the portfolio naked.

The August timing matters for tax planning. [Covered call](/covered-call/) funds (which sell calls without the protective puts) often reset in December; GAUG's August reset is different, and investors should know when the roll happens to time exits and entries if they are concerned about realizing gains.

## Who the fund is for, and why

This structure appeals primarily to investors near retirement or in drawdown mode who want to stay invested in equities but cannot tolerate a severe loss. A retiree drawing 4% of a portfolio annually cannot afford a 50% drop — it would force them to cut spending or sell assets at the worst time. GAUG's buffer does not eliminate that risk entirely, but it reduces the magnitude: a 30% market decline becomes a 15% fund decline, which is more manageable.

The tradeoff is real and must be understood. In a strong bull market, GAUG underperforms a plain S&P 500 ETF because the short calls cap the upside. Someone holding GAUG from 2023 to 2024, for example, would have captured the equity market's gains but capped well below the market's full rise. That opportunity cost is the cost of the insurance.

For long-term accumulators with decades until retirement, the buffer is likely a poor trade — the chance of a severe loss is offset by the certainty of missing outsized gains. For someone already retired and sensitive to losses, it can make psychological and practical sense.

## Costs and real risks

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) of a buffered ETF is typically higher than a plain [index fund](/index-fund/), because the options rebalancing and monitoring incur costs. That drag matters over decades; a 0.50% annual expense ratio is five times the 0.10% of a standard [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) fund. Over 20 years, the compounding difference is substantial.

The buffer is not costless, and it has gaps. If the market drops exactly 15% to the buffer boundary, the fund breaks even — no loss absorbed, but no gain either. If it drops 14%, the fund has a modest loss. The protection is real, but it does not mean "losses are impossible"; it means "losses are capped at around X%." During the 2020 COVID crash, when the S&P 500 fell 34%, the benefit was meaningful; in a typical shallow 10% correction, much of the protection remains unused and represents cost for insurance that did not fire.

Concentrated equity risk remains present — the fund still holds US large-cap stocks, so it is sensitive to [sector rotations](/sector-rotation/) and valuations. A crash in US valuations affects GAUG, albeit muted by the collar. There is also tracking risk: the fund's returns may diverge slightly from the theoretical buffer model due to execution timing and costs.

## Researching GAUG

Read the fund's prospectus carefully, particularly the section on the collar strategy. Understand the exact striking levels of the puts and calls at the current roll. The fact sheet should show recent performance compared to a plain S&P 500 index — that gap illustrates the opportunity cost of the buffer over the chosen time period. Look at annualized returns over 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year periods; in many periods, the buffer's cost outweighs its benefit for long-term investors.

Ask yourself whether you truly need the downside protection, or whether you simply dislike volatility — the distinction matters. Volatility is normal and necessary; sharp declines are rare. If you are 30, wealthy, and simply uncomfortable with market swings, a buffer ETF is an expensive solution to a problem that might be better solved by reducing equity allocation overall. If you are 68, living off your portfolio, and a major loss would force you to sell depreciated assets, the buffer serves a real purpose.
