The [Goldman Sachs](/goldman-sachs/) MarketBeta U.S. 1000 [Equity ETF](/equity-etf/) (GUSA) traces its roots to Goldman Sachs' own internal research and trading desks, where the firm spent decades building quantitative models for stock selection. When Goldman Sachs launched GUSA, it did so not to create an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) but to offer investors access to its quantitative equity framework—a disciplined set of rules for picking which stocks to hold from the universe of large and mid-cap American companies. The fund launched in 2017 as an institutional-grade alternative to cap-weighted [index funds](/index-fund/), and it remains Goldman's statement to the market about how systematic, factor-aware investing should work.

## From Goldman's trading desk to public markets

Goldman Sachs is one of the oldest and largest financial institutions in the world, and its equity-research and trading operations are legendary. For generations, Goldman's traders and researchers built models to predict which stocks would outperform based on fundamental factors—earnings growth, valuation, profitability, and other signals drawn from balance-sheet and income-statement data. These models operated in Goldman's proprietary trading business and advisory work, capturing the firm's accumulated conviction about what makes a stock worth owning.

GUSA represents a partial opening of that vault to the public. Rather than a fully active strategy where a portfolio manager picks stocks, GUSA follows a rules-based approach using Goldman's market-selection criteria. The fund does not employ a single stock-picker or claim to beat the market through skill; instead, it offers a transparent, replicable set of selection rules that can be applied mechanically each quarter. This is the core distinction between GUSA and an actively managed Goldman Sachs equity fund: transparency, replicability, and lower cost. It lets investors benefit from Goldman's long experience in identifying stocks that tend to outperform on fundamental metrics, without paying the premium for active management or bearing the risk that one portfolio manager's particular genius may not endure.

## How the portfolio is selected and weighted

GUSA holds roughly one thousand stocks selected from the large and mid-cap universe of the US equity market. The fund does not weigh all of them equally; instead it uses a rules-based scoring system that emphasizes fundamental quality and value. Stocks with strong earnings growth, high profit margins, improving balance-sheet strength, and reasonable valuations receive larger positions; those with deteriorating fundamentals or stretched valuations receive smaller ones or are excluded. The exact formulas are published in the fund's documentation, so an investor can see precisely how GUSA would rank and weight any given stock.

Rebalancing happens quarterly. Each quarter, GUSA recalculates scores for all eligible stocks, adjusts weightings, and trims or adds positions. This contrasts with a cap-weighted index fund, which mechanically holds all stocks in proportion to their market size and rebalances only when constituents drift—a far more passive process. GUSA's active reweighting introduces real costs (trading friction, market-impact costs, taxes) but also the possibility of systematic outperformance if the underlying selection criteria identify genuine patterns in stock returns.

The portfolio carries a tilt toward higher-quality, more profitable companies, which means its sector exposure is not purely cap-weighted. In years when the market favors growth and unprofitable tech, GUSA will typically lag because it does not overweight the frothiest stocks. In years when value and profitability matter, GUSA will outperform. This is not a flaw; it is the stated design.

## Costs and the fund's place in Goldman's ecosystem

GUSA's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is materially lower than an active equity [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/) (typically in the 0.20–0.30% range), reflecting the mechanical nature of its construction and the absence of a traditional portfolio manager's salary. It is higher than a cap-weighted index fund like those offered by Vanguard or iShares, which charge 0.03–0.05% annually, but GUSA's advocates argue that the systematic outperformance from the selection rules justifies the difference.

The fund trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) during regular market hours with solid liquidity, though trading volume is less than that of the most popular broad-market index ETFs. Goldman Sachs benefits from users adopting GUSA because it anchors a broader ecosystem of factor-based and quantitative products and deepens relationships with advisors and institutional clients. For investors, GUSA offers a middle ground: more disciplined than an active manager's discretion but more tailored than a simple market-cap index.

## The strategy's edge and its limits

The case for GUSA rests on two pillars: that Goldman's quantitative models have genuine predictive power for stock returns, and that those models will continue to work when applied systematically and transparently. Both are contestable. Decades of academic research show that simple factor models—scores based on value, earnings growth, profitability, and momentum—do have some explanatory power for future returns; Goldman's models are more sophisticated versions of such approaches. But markets adapt; once a strategy is publicized and widely followed, its edge can flatten as competitors replicate it and prices adjust.

GUSA's quarterly rebalancing also introduces timing risk. If Goldman's models are strongest at identifying mispriced stocks in the short term, holding them for a quarter dulls that advantage. If other investors are front-running the rebalancing or quickly pricing in the same fundamental signals, GUSA may be buying yesterday's winner and selling yesterday's loser. The fund documents its turnover (the fraction of holdings that change each year), which is meaningful but not extreme—investors considering GUSA should review these figures and tax implications carefully.

## How to evaluate GUSA

Start with the fund's prospectus and the detailed explanation of its selection methodology on Goldman Sachs' website. Review the historical returns relative to broader market benchmarks (the Russell 1000 Index is a natural comparison) and against other rules-based, factor-tilted funds to gauge whether GUSA's outperformance, if any, has been real and persistent or driven by timing. Watch the expense ratio and compare it to other factor-based offerings; as competition in this space has intensified, costs have fallen industry-wide, so GUSA may not always be the cheapest option.

Track GUSA's holdings and notice which sectors and styles dominate, then ask whether that composition aligns with your own investment outlook. If the fund is heavy in financials or value stocks during a year when growth is soaring, that is not a flaw in execution—it is the fund's design—but you should understand whether you are comfortable owning that tilt. Finally, as with all ETFs, price matters; GUSA trades at a premium or discount to its underlying [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) depending on supply and demand, so compare the quoted market price to the fund's daily published net-asset-value before placing a large order.
