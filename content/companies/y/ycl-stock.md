ProShares created its inverse [crude oil](/crude-oil/) fund in response to investor demand for simple, transparent tools to profit from falling energy prices. Before such funds existed, the only practical ways to short oil required access to the futures market — complex, expensive, and out of reach for most retail investors. YCL arrived as part of a broad wave of [inverse ETF](/inverse-etf/) launches, filling a gap by letting ordinary brokerage account holders take a bearish stance on crude without [margin calls](/margin-call-forex/) or rolling contracts.

## The origin: filling a hedging gap

Energy companies, airlines, petrochemical manufacturers, and refineries all rely on crude oil for their core operations. For decades, the only way to hedge oil price risk was through the commodity futures market — expensive, illiquid for small positions, and restricted to professional traders and large institutions with access to futures [brokers](/broker/). Retail investors had no straightforward mechanism to express a bearish view on oil or to protect against rising energy prices.

ProShares recognized this gap and launched a suite of inverse commodity funds in the mid-2000s, including YCL, to make bearish commodity exposure accessible to ordinary investors holding ordinary brokerage accounts. The timing proved important: crude oil prices oscillated dramatically in the decade that followed, from lows near ten dollars per barrel to peaks above one hundred, creating persistent demand from investors seeking downside protection and tactical shorting tools.

## How YCL operates today

The fund holds a portfolio of crude oil [futures contracts](/futures-contract/) and related derivatives, continuously rebalanced to maintain a negative one-times exposure to the daily percentage move in crude oil prices. When oil falls, YCL tends to rise; when oil climbs, YCL falls. The rebalancing happens every trading day, which means the fund is designed to deliver approximately the inverse of the daily return, not necessarily the inverse of the total return over longer periods.

YCL follows the same daily-reset structure as other inverse commodity funds in the ProShares family. It does not hold physical crude, does not take delivery of barrels, and does not speculate in actual oil markets — instead, it uses financial instruments to create an economic exposure that tracks the opposite direction of crude oil prices in the near term. This structure is what enables the fund to exist within a standard ETF wrapper that any investor can buy in a brokerage account.

## The shifting energy landscape

From its inception through today, YCL has operated against a backdrop of profound changes in global energy markets. The rise of tight oil and shale drilling expanded U.S. crude production and loosened OPEC's grip on global pricing. Renewable energy adoption began displacing oil demand in power generation and transportation. Geopolitical shocks — sanctions, regional conflicts, supply disruptions — continued to create sharp price swings. Through all of this, the fundamental mechanics of YCL remained unchanged: financial derivatives structured to move opposite to crude oil's daily price.

Investors used YCL for different purposes as conditions evolved. During periods of rapid oil price increases, some held YCL as insurance against further energy cost [inflation](/inflation/). When oil seemed overvalued or when weakness in manufacturing suggested lower demand ahead, others used it as a tactical bearish bet. Airlines and transportation companies sometimes held positions as a hedge. The fund's consistency — its predictable daily inverse mechanics — made it a useful tool for expressing temporary views without committing to the complexity of futures trading.

## The compounding and decay challenge

Despite its conceptual simplicity, YCL's actual long-term performance behavior is more complex than many investors anticipate. Because the fund resets daily, its multi-day returns depend on the path of oil prices, not just where oil starts and ends. If crude falls sharply on Monday and rises sharply on Tuesday, even if it ends near where it started, YCL will have gained and then lost, leaving it worse off than the simple inverse math would suggest.

Over longer [holding periods](/holding-period/), volatility in crude oil prices tends to erode YCL's returns — a phenomenon called decay or slippage. High volatility accelerates this decay. In very quiet markets, where oil drifts gently in one direction, YCL's tracking remains tight. In choppy markets, the decay compounds more quickly. Investors holding YCL for weeks or months often discover that its total return is much worse than they expected given the actual movement in crude prices.

Additionally, the fund charges an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) to cover its operations. These costs accumulate and further reduce returns, particularly for investors holding the fund over extended periods.

## The present state and how to research it

Today, YCL continues to offer what it always has: a liquid, simple way to take a bearish commodity stance without trading futures. The fund remains part of the broader ProShares inverse ETF suite and continues to track crude oil prices through daily-reset derivatives.

Anyone considering YCL should begin by understanding the crude oil market itself: geopolitical risks, OPEC decisions, shale production trends, refinery utilization, storage levels, and the macroeconomic drivers of demand all influence prices. Reviewing the U.S. Energy Information Administration's weekly reports on crude inventory and production provides essential context.

YCL's prospectus and fact sheet (SEC CIK 0001415311) detail the fund's holdings, expense ratio, and historical tracking error — the actual gap between YCL's performance and a perfect mathematical inverse of crude prices. Compare this historical tracking error against the volatility of crude over your intended holding period to estimate how much decay you should expect. Because of the daily reset mechanism, YCL is fundamentally a short-term tactical tool; holding it for months or years requires accepting significant slippage as a cost of the simplicity it offers.
