The YOKE Core ETF, trading on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) under the ticker YOKE, is an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) designed around a deliberate premise: rather than holding a static allocation to a fixed set of holdings, the portfolio adjusts its positioning based on the current market environment and the outlook for the next several quarters. Launched in 2025 by Empowered Funds with sub-advisory input from Smart Money Group, the fund seeks capital appreciation by deploying approximately one-third of its assets to each of three distinct strategies—quality assessment, risk-on positioning, and risk-off positioning—and then adjusting those allocations in response to market moves and the managers' outlook.

This is an active fund in the deepest sense. The managers are not building a passive tracker of an index. They are making directional bets about where equity markets are heading and positioning the portfolio accordingly. The strategy reflects a conviction that tactical shifting between growth and defensive positioning can reduce losses during downturns and capture the rewards of rallies, thereby smoothing total returns over a full [market cycle](/market-cycle/).

The fund invests primarily in domestic equities (around ninety-three percent U.S. stocks), with a small foreign allocation (roughly seven percent). It holds roughly 107 securities across those allocations. The securities can include ordinary stocks, [real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/), [American Depositary Receipts](/adr/), and other equity instruments. What ties them together is not a rigid definition but the managers' view of which equities are positioned favorably given the current regime.

The quality-assessment piece looks for companies with strong fundamentals: profitable operations, reasonable valuations, clean [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/), and management teams that have a track record of returning cash to shareholders. In every market regime—boom or bust—there are stocks trading at reasonable prices with clean operations. The quality bucket is meant to capture those stocks across the full market, reducing the chance that the portfolio is forced to hold expensive garbage just because the broader market is frothy.

The risk-on positioning is typically deployed when the outlook is favorable: economic growth is accelerating, earnings expectations are rising, and investor sentiment is buoyant. In these periods, the managers tilt the portfolio toward the highest-beta, highest-growth stocks in the market—the companies whose stock prices rise the most in a rally. This positioning captures the bulk of the gains when equity markets are advancing.

The risk-off positioning flips the script. When [recession](/recession/) risks are rising, earnings are expected to decline, or sentiment is turning fearful, the managers shift into defensive stocks: companies with stable earnings, pricing power, [dividend](/dividend/) histories, and lower sensitivity to economic cycles. Utilities, consumer staples, health care, and financials tend to perform better during downturns than technology and consumer discretionary. The risk-off positioning buys time and capital preservation when the broader market is under pressure.

The active allocation between these three strategies is where the skill claim lies. The managers are trying to call the cycle: to move into risk-on when the cycle is early, hold it through the middle, and shift to risk-off before the downturn hits hardest. This requires both accurate judgment about the macro environment and the discipline to act when crowded sentiment pushes in the opposite direction. In practice, market-timing is notoriously difficult, and funds that attempt it often whipsaw—shifting out just before the market rallies, then shifting back in just after. Whether YOKE's managers avoid those mistakes is an empirical question that depends on their execution.

The fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) sits at thirty basis points (0.30%), which is roughly competitive for an actively managed U.S. equity fund and notably below the average for its category. This reflects a cost structure that is lean relative to the active management being delivered. The fund is reasonably liquid, trading on NASDAQ with daily volume sufficient for most investors to enter and exit without meaningful slippage.

The key risk is that tactical positioning simply does not work. Decades of academic research suggest that most active managers, even skilled ones, struggle to beat passive indices after fees over long periods. Tactical allocation—shifting in and out of asset classes or from growth to defensive positioning—is perhaps the hardest form of active management to execute consistently, because it requires predicting cycles accurately and often requires acting contrarian to prevailing sentiment. If the managers make poor judgment calls about when to shift, the portfolio will lag a simple passive core fund that just holds the market. In good markets, risk-off positioning will drag. In bad markets, if the managers shifted too late or not far enough, the portfolio will still suffer large losses.

A second risk is volatility. Even with the [diversification](/diversification/) across three strategies, the fund is still heavily U.S. equity-focused and will move with broad equity markets. In a severe [bear market](/bear-market/), the fund could easily decline twenty percent or more. The "risk-off" positioning is designed to cushion the blow, but it is not a guarantee.

An investor considering YOKE should think carefully about whether they believe the managers can call macro cycles better than the market can. If the answer is yes, then the fund's fee of 0.30% is reasonable compensation for that skill and could generate meaningful alpha. If the answer is no—if market-timing is essentially impossible—then the investor is better served by a low-cost passive equity fund that does not attempt to predict cycles. Over a one-year period, the fund's performance will vary widely depending on how well the managers' calls aligned with what actually happened. Over a full market cycle (five years or more), the persistent ability to time shifts between risk-on and risk-off is what differentiates a skilled active manager from a lucky one.

Research the fund by reviewing its prospectus, examining its actual holdings, and looking at its performance relative to a simple [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) fund over complete market cycles (bull and bear markets). Ask whether the managers shifted defensively before the last two or three market corrections, or whether they were caught holding full allocations to growth stocks. Past performance does not guarantee future results, but patterns of consistent good (or bad) judgment reveal something about process. Watch the fund's turnover: constant trading suggests the managers are whipsawing rather than sticking to a coherent view.
