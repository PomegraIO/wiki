> "Twenty-year Treasuries sit in a curious middle ground — longer than the benchmark ten-year note, shorter than the 30-year long bond, and far more volatile than intermediate maturities."

UTWY holds US [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) maturing in approximately twenty years, placing it at the long end of the Treasury curve but not quite at the extreme. Twenty-year [bonds](/bond/) are substantially more sensitive to interest-rate moves than shorter maturities, making the fund a tool for investors taking an explicit view on where long-term rates are heading or seeking the yield that extended duration can offer.

## The twenty-year maturity and rate sensitivity

The defining characteristic of twenty-year Treasuries is their duration — a measure of how much their price will move if [interest rates](/interest-rate/) shift. A one percentage point change in the twenty-year yield typically translates to a movement of roughly fifteen to eighteen percent in the price of the bonds. This is far more dramatic than the two or three percent moves in shorter-maturity notes, and it means UTWY itself will experience meaningful price swings in volatile rate environments.

That volatility is not a defect of the fund; it is the entire point. Investors buy long-duration bonds when they believe interest rates will fall (and thus prices will rise) or when they have a long-term liability to match and want to immunize against rate changes by buying bonds of similar maturity. A pension plan expecting obligations decades out might use twenty-year Treasuries to hedge; an investor who is bearish on near-term economic growth might buy them expecting the Fed to cut rates.

The flip side is that rising-rate environments can be brutal for twenty-year bond prices. If the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) begins a sustained tightening cycle or if [inflation](/inflation/) picks up unexpectedly, long-duration bonds will take losses. The fund's price can easily fall ten, fifteen, or twenty percent in such scenarios, even though the underlying Treasury bonds are risk-free in the credit sense. This duration risk is the reason the fund carries a yield premium — it compensates investors for the real volatility they are taking on.

## How the fund maintains its target maturity

UTWY focuses on Treasury bonds in the eighteen-to-twenty-year maturity range and gradually rolls the portfolio as bonds age and new issuance comes available. The Treasury issues twenty-year bonds less frequently than two-year or ten-year notes, so the fund may also hold nineteen-year or twenty-one-year bonds to maintain [diversification](/diversification/) and smooth its index construction. As bonds approach the target maturity range, the fund sells them and buys fresh securities, managing turnover to keep expenses low.

The fund's yield will typically exceed that of shorter-maturity Treasury ETFs by a meaningful amount — sometimes by more than one percentage point — reflecting the additional [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) and the opportunity cost of locking capital up for two decades. But that yield is not guaranteed; it depends on the shape of the [yield curve](/yield-curve/) at any given moment.

## Price swings and what they mean for investors

Because bond prices fall as rates rise, holding UTWY through a period of Fed tightening or [inflation surprises](/inflation-surprise/) will result in real losses. An investor who buys at a certain price and then sees rates jump by two percentage points might see the fund's price decline by twenty-five or thirty percent, even though the underlying Treasuries are still backed by the US government and will ultimately mature and return face value.

This is where duration risk becomes personal. A twenty-year bond investor who must sell before maturity in a rising-rate environment will lock in a loss. But an investor who can hold to maturity (or who is explicitly trying to profit from falling rates) benefits from the leverage that duration provides. A one percentage point fall in the twenty-year yield can produce a fifteen percent gain in bond prices — far larger than the return from the coupon alone.

## Who this fund is for and how to use it

UTWY is useful for long-term investors with liabilities or time horizons extending into the next decade or two, for those managing a bond portfolio who want systematic exposure to the long end of the curve, and for tactical investors betting that rates will fall. It is not useful for those expecting rising rates or for investors who need principal stability in the near term. The fund is also not appropriate as a cash-equivalent holding or as the core of a low-volatility portfolio; its place is as a deliberate duration bet or as a matching tool for long-dated obligations.

Tax-wise, like all Treasury funds, it generates ordinary income distributions that are taxable in most accounts. For taxable investors, the fund is best held in a tax-advantaged account like an IRA, where the daily rebalancing and distributions do not create tax drag.

Understanding the trade-off between the twenty-year yield and the [volatility risk](/volatility-risk/) is essential. Reading the fund's fact sheet for its duration, its holdings, and its recent yield, combined with a view on the Fed and the economic outlook, is the starting point for deciding whether the long end of the curve is worth owning at a given moment.
