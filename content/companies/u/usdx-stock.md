> *The paradox of factor investing is that it works precisely because most investors do not believe in it.*

The SGI Enhanced Core ETF attempts to beat a traditional market-cap-weighted index by applying quantitative screens for quality and value. Rather than owning the five-hundred largest U.S. companies in strict proportion to their market caps, USDX owns a slimmer, reweighted portfolio tilted toward companies with stronger balance sheets, steadier earnings, lower valuations, and higher cash generation. It is not an active fund managed by a team of stock-pickers; it is a rules-based quantitative approach hardwired into an index.

The mandate is to hold large-cap U.S. stocks — the universe is the largest 1,000 or so companies by market cap — but to concentrate in those that pass specific screens. A company might be eliminated if its debt-to-equity ratio is too high, if it is losing money, or if its price is stretched relative to its earnings. Another company gets a heavier weight if it is trading cheaply and generating robust free cash flow. The methodology rebalances quarterly, capturing new data and recalibrating the tilts.

The appeal is straightforward in theory. A century of academic research argues that value (cheap companies) and quality (profitable, low-leverage firms) have delivered excess returns over very long periods. If true, a fund that systematically buys value and quality and avoids overpriced trash should outpace an index that owns everything in market-cap proportion. The fund's name — Enhanced Core — signals that ambition: core U.S. equity exposure, enhanced through disciplined filtering.

## How the screening works and why it matters

The exact screens are proprietary to the index sponsor, but common ingredients include earnings quality (do profits convert to cash?), profitability (return on capital, margin trends), balance-sheet strength (debt levels, working-capital efficiency), and valuation metrics (price-to-earnings, price-to-book). A company scoring high on these dimensions gets overweighted; one scoring low gets underweighted or excluded entirely.

The rebalancing discipline is the hidden advantage. A stock can soar, shift from cheap to expensive, and get automatically trimmed by the index, forcing the fund to sell high. Conversely, a beaten-down company that still earns strong cash flow gets bought on weakness. That mechanical sell-high, buy-low behavior is foreign to most investors, yet it is how the best long-term market participants act. A quantitative index codifies that discipline.

## Performance, costs, and the factor-betting gamble

USDX's performance relative to the broad market depends on whether the factors it targets — value, quality, cash generation — are in or out of favor. In the 2010s, growth stocks vastly outpaced value; a fund tilted toward value and quality lagged badly during that decade. In 2022, as high-growth tech crashed, quality and value rebounded, and factor funds rallied. An investor in USDX has made an implicit bet that quality and value will outpace expensive growth over the next several years.

The expense ratio is modest but higher than a passive market-cap-weighted index. The extra cost is the price of the screens and rebalancing. Returns must exceed costs for the strategy to add value; in flat markets or when quality underperforms, net returns can lag a cheaper alternative.

Liquidity is strong — USDX trades with tight spreads — and tracking error is low. The fund's performance versus its index is usually within 1–2 basis points annually.

## What can go wrong

The most obvious risk is factor failure. If expensive growth stocks keep dominating for another decade, quality and value investors will underperform and regret their positioning. That is not a fund failure; it is a market regime where the selected factors do not deliver.

Concentration is another hazard. By excluding high-leverage firms and expensive stocks, the fund may own a narrower band of companies than the broad index. A downturn that hits all large-cap stocks equally, or that disproportionately affects the fund's specific holdings, can magnify losses.

Finally, there is the risk of engineering failure. The screens are only as good as the data they use. If the index provider misclassifies leverage or profit quality, the methodology can select bad companies while rejecting good ones. That is rare but possible.

## Who USDX suits, and how to evaluate it

USDX is for investors who believe in the long-term outperformance of value and quality, can tolerate underperformance in growth-heavy bull markets, and want a systematic, transparent approach to factor tilting. It works well as a core U.S. holding or as a complement to a growth-focused international fund.

Research starts with the prospectus and the index methodology document, which spell out the selection rules and rebalancing cadence. Comparing the fund's holdings to the S&P 500 is instructive — the absence of the most expensive tech giants and the overweight to industrials and financials show the quality tilt at work. Tracking the fund's performance versus a broad index over a full market cycle reveals whether the factor bet is paying off or dragging. A reader should be comfortable holding it for at least five years, because value and quality cycles come in waves longer than quarterly earnings calls.
