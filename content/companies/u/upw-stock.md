ProShares Ultra Utilities — UPW — is a [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) that targets a simple goal: deliver twice the daily return of the Dow Jones U.S. Utilities Index. The fund trades on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) and holds a concentrated portfolio of U.S. electric utilities, gas utilities, water companies, and related infrastructure-heavy businesses. Like all daily-leverage products, it is built for short-term traders, not buy-and-hold investors.

The underlying index tracks companies that generate, distribute, or supply essential services — electricity, [natural gas](/natural-gas/), and water. These are typically mature, regulated businesses with stable cash flows and [dividend yields](/dividend-yield/) higher than the overall [stock market](/stock-market/). The index focuses on large-cap names, so UPW's holdings include familiar utility companies recognized across most of North America.

## The sector that owns the leverage

Utilities are a low-volatility sector. Companies in this space tend to move less dramatically than the overall market because their earnings are predictable and their prices are regulated or heavily scrutinized by public service commissions. That low volatility is actually a double-edged sword for a leveraged ETF. Volatility decay — the silent cost that accumulates when leverage compounds over time — tends to be less severe in stable sectors than in volatile ones. A fund tracking utilities may drift less far from 2 times the index's true return than a leveraged tech fund would.

But low volatility also means lower absolute returns. If the utilities index rises 8 percent per year on average, a perfectly executed 2X fund would aim for 16 percent per year — still below the broader stock market's historical returns. Most UPW traders are not after a long-term return; they are timing a sector move.

## Structure and holdings

UPW holds the stocks of roughly 60 to 70 major U.S. utilities, reweighted to deliver the 2X leverage effect. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is higher than a simple utility [index fund](/index-fund/) but in line with other ProShares 2X products. The fund rebalances daily, selling winners and buying losers (or buying more of winners and selling more of losers, depending on daily movements) to keep the leverage target tight. This daily rebalancing is what makes the tracking as good as it is, but it also incurs trading costs that accumulate.

The fund is structurally straightforward: it holds actual stocks and derivatives, not debt, so there is no issuer [credit risk](/credit-risk/) to worry about.

## When UPW makes sense

UPW appeals to traders who believe U.S. utilities are about to outperform over a short window — maybe a few weeks when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) are expected to fall (which is bullish for [bonds](/bond/) and by extension for dividend-paying utilities), or when energy markets tighten and power demand spikes. The leverage amplifies those moves without having to open a margin account at a brokerage.

It is not suitable for:

- Passive, long-term investors in the utility sector (use a regular utility index fund instead).
- Anyone who does not understand daily reset and volatility decay.
- People who want a [dividend](/dividend/) yield (the leverage mechanics work against capturing the full dividend effect).

## Costs and constraints

Beyond the expense ratio, there are two real costs. First, if you hold UPW for long periods, volatility decay siphons away returns. If utilities rally 30 percent over a year but do so with frequent pullbacks, UPW might gain only 55 percent instead of the target 60 percent. Second, the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) (the gap between the buy and sell price at any moment) can be wider than with larger, more heavily traded ETFs. If you need to exit quickly, that spread costs real money.

## The utility sector itself

Before trading UPW, understand what the underlying utilities index actually holds. The large-cap utility sector in the U.S. includes companies that own power plants, transmission lines, and distribution networks; others that are primarily natural gas; and a few water companies. Some are regional (serving one state), others multistate. Regulations vary by state and change slowly. A significant portion of utility earnings comes from regulated rate bases where profits are limited by law but stability is guaranteed. This makes utilities defensive and predictable but also means they are vulnerable to regulatory change and [inflation](/inflation/) squeezes if costs rise faster than rates adjust.

## Reading the holdings and prospects

To research UPW seriously, start with the prospectus and holdings list. See which utility companies are largest and what they do. Then examine the utility sector's interest-rate sensitivity: when rates fall, utilities typically outperform because their high dividend yields become more attractive relative to bonds. When rates rise sharply, utilities can struggle. Energy policy, renewable-energy adoption, and the pace of grid modernization also shape the sector's trajectory. If you believe utilities are cheap and about to rally, UPW is a levered bet on that thesis; just remember it resets daily and works best for bets lasting days to a few weeks, not months or years.
