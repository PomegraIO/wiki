*A [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) is a capital structure by design. **Cousins Properties Inc** (ticker **CUZ**, SEC CIK 25232) owns and manages a portfolio of office buildings, shopping centers, and mixed-use properties concentrated in the Sunbelt, particularly Atlanta. The company's [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/), dividend policy, and debt levels are all choreographed around a simple mandate: generate stable [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) from rents, return much of that flow to [shareholders](/common-stock/) as [dividends](/dividend/), and leverage the portfolio to maximize [return on equity](/return-on-equity/).*

## The REIT Dividend Machine: Cash Flow From Property

A [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) is a company structured to own and operate real property (office buildings, apartments, retail centers, warehouses) and distribute most of its taxable income to [shareholders](/common-stock/) as [dividends](/dividend/). The tax incentive is the glue: if a [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) distributes at least 90% of taxable income to [shareholders](/common-stock/), it pays no [corporate income tax](/corporate-income-tax/). Shareholders pay tax on the [dividends](/dividend/) they receive. This structure encourages long-term holding and yields, since the company returns cash to owners rather than retaining earnings to pay corporate tax.

Cousins' income comes from tenants: office workers, retailers, restaurants, and other businesses that occupy its buildings and pay rent. That rent revenue is stable (multi-year leases protect against near-term volatility), predictable ([vacancy rates](/vacancy-rate-property/) in desirable Sunbelt markets are typically low), and grow modestly over time (lease renewal clauses often include rent escalators). Cousins deducts operating costs (property taxes, utilities, maintenance, insurance, property management staff) and [debt](/corporate-bond/) service (interest on loans backing the properties) and distributes what remains to [shareholders](/common-stock/). The [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) that Cousins generates is therefore a function of rent growth, occupancy rates, operating efficiency, and [interest rates](/interest-rate/) on its [debt](/corporate-bond/).

## Leverage and the REIT Model

Cousins does not own its properties free and clear. Like most [REITs](/real-estate-investment-trust/), the company finances [acquisitions](/acquisition/) and operations using a mix of [equity](/common-stock/) (investor capital) and [debt](/corporate-bond/) (bank loans and [bonds](/bond/)). A typical [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) might be 40-60% financed by [debt](/corporate-bond/) and 40-60% financed by [equity](/common-stock/). The leverage amplifies [returns](/return-on-equity/): if a property earns a 5% cap rate (gross [return on equity](/return-on-equity/)) and is financed 50% with [debt](/corporate-bond/) at 3%, the [equity](/common-stock/) [tranche](/tranche/) earns roughly 7%. Leverage is attractive as long as the spread holds and interest rates don't spike.

However, leverage is also a risk. If occupancy falls (tenants leave, office space remains dark), rent income declines, and Cousins' capacity to service [debt](/corporate-bond/) and pay [dividends](/dividend/) shrinks. If interest rates rise significantly, refinancing existing [debt](/corporate-bond/) becomes expensive, pushing up [debt](/corporate-bond/) service costs and squeezing [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/). If property values fall (due to [recession](/recession/), neighborhood decline, or market over-supply), the [equity](/common-stock/) value of Cousins' [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) deteriorates, even if rents and operations remain stable.

## Real-Estate Cycles and Capital Structure Stress

[REITs](/real-estate-investment-trust/) are sensitive to macroeconomic cycles. [Office REITs](/office-reit/), in particular, have faced structural headwinds in recent years: remote work reduced office demand, large tech and financial firms consolidated space and vacated excess locations, and development continued to outpace demand in many markets. A [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) with a portfolio skewed to office space faces pressure on rents, occupancy, and [property valuations](/enterprise-value/). That pressure flows directly to [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) and, therefore, [dividend](/dividend/) capacity.

Cousins, with a diversified portfolio including retail and mixed-use properties as well as office, has some insulation. Retail has also faced headwinds (e-commerce competition, changing consumer behavior), but essential tenants (grocery stores, pharmacies, entertainment venues) provide base-load demand. Mixed-use properties (combining office, retail, and residential in one location) are structurally resilient because they serve multiple customer segments.

Still, any prolonged downturn in real-estate demand, occupancy, or [cap rates](/cap-rate/) would force Cousins to navigate a difficult tradeoff: maintain [dividend](/dividend/) payments (to keep [shareholders](/common-stock/) happy and the [stock price](/stock/) stable), or reduce [dividends](/dividend/) to preserve cash and [debt](/corporate-bond/) service capacity. Most [REITs](/real-estate-investment-trust/) choose the former in mild downturns (they borrow more or sell assets to fund [dividends](/dividend/)) and the latter in severe downturns (they cut [dividends](/dividend/) to avoid default).

## Debt Maturity, Refinancing, and Interest-Rate Risk

Cousins' [debt](/corporate-bond/) portfolio matures on a schedule: certain loans come due in 2025, others in 2027, 2030, and beyond. When a loan matures, Cousins must either repay it in cash (using reserves or asset sales) or refinance it (borrow new money to pay off the old debt). Refinancing is cheaper and more convenient, but it exposes Cousins to [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/). If interest rates have risen since the original loan was issued, new loans will carry higher interest rates, increasing [debt](/corporate-bond/) service costs and reducing [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) available for [dividends](/dividend/).

A [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) manager therefore monitors maturity schedules closely and often locks in interest rates by refinancing early if rates are favorable. Cousins likely maintains a laddered maturity schedule (a mix of short-term and long-term [debt](/corporate-bond/)) to avoid having to refinance large amounts in a single unfavorable year.

## The Dividend as a Signal and Obligation

Cousins' [dividend](/dividend/) is not discretionary like that of a software company or industrial manufacturer. It is a structural consequence of the [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) model: 90% of taxable income must be distributed. Reducing the [dividend](/dividend/) signals to the market that earnings have fallen—either because rents are declining, occupancy is deteriorating, or [debt](/corporate-bond/) service costs are rising. A [dividend](/dividend/) cut often triggers a [stock](/stock/) price decline, since [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) [shareholders](/common-stock/) buy for [dividend](/dividend/) yield, not capital appreciation.

Cousins therefore has strong incentives to maintain the [dividend](/dividend/) even when [cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) is weak. This can involve refinancing [debt](/corporate-bond/), selling non-core assets, or raising new [equity](/common-stock/) capital. Each option has trade-offs: refinancing increases [debt](/corporate-bond/), asset sales shrink the portfolio, and [equity](/common-stock/) raises dilute existing [shareholders](/common-stock/). But the [dividend](/dividend/) remains a sacred obligation.

## Real-Estate Values and [Balance Sheet](/balance-sheet/) Accounting

[REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/) are typically marked to a mix of [historical cost](/historical-cost/) and [fair value](/fair-value/) estimates. Properties are often carried at original purchase price plus capital improvements, even as market values fluctuate. This means a [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) with properties that have appreciated significantly can appear underleveraged on the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) but is actually at risk if market values suddenly fall. Conversely, a [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) with properties that have depreciated may appear overleveraged even if the underlying properties are still cash-generative.

Cousins' [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) and credit metrics must be interpreted against the backdrop of local Atlanta and Sunbelt real-estate values. Strong supply-demand dynamics and population growth in these regions support Cousins' long-term positioning, but cyclical downturns would reveal any structural vulnerabilities.

## Comparative Dividend and Yield

[REITs](/real-estate-investment-trust/) typically yield 3-6%, depending on asset class, leverage, and market conditions. Cousins' [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) places it in the income-oriented [stock](/stock/) category, attracting retirees, insurance companies, and other income-seeking investors. The yield is higher than that of a [stock](/stock/) index (which typically yields 1-2%), compensating investors for [real estate](/real-estate-investment-trust/) illiquidity and [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/).

## Gateway to Understanding Real-Estate Capital Markets

Cousins' capital structure—the interplay of property ownership, [rent](/free-cash-flow/) generation, [debt financing](/corporate-bond/), and [shareholder](/common-stock/) distributions—is the backbone of institutional real-estate investing. The company exemplifies how [leverage](/corporate-bond/), [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/), and [dividend policy](/dividend/) are choreographed in an asset-rich, cash-generative business. Its challenges (refinancing, occupancy cycles, [debt](/corporate-bond/) maturity walls) reflect the eternal tensions of [REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/) management; its resilience (diversified portfolio, Sunbelt location, long tenant relationships) provides a blueprint for survival.
