*The residential mortgage finance market remains cyclical and sensitive to rate movements, and **Cherry Hill Mortgage Investment Corp** (CHMI) sits squarely at that nexus. As a [mortgage REIT](/real-estate-investment-trust/), the firm sources returns by acquiring and holding mortgage-backed securities—bundles of home loans packaged by originators—and managing the mismatches between what it pays to fund those assets and what those assets earn. Understanding CHMI requires tracing how its capital stack is built, how interest-rate hedges reshape its returns, and why mortgage REITs distribute most of what they earn rather than retain it.*

## How the Funding Machine Works

Cherry Hill's business model is purely financial engineering: buy mortgages or mortgage securities with borrowed money, earn the spread, and harvest the difference. The mechanics are simple in principle but require constant recalibration. CHMI issues equity to raise permanent capital, then uses that equity as a cushion while borrowing against its mortgage holdings via repurchase agreements (repo)—short-term loans collateralized by the mortgages themselves. Because repo rates fluctuate with market conditions and CHMI's funding needs, every change in overnight rates or credit spreads ripples through the firm's profitability.

Mortgage REITs are required to distribute at least 90 percent of taxable income to shareholders, which means CHMI cannot reinvest earnings to grow assets. Instead, the firm grows only when it raises new equity. This structural constraint shapes both opportunity and vulnerability: if CHMI's cost of funding (repo rates plus equity raises) rises faster than the yield it earns on mortgages, the gap narrows and returns compress.

## Equity Capital and the Leverage Dynamic

CHMI's equity base is its margin of safety. Shareholders own a fixed pool of capital while the firm uses leverage—borrowing through repo and other secured funding—to amplify returns on that equity. If mortgages yield 3 percent and CHMI borrows at 2 percent, the 1 percent spread multiplied by leverage can yield 8–10 percent on equity. But that math works only if spread remains positive; a funding crisis or portfolio impairment can erase gains quickly.

The firm is transparent about its leverage ratio—the ratio of total mortgage assets to shareholders' equity. In normal environments, mortgage REITs run leverage of 6:1 to 9:1; CHMI has historically operated in that range. Higher leverage magnifies returns in benign markets but also magnifies losses if mortgage prices fall or funding markets seize up.

## Interest-Rate Hedging and the Cost of Certainty

Because CHMI earns its spread primarily from holding long mortgages funded with short-term repo, it faces duration risk: if rates rise sharply, the value of mortgages falls and funding costs spike simultaneously. To mitigate this, CHMI uses interest-rate swaps, swaptions, and other derivatives to hedge its exposures. The hedge is not free—every swaption or swap costs premium that reduces net returns—but it reduces the variance in earnings and protects the equity cushion during shock events.

The hedging decision is a strategic choice balancing stability against upside. Heavily hedged mortgage REITs offer smoother earnings and capital preservation; lightly hedged REITs chase higher returns in stable rate environments but bleed equity during reversals. CHMI typically hedges 50–70 percent of its duration gap, a middle ground that accepts some volatility for retained upside.

## Dividends and Capital Allocation

Because CHMI must pay out 90 percent of taxable income, the dividend is not chosen—it is mechanically determined by earnings. A sharp quarter produces a high dividend; a weak quarter produces a lower one. For investors seeking yield, this volatility is both a feature and a risk: CHMI's dividend can swing 40–50 percent year-over-year based on rate moves, credit performance, and mortgage valuations. The dividend is not stable management's discretion but rather the residual of the underlying mortgage portfolio.

Capital return is thus non-discretionary. If CHMI earns $0.40 per share per quarter, it must distribute roughly $0.36. The only way to enhance shareholder returns is to grow the portfolio by raising new equity, which dilutes existing shareholders' ownership unless the new capital can be deployed at returns above the cost of equity.

## The Cyclical Tailwinds and Headwinds

Mortgage REIT economics reward compressed spreads and high asset volumes. When the Federal Reserve holds rates steady and mortgage rates settle in a narrow band, funding costs stabilize and CHMI can harvest steady carry. When rates begin to move—whether rising or falling sharply—valuations swing and hedging drains returns. The cyclicality creates periods of lush returns (2010–2018) and periods of margin compression (2022–2023 and beyond).

CHMI's entry into prepayment-sensitive mortgages also matters. As mortgage rates fall, borrowers refinance and CHMI's portfolio shrinks—the "negative convexity" problem endemic to fixed-income REITs. CHMI has attempted to address this through agency mortgage exposure (implicitly backed by [Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac](/common-stock/)) and through careful duration management, but it remains a structural headwind when rates decline.

## Balance Sheet and Liquidity

CHMI maintains a liquidity buffer through availability of unsecured funding, cash reserves, and access to its credit facilities. Mortgage REITs carry no debt covenants but face market discipline: as the cost of repo rises or credit spreads widen, CHMI's funding costs increase and returns compress. The firm is also subject to interest-rate risk reportable in its 10-K, where it discloses the sensitivity of net income and equity to a 100-basis-point move in rates.
