*Cadiz Inc. (**CDZI**), a California corporation and US-listed public company registered with the SEC under Central Index Key 727273, operates as a natural-resource holding company owning agricultural land, groundwater rights, and water-delivery infrastructure in the Mojave Desert and surrounding regions of California. The company's filings emphasize its positioning in California's water-scarcity environment, where competition for scarce groundwater and surface-water rights creates economic value for entities holding rights to high-quality, abundant aquifers, and where regulatory frameworks—particularly the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act—govern the extraction and use of groundwater.*

## Core Assets and Water Rights

Cadiz's SEC filings establish the firm's primary assets: agricultural land holdings in the Mojave Desert and basin areas of California, and critically, the groundwater rights appurtenant to those lands. The company discloses the acreage of land it owns, the geological and hydrological characteristics of the aquifers underlying its properties, and the quality and quantity of groundwater accessible through existing and potential wells. These filings signal that Cadiz is fundamentally a real-estate and resource-holding company—its value derives not from operations it conducts but from the scarcity and regional value of the water resources it controls.

The company's regulatory filings detail the legal basis for its water rights: California water law is complex, distinguishing between riparian rights (attached to land adjoining surface water), appropriative rights (permits to extract and use water), and correlative rights (shared rights to underlying groundwater). Cadiz's disclosures describe which categories of rights it holds, whether those rights are senior or junior to other claims, and any regulatory or legal proceedings that might affect the security or enforceability of its rights.

## Water Supply Agreements and Revenue Models

Cadiz's filings disclose its revenue-generating activities, which center on water-supply agreements with California municipalities, water agencies, and agricultural or industrial customers. The company enters into contracts to extract groundwater from its properties, treat it if necessary, and deliver it to customers. These agreements typically specify the volume of water to be delivered annually, the price per acre-foot or per unit volume, and the duration of the contract. The filings reveal the material contracts the company has executed or is negotiating, the creditworthiness of the counterparties, and the duration of commitments.

The company's [10-k](/10-k/) filings disclose realized and contracted revenue from water sales, affording investors visibility into the durability and growth of the company's cash flows. Long-term, fixed-price contracts reduce uncertainty but may undervalue water if scarcity increases; variable-price contracts or periodic renegotiations preserve upside but create renewal risk if customers opt not to continue purchasing from Cadiz.

## Regulatory Environment and Groundwater Management

California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA), enacted in 2014, fundamentally reshaped groundwater regulation in California, requiring local agencies to manage groundwater basins in a manner deemed "sustainable"—extracting no more than can be naturally replenished or stored. Cadiz's filings emphasize how SGMA affects the company's assets and revenue opportunities: if a groundwater basin is deemed over-pumped or unsustainable, the regulatory authorities may impose extraction limits, reducing the amount of water that can be pumped from that basin. Conversely, SGMA creates opportunities for entities holding water rights in well-managed or abundant basins to contract with water-stressed regions seeking additional supplies.

The company discloses its basins' classification status and any local groundwater sustainability agency proceedings affecting its properties. These disclosures reveal whether Cadiz is operating in a favorable regulatory environment (where groundwater is plentiful and sustainable extractions are permitted) or a constrained one (where over-pumping or basin stress might limit the company's future extraction rights).

The company discloses capital expenditures undertaken or planned to develop new wells, maintain existing infrastructure, or optimize delivery systems. ## Agricultural Operations and Diversification

Historically, Cadiz has operated agricultural operations on its land holdings, generating revenue from crop production. The filings disclose agricultural acreage in production, crops grown, sales prices, and any seasonal or cyclical dynamics affecting profitability. However, the company's strategic direction appears increasingly oriented toward water supply rather than agriculture—a shift reflecting the greater economic value of water relative to agricultural commodities in California's water-stressed environment.

The company's disclosures reveal any plans to retire agricultural operations or reduce agricultural acreage as groundwater development accelerates, signaling management's allocation of land and capital toward higher-value water-supply activities.

## Competitive Dynamics and Regional Water Market

Cadiz's filings situate the company within California's regional water market, competing with other water-rights holders, water agencies, and potential alternative sources (such as desalination, recycled wastewater, or transfers from regions with surplus water). The company discloses its competitive positioning—the cost of the water it can deliver relative to alternatives, the reliability of supply, and any regulatory or geographic advantages it enjoys.

The filings also describe the broader water-supply context in the region: drought conditions, competing demands from agricultural, municipal, and environmental interests, and the price dynamics of California's water markets. These contextual factors affect the commercial viability of the company's water-supply projects and the valuations customers are willing to place on long-term contracts.

## Development Projects and Commercial Status

Cadiz's filings detail any major water-supply projects in development—such as the Cardinal project, focused on developing groundwater supplies and conveying them to regional water agencies. Projects that have secured long-term purchase agreements and regulatory approvals progress toward development; those lacking customer commitments or facing regulatory opposition create uncertainty about whether expected cash flows will materialize.

## Risk Factors and Long-Term Viability

Cadiz's SEC filings emphasize risks inherent to owning water rights in a water-scarce region: climate change and mega-drought conditions may reduce groundwater replenishment, making historical extraction rates unsustainable; regulatory changes could tighten extraction limits; environmental groups may challenge water-supply projects on grounds of environmental impact or indigenous-rights concerns; and customers may reduce demand for purchased water if they develop alternative supplies or reduce consumption.
