> *A floating-rate bond moves with interest rates rather than against them — when rates rise, the bondholder earns more, and the bond's price remains stable, a feature that appeals when the rate cycle is turning upward.*

## The structure

The Invesco Variable Rate Investment Grade ETF (VRIG) holds corporate bonds whose interest payments are not fixed but rather reset periodically — typically quarterly — to a new level based on a reference rate plus a spread. The reference rate is usually the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR), though some older bonds in the fund still track the London Interbank Offered Rate (LIBOR), which was phased out through 2023. This mechanics means that as the Federal Reserve raises interest rates, the fund's newly reset coupons rise as well, roughly keeping the bond's market price stable despite the broader decline in bond values that accompanies rising rates.

This is the core appeal: traditional fixed-rate bonds fall sharply in value when rates rise, because an investor holding a 3 per cent bond becomes unhappy watching new issuers offer 5 per cent. A floating-rate bond, by contrast, reprices automatically. The investor receives more interest, and the market price of the bond stays near par. That stability comes at a cost — the initial yield on a floating-rate bond is typically lower than a comparable fixed-rate bond, because the holder gives up the upside of locking in today's rate if rates were to fall.

## What is in VRIG?

VRIG holds investment-grade corporate floating-rate notes issued by banks, industrial companies, utilities, financial institutions, and other established borrowers. The credit quality is constrained to investment grade — ratings of BBB or higher — which filters out the riskiest borrowers but still includes a meaningful slice of companies that are not quite blue-chip. The portfolio spans dozens of issuers and hundreds of distinct securities, with duration typically very short (a year or less), because floating-rate bonds reprice so frequently that interest-rate risk is minimal.

The fund trades on NASDAQ with moderate volume and tight spreads for most trade sizes. Distributions are paid monthly as the resetting coupons accrue.

## When does VRIG shine, and when does it falter?

VRIG is most attractive in a rising-rate environment. When the Federal Reserve is hiking rates, the fund's distribution rises in step, boosting income, and the price remains stable. This contrasts sharply with a traditional bond fund, which experiences both a rising yield (good) and a falling net asset value as existing bonds lose relative appeal (bad). A floating-rate fund avoids that capital loss.

Conversely, VRIG underperforms in a falling-rate environment. When rates drop, the coupons reset lower, so income declines. More importantly, the bonds do not appreciate the way fixed-rate bonds do when rates fall. An investor holding fixed-rate bonds is holding a depreciating asset that suddenly becomes more valuable. A floating-rate holder misses that capital gain.

Over a full interest-rate cycle, the performance trade-off is complex. The fund's lower yield in rising-rate periods and lower capital appreciation in falling-rate periods can mean it lags a diversified bond strategy that includes both fixed-rate and floating-rate positions. The real advantage is precise matching: an investor certain that rates are going to rise can use VRIG to sidestep the capital losses that fixed-rate bonds would suffer.

## Credit risk

Because VRIG holds investment-grade corporate bonds, the fund is exposed to corporate credit risk: if an issuer downgrades or defaults, the fund's net asset value falls. This risk is lower than in a high-yield fund, but it is real. A broad recession that prompts ratings downgrades among corporate issuers would harm VRIG's value.

Liquidity risk is also worth noting. Floating-rate corporate bonds are less liquid than Treasuries or even fixed-rate corporates; some of the bonds in the fund trade infrequently. If the fund experiences heavy redemptions or if credit markets seize up, selling a large position in a less-liquid bond can be difficult.

## Who holds VRIG?

VRIG suits investors who expect interest rates to rise and who want to minimize the capital losses that fixed-rate bonds suffer in that scenario. It is typically held as a tactical position — a temporary shift to floating-rate bonds when rate-hike cycles begin — rather than a permanent core holding. In periods of stable or falling rates, a traditional bond fund will often outperform. For this reason, VRIG is more appropriate for experienced investors or advisers who can time the rate cycle with some confidence than for buy-and-hold investors seeking set-and-forget bond exposure.

Those researching VRIG should review the fund's fact sheet and holdings list on Invesco's website, which show the maturity schedule, credit quality breakdown, and the reference-rate composition (SOFR-linked versus legacy LIBOR-linked bonds). Comparing performance to a fixed-rate bond fund like AGG or VPLS over various interest-rate environments reveals the precise tradeoff. Understanding the mechanics of coupon resets and SOFR is essential; the Federal Reserve publishes SOFR methodology and values, and bond market resources like SIFMA or the Treasury market data sites provide educational material.
