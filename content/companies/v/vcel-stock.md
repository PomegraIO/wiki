Vericel manufactures and sells cell therapy products for patients with severe wounds, cartilage damage, and extensive burns. The products are derived from the patient's own tissue—skin or cartilage—grown in the company's manufacturing facilities and returned as a therapeutic graft. This is regenerative medicine without drugs: the patient becomes part of the manufacturing chain. Shares trade on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) as VCEL.

## The product line: biology as a process

Vericel operates three main franchises, each built on the same fundamental idea: extract cells from a patient, expand them in culture, embed them in a scaffold, and send back a living graft.

APLIGRAF is a skin substitute for severe wounds—burns, diabetic ulcers, chronic venous ulcers—where simple bandaging fails. Clinicians take a skin biopsy from the patient (from an unburned area if the patient is burned), send it to Vericel, and the company cultures both the epidermis and dermis layers. Within weeks, Apligraf ships back: a living, metabolically active skin equivalent that can integrate into the patient's body and accelerate healing.

OrCel is the cartilage equivalent. For patients with knee or ankle damage from injury or osteoarthritis, clinicians biopsy healthy cartilage, Vericel expands the cells, and the company produces a implant that can be surgically placed into the defect. Because the cells come from the patient's own tissue, the immune system treats it as self, not foreign—no rejection risk, no lifelong immunosuppression.

GINTUIT is used in periodontal surgery—reconstructing gum and bone tissue lost to advanced disease. Same model: patient tissue, culture, graft, implant.

What ties these together is not the indication but the operational core: controlled manufacturing of living human cells at scale. Vericel is not a synthetic-drug maker; it is a cellular production facility.

## How the economics work

These products generate revenue in a very different pattern than typical pharmaceuticals. Each graft is made to order, from the individual patient's tissue. That means no inventory, no batch production, no economies of scale in the way pill manufacturing has them. Instead, revenue scales with the number of patient treatments the company processes. Reimbursement comes from hospitals and payers who cover the procedure, typically ranging from several thousand to tens of thousands of dollars per graft depending on the indication, complexity, and geography.

The business is genuinely recurring in the sense that once a hospital or surgeon adopts Vericel products, they reorder with each eligible patient. But it requires consistent clinical adoption, reimbursement coverage, and the operational discipline to execute thousands of manufacturing runs per year without errors, contamination, or delays. A single failed graft—cells that die in culture, contamination, manufacturing error—represents a lost sale and a patient setback.

Margins depend on manufacturing efficiency and scale. Vericel has invested heavily in centralizing production to a few large facilities, which improves utilization and reduces per-unit costs. Scaling volume is the path to profitability; low-volume specialty use will never sustain the business.

## The challenge: convincing the market

Vericel's products work—clinical evidence for all three is substantial—but the adoption curve has been frustratingly slow. Surgeons and hospital purchasing departments are risk-averse. A new graft technology competes not just on efficacy but on surgeon familiarity, hospital protocol, reimbursement coverage, and the switching cost of changing what they already use. A surgeon trained on a competing product, or simply comfortable with the standard approach, will not switch without strong clinical evidence or pressure from patients.

Reimbursement is a gate. If insurance companies or government programs decline to cover a product, hospitals will not buy it regardless of clinical merit. Vericel has fought constant battles to maintain reimbursement levels and to expand coverage into new indications and geographies. Even in the United States, where coverage is relatively broad, the company faces ongoing pressure from payers demanding price concessions.

The second challenge is geographic. These products are FDA-approved in the United States and have regulatory pathways in Europe, but they are not sold globally with uniform labeling or reimbursement. Expanding into new countries requires separate regulatory submissions, new manufacturing certifications, and often new reimbursement negotiations. This limits the addressable market compared to drugs that can scale internationally on one regulatory approval.

## Operational execution and risks

Because each graft is custom-manufactured from patient cells, quality control is paramount. A manufacturing failure destroys both the sale and the patient outcome. Vericel has invested in automation and standardization to reduce errors, but the inherent complexity of biological manufacturing means risk never goes to zero.

The company is also sensitive to supply-chain disruptions. It relies on incoming tissue biopsies from hospitals, specialized culture media and reagents from suppliers, and logistics to ship the finished graft back within a narrow time window. A supplier shortage, transportation delay, or facility issue can cascade quickly into lost treatments.

Competition is emerging. Academic medical centers are developing in-house cell processing capabilities; larger medical-device companies are entering regenerative medicine; and other cell-therapy firms are pursuing similar approaches in different indications. Vericel's moat is its installed base of surgeons and hospital relationships, its manufacturing expertise, and its clinical data—not intellectual property that lasts forever.

## How to research Vericel

Start with the 10-K (SEC CIK 0000887359) for detailed revenue by product, geographic breakdown, and a candid risk discussion. Quarterly earnings calls are crucial: listen for trends in unit volume (number of procedures), average selling price, gross margin, and reimbursement commentary. Key metrics include procedures per quarter (the volume driver), revenue per procedure (reimbursement trends), gross margin (manufacturing efficiency), and cash burn (the company may not be free-cash-flow positive yet).

Watch for new indications in clinical trials and regulatory approvals—these are the long-term growth drivers. Also track reimbursement changes in major markets; a cut in a key code or delisting would be material. Competitors and new clinical evidence from rival products should be on the watch list.
