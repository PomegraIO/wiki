*The **Vanguard Mortgage-Backed Securities ETF** (NYSE: VMBS) holds a broad portfolio of US mortgage-backed securities, the standardised debt instruments created when banks pool home loans and sell them to investors. It is a low-cost way for retail and institutional investors to hold residential mortgage exposure without buying individual securities, and it sits at the centre of the broader fixed-income landscape.*

## What VMBS holds

VMBS tracks the Bloomberg Aggregate MBS Index, which captures the universe of investment-grade mortgage-backed securities issued or guaranteed by US government-sponsored enterprises — principally Ginnie Mae (Government National Mortgage Association), Fannie Mae (Federal National Mortgage Association), and Freddie Mac (Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation). These are not individual home loans; they are standardised debt tranches backed by pools of tens of thousands of mortgages. When a homeowner takes out a mortgage, the bank originates the loan, then bundles it with others and sells the pool to the secondary market. These mortgage pools become the securities that VMBS holds.

The index itself contains the broadest segment of the US mortgage market — agency-backed securities with investment-grade ratings. This is distinct from non-agency MBS (mortgages without government backing) and subprime securities (which triggered the 2008 financial crisis but are a small corner of today's market). VMBS concentrates on the largest, most liquid, and most actively traded portion of the landscape: the mortgages that the government has explicitly guaranteed or supported through its housing agencies.

Each holding in VMBS pays monthly or semi-annual interest, plus principal repayment, as the underlying homeowners make their mortgage payments. This cash flow is the fuel that makes VMBS attractive to income-focused investors and institutions managing fixed-income exposure.

## Cost, structure, and liquidity

VMBS operates as a standard exchange-traded fund — no leverage, no inverse mechanics, plain vanilla. It holds the actual securities (not swaps or derivatives), so dividend payments from the fund track the pass-through of interest and principal from the mortgages themselves. The expense ratio is vanishingly small, a fraction of 0.1% annually, making it one of the cheapest ways to gain mortgage exposure.

Because VMBS is listed on the New York Stock Exchange, it trades like a stock during regular market hours. This liquidity matters: investors can buy and sell shares at any point the exchange is open, with bid-ask spreads that are typically very tight given the fund's size and the underlying index's deep liquidity. Large institutions use VMBS as a core holding in fixed-income allocations because it is cheap, liquid, and transparent.

The fund reinvests or distributes the income from the underlying mortgages on a regular basis — typically as a monthly distribution — so holders receive a steady yield. The actual yield depends on the level of prevailing interest rates and the coupon rates on the mortgages the fund holds; when rates are higher, newly originated mortgages tend to carry higher coupons, so new pools entering the market pay more, but existing pools in the fund pay their original rate regardless.

## Duration, interest-rate risk, and prepayment

The core driver of VMBS's total return is not income but price movement, which is a function of interest rates and prepayment behaviour. Mortgage-backed securities carry significant duration risk: when interest rates fall sharply, homeowners refinance, the securities are repaid early, and the portfolio is forced to reinvest the proceeds at lower rates. Conversely, when rates rise, prepayments slow, duration extends, and the price of existing holdings falls more than conventional bonds would.

This is the prepayment option — a structural feature of mortgages. Unlike a traditional bond, where the bondholder holds the security until maturity, a mortgage can be paid off early without penalty. In a falling-rate environment, this works against VMBS shareholders: the fund captures less of the price appreciation that pure fixed-rate bonds would enjoy. In a rising-rate environment, the extension risk can be painful — holders lock in losses as rates move against them and the expected payoff date recedes.

The Bloomberg Aggregate MBS Index manages this to some degree by holding a mix of mortgage coupons and vintages, so no single prepayment scenario dominates. But VMBS is not immune to duration and prepayment risk; these are endemic to mortgages. Investors should understand that holding VMBS is not the same as holding a treasury bond or corporate bond of similar stated maturity — the true duration can shift with market conditions.

## Who holds VMBS and why

VMBS appeals to several cohorts. Institutional investors — pension funds, endowments, insurance companies — hold it as a core fixed-income allocation, partly because of the government backing (which reduces credit risk to near-zero) and partly because mortgages offer a yield premium over treasuries of comparable duration. Individual investors seeking bond exposure without credit risk might use VMBS as a way to access mortgage pools without the friction of buying individual securities. Financial advisors often include VMBS in balanced or income-focused portfolios as a low-cost, diversified mortgage hedge.

The fund also appears in broader bond indices, so any investor holding a total-bond-market ETF owns a slice of VMBS indirectly.

## How to research VMBS

Start with the fund prospectus and fact sheet on Vanguard's website, which lay out the index methodology, the current average duration, the yield, and the distribution frequency. The Bloomberg Aggregate MBS Index's own methodology document explains the inclusion criteria and weighting scheme. For deeper research, the FINRA Bond Center publishes prices and yields for agency MBS, and the secondary mortgage market data providers (SIFMA, MBS.com) publish pool-level details. The US Treasury's mortgage market analysis and Federal Reserve statements on mortgage interest rates provide macroeconomic context for how VMBS's returns are likely to behave in different rate environments. Anyone evaluating VMBS should understand the fund's interest-rate sensitivity — its duration — and compare it against their own expectations for where rates are heading and where they are in the cycle.
