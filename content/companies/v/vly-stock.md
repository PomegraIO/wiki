Valley National Bancorp is a mid-sized regional bank holding company headquartered in Paramus, New Jersey, that operates [Valley Bank](/wiki/public-company/) across its core markets in the New York City metro area, New Jersey, and Florida. Like most regional banks, its core business is taking deposits and making loans—predominantly in commercial real estate, a sector that exposes the firm to both cyclical property market risks and rising interest-rate pressures that squeeze lending margins.

## The path from local New Jersey fixture to multistate operator

Valley Bank was founded in 1983 as a small community lender in Bergen County, New Jersey, during the early 1980s savings-and-loan crisis. While larger national players were retreating from commercial real estate, Valley began building a local franchise around serving small and mid-sized businesses and commercial property owners in the New York metro region. For decades it remained a regional player, though respected for its underwriting discipline and local market knowledge. The bank expanded gradually through the 1990s and 2000s, opening branches in New Jersey and New York, and establishing itself as a reliable lender to commercial property operators who valued close banking relationships over the standardized terms offered by megabanks.

The inflection point came with a major acquisition in 2021, when Valley National Bancorp bought Westbury Bancorp, a New York-focused institution. This marked a conscious shift toward scale and geographic diversification. The deal also brought additional Florida operations into the fold, extending Valley's presence south—a logical move for a real estate-heavy lender seeking to lower concentration risk and tap the high-growth Florida property market.

Entering 2023–2024, Valley confronted the same interest-rate environment that challenged all regional banks: the Federal Reserve's rapid rate increases through 2022 and early 2023 raised funding costs, compressed loan-deposit spreads, and exposed legacy loan books to repricing pressures. Like peers such as [KeyCorp](/wiki/key-stock/) and [Comerica](/wiki/cms-stock/), Valley has had to rethink its deposit strategy and lending positioning in a higher-rate world. The [10-K](/wiki/10-k/) and quarterly earnings releases reveal a bank managing significant commercial real estate exposure—both commercial mortgages and construction loans—against a backdrop of shifting capitalization ratios and deposit flight to higher-yielding money market funds.

## What the bank lends and how it earns

Valley National's revenue comes almost entirely from net interest margin—the difference between rates earned on loans and paid on deposits. Commercial real estate loans form the largest portion of the portfolio, a legacy of the bank's original market niche and the high-yielding nature of CRE lending. This concentration is a defining feature: roughly 60% of the portfolio carries some form of commercial real estate risk, whether office, industrial, multifamily, or retail property. Smaller segments include commercial and industrial loans to business clients, consumer mortgages, and indirect auto lending.

Fee income—from loan origination, cash management services, and derivatives—supplements interest earnings but remains secondary. Like all [regional banks](/wiki/public-company/), Valley is particularly sensitive to the deposit base. When interest rates rise, depositors move money to money market funds and Treasury accounts, forcing the bank to raise deposit rates or lose funding. This "deposit flight" can either erode net margins (if the bank raises rates to retain deposits) or shrink the balance sheet (if it accepts the outflow).

What sets Valley apart from pure cost-efficiency peers is its willingness to underwrite complex CRE deals. The bank maintains deep relationships with property sponsors and developers in New York, New Jersey, and Florida, giving it informational advantages on local markets that out-of-state competitors lack. In stable property markets, this is an edge; in downturns, concentration risk can cut the opposite way.

## The current positioning and risks

As of the mid-2020s, Valley National navigates a set of competing pressures. The bank's CRE concentration—above industry averages for non-superregional banks—offers upside if property markets remain stable or recover, but exposes shareholders to outsized losses if commercial real estate enters a severe downturn. Office properties are a particular concern: the long-term shift to remote work has hollowed out demand for downtown office space in some markets, and although New York and New Jersey have pockets of well-leased trophy properties, secondary and tertiary office inventory carries real risk.

Higher interest rates also create repricing risk on adjustable-rate mortgages and construction loans. If the [Federal Reserve](/wiki/central-bank/) holds rates elevated for an extended period, borrowers refinancing or renewing loans will face higher payment obligations, potentially stressing cash flows and limiting new lending volume.

Valley's capital position and regulatory compliance—tracked through [capital-adequacy](/wiki/capital-adequacy/) ratios—remain stable, though the bank, like all mid-size operators, is subject to periodic stress tests and liquidity requirements that limit its flexibility.

The bank's growth strategy hinges on digesting the Westbury acquisition, stabilizing deposit costs, and selectively deploying excess capital. In a benign environment with stable property valuations and normalized rates, Valley could emerge as an attractive regional franchise with a loyal customer base and deep market roots. In a recessionary scenario with significant commercial property stress, the bank's concentrated portfolio could suffer material credit losses.

Investors monitoring Valley should watch: quarterly trends in net interest margin (is it stabilizing after recent compression?), deposit costs and inflows (can the bank compete for stable funding?), and the classified and non-performing loan trends in the CRE book (early warning of portfolio stress). The [10-K](/wiki/10-k/) and quarterly earnings releases contain forward-looking statements on the economic outlook and management's risk appetite, which signal confidence or caution about the path ahead.
