Invesco Senior Income Trust trades under the ticker VVR on the [New York Stock Exchange](/new-york-stock-exchange/) and is a closed-end investment fund whose primary purpose is to provide shareholders with a stream of monthly income from a portfolio of senior secured bank loans and other fixed-income instruments. Like all [closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/), VVR issues a fixed number of shares and trades at prices determined by the market—sometimes at a premium to its underlying asset value, sometimes at a discount.

## What are senior secured loans?

A senior secured loan is a form of debt where the lender (in this case, the fund) holds a claim that sits ahead of other creditors if a borrower runs into trouble. The loan is typically backed by collateral and ranks first in line for repayment. Most of the loans VVR holds are "floating-rate," meaning their interest payment moves up or down with short-term market rates—a feature that provides some protection against rising [interest rates](/interest-rate/), since higher rates mean higher income for the fund.

## Why does VVR distribute monthly?

Closed-end funds like VVR operate under Investment Company Act rules that allow them to distribute capital gains, interest income, and even a modest amount of return of capital to shareholders without the same constraints that govern open-ended [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/). The fund's management pursues a "managed distribution" policy—a target payout designed to be appealing to income-seeking shareholders—but that target is not guaranteed and may be adjusted based on the fund's performance and the market environment.

## How does the fund use leverage?

A distinctive feature of VVR is that it uses leverage—it borrows money to amplify its investment capacity. The fund might deploy equity capital of $1 billion but borrow an additional $300 million or more, creating a larger pool of assets to invest. This amplifies both returns and risks: in good markets, the borrowed money boosts the income that flows to shareholders; in bad ones, the interest cost on that borrowing cuts into returns. The leverage is constrained by regulation; the Investment Company Act sets limits on the ratio of debt to assets a fund of this type is permitted to carry.

## What are the risks?

The core risk is credit: if borrowers default on their loans, VVR loses principal. Senior secured loans rank ahead of other debt and carry collateral, which provides some cushion, but they are not risk-free—especially in a severe [recession](/recession/) when multiple borrowers might struggle at once. A sharp drop in loan values or a spike in defaults would reduce the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) and the income available to distribute.

[Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) works both ways. The floating-rate structure protects against rising rates—higher rates mean higher interest income—but also exposes the fund to the credit-sensitive nature of leveraged-loan borrowing. When credit conditions tighten, the spreads on senior loans widen (borrowers must pay more to compensate lenders for risk), which can depress the market price of existing loans and hurt the fund's value even if the borrowers do not default.

The leverage itself is another form of risk. In a market stress, the fund might face [margin calls](/margin-call-forex/) or difficulty refinancing its borrowings, forcing it to sell assets at unfavorable prices. And if the fund's net asset value falls sharply, the distribution policy may prove unsustainable and distributions may be cut, which often triggers a drop in the share price as income-seeking investors lose interest.

## The regulatory frame

VVR operates inside the [Investment Company Act of 1940](/investment-company-act-of-1940/), which regulates funds' use of leverage, their ability to distribute returns of capital, and their portfolio holdings. The Act requires the fund to maintain minimum levels of asset coverage relative to its debt. VVR also files a regulatory schedule that discloses its holdings quarterly, so a researcher can see exactly what loans the fund owns and their terms—this transparency is part of what makes closed-end funds investable for institutional holders and advisors who want specificity about what they own.

## How to research VVR

Start with the fund's annual prospectus and the most recent fact sheet, which lay out the distribution policy and the fund's current composition. Check the 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001059386), which includes audited financials and a breakdown of holdings by issuer and industry. Track the fund's net asset value and premium or discount to that NAV—if it trades at a wide discount, the income stream may be more attractive, but the discount itself may signal that the market has concerns about the underlying loans or the fund's ability to sustain its distribution. Finally, watch the yield (the annualized distribution rate relative to the share price) and any announcements about changes to the distribution policy, which are the clearest signals of the fund's financial health.
