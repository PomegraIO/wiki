## What is VTC, and what does it hold?

The Vanguard Total Corporate Bond ETF, trading under the ticker VTC, is a passively managed fund that holds a broad slice of the US corporate bond market. It owns both investment-grade bonds (debt from companies with strong credit ratings) and high-yield bonds (riskier debt from weaker credits), across all maturities from short-term notes to long-term bonds. Vanguard designed VTC to replicate the broad corporate bond market rather than picking individual securities, making it a core fixed-income tool for investors seeking diversified exposure to corporate credit.

The fund invests in thousands of individual bonds, so no single company's default or credit event materially dents the portfolio. That diversification is the fund's chief virtue: an investor gets the returns that corporate bonds, as a whole, have historically offered, minus a low expense ratio.

## How does VTC compare to other bond options?

Corporate bonds sit in a middle ground between Treasury bonds and stocks. A Treasury bond is backed by the US government's taxing power — nearly default-proof but offering modest yields. A corporate bond is backed only by the company that issued it — far riskier, but typically yielding more. VTC blends both investment-grade (very low risk) and high-yield (higher risk) bonds into one fund, giving the investor an average credit-risk profile somewhere between a safe bond fund and a junk-bond fund.

For investors building a portfolio, VTC works as the core fixed-income engine: stable, diversified, and yielding more than a Treasury fund without the extreme volatility of a pure high-yield fund. It complements equity holdings by providing ballast — bonds tend to hold value or rise when stocks fall, especially when the market is seized by fear rather than economic pessimism.

## What are the main risks?

VTC faces two principal risks: interest-rate risk and credit risk.

**Interest-rate risk** is the dominant force. When interest rates rise, the value of existing bonds falls, because new bonds being issued offer higher yields, making old bonds worth less. When rates fall, old bonds rise in value. VTC holds thousands of bonds with different maturity dates, so the fund is exposed to rate moves across the entire curve. If an investor buys VTC and holds it for five years during a rising-rate period, the fund's price may fall 10%, 15%, or more, even if no company defaults. If the investor buys at a high-rate environment and rates fall, the fund appreciates.

**Credit risk** is the chance that one or more of the companies in the portfolio will struggle to pay interest or principal. VTC mitigates this by holding thousands of companies, so a single bankruptcy does not crater the fund. But in a recession or a sharp market shock, credit spreads widen — investors demand extra yield to compensate for higher default risk — and corporate bonds fall in price across the board, even if the companies are still solvent. A portfolio of investment-grade bonds is far less vulnerable to this than high-yield, but VTC holds both, so it carries moderate credit risk.

A third, more subtle risk is reinvestment risk: when a bond matures or pays interest, VTC must reinvest the proceeds at whatever interest rates exist at that moment. In a declining-rate environment, reinvestment means buying bonds at lower yields, which erodes total return.

## How much does VTC cost?

Vanguard charges an expense ratio of roughly 0.05% to 0.07% annually, which is among the lowest in the industry for a broad bond fund. That ultra-low cost is a Vanguard signature and a material advantage over active bond managers, who often charge ten times as much and rarely add enough value to offset the fee difference.

## Who should own VTC?

VTC is designed for any investor who needs a core fixed-income allocation: a retiree seeking stability, a young saver building a diversified portfolio, or a professional manager seeking a simple, low-cost vehicle for corporate credit exposure. It suits investors who do not have strong views on which individual companies will outperform and would rather own the entire market at low cost.

VTC is less suitable for an investor who believes rates will rise sharply and wants to minimise duration risk (long-term interest-rate sensitivity), or for someone betting that credit will deteriorate and is willing to exit bonds entirely. In those cases, a shorter-duration bond fund or a Treasury fund might be more appropriate.

## How do you research VTC?

Begin with Vanguard's fund fact sheet, which discloses the fund's current yield, average maturity, duration (a measure of interest-rate sensitivity), and the breakdown of holdings by credit rating. Understanding these metrics gives a clear picture of the fund's positioning.

Next, check the composition by issuer type: what fraction of the portfolio is in financial companies, industrials, utilities, or other sectors? Vanguard publishes this detail, and it matters, because a portfolio heavy in bank debt behaves differently from one with more utility or manufacturing bonds.

Compare VTC's trailing yield to current interest-rate levels and to the yields available on Treasury bonds. If VTC's yield premium over Treasuries seems unusually tight, it may signal that credit risk is priced too cheaply, and a credit shock could follow. Conversely, if VTC's premium is wide, credit is expensive, which may mean slower returns ahead but less downside risk.

Finally, examine VTC's behaviour during recent downturns — March 2020, September 2022 — to see how much it fell. Those episodes reveal the fund's real-world sensitivity to stress. Over longer horizons, track whether VTC has delivered positive real returns (returns above inflation) and whether it has been an effective diversifier to stocks in your portfolio.

## The reinvestment question

A subtle consideration: if you are holding VTC to live off the income, understand that the fund's annual distribution reflects both the interest the bonds pay and the realised gains or losses from sales and maturities. The fund's yield is therefore a snapshot that does not guarantee future income. If you are relying on VTC as an income source, monitor whether distributions are stable or declining over time, which could signal either lower reinvestment rates or credit deterioration.

VTC is ultimately a buy-and-hold tool for investors comfortable with moderate volatility in their fixed-income sleeve and trusting that corporate credit will persist as a long-term asset class. For a retiree or a conservative saver, it fills the bond portion of a portfolio efficiently.
