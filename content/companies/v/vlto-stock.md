Veralto is a diagnostics and measurement company that provides instruments, software, and consumables used in clinical laboratories, research institutions, and industrial settings to detect, measure, and characterize biological and chemical samples. The company was created in 2023 as a spin-off from Fortive Corporation, a diversified industrial conglomerate, and emerged as a focused pure-play in the fast-growing diagnostics and life-sciences space. Veralto's portfolio includes analyzer instruments that process blood and bodily fluids for hospitals, specialty diagnostics equipment used in research and clinical settings, and a suite of software solutions that integrate diagnostic data into laboratory workflows.

## The Fortive era: Building blocks for diagnostics

Before Veralto became an independent company, its businesses were nested within Fortive, an industrial-software-and-measurement conglomerate that Fortive itself spun out of Danaher Corporation in 2015. Within Fortive, diagnostics-focused divisions accumulated over two decades — companies acquired and incubated that made diagnostic analyzers, laboratory instruments, quality-control systems, and measurement software.

These businesses thrived within Fortive's operating model, which emphasized operational excellence, continuous improvement, and organic growth through product innovation and bolt-on acquisitions. Fortive invested in upgrading manufacturing efficiency, integrating software into traditionally hardware-heavy businesses, and expanding geographically. By the late 2010s, the diagnostics and life-sciences portfolio represented one of Fortive's most attractive growth segments, with exposure to the secular expansion of diagnostic testing, precision medicine, and life-sciences research spending.

However, Fortive is fundamentally an industrial-software and manufacturing conglomerate, with businesses ranging from electrical distribution to professional instrumentation to water and environmental testing. The diagnostics portfolio did not fit neatly into that identity and competed internally with other priorities. In 2022, Fortive announced plans to separate the diagnostics business into an independent public company, reasoning that a focused diagnostics company would have more flexibility to invest in innovation, make acquisitions, and communicate a clearer story to investors.

## The spinoff and formation of Veralto

In October 2023, Fortive distributed its diagnostics and life-sciences businesses to shareholders as Veralto, a new public company trading on the NYSE. Veralto inherited several complementary but distinct diagnostic platforms:

**Core diagnostics**: Analytical instruments used in hospital clinical laboratories to analyze blood, urine, and other body fluids — measuring chemistry panels, cell counts, and biomarkers. These machines sit in the laboratory, process thousands of samples daily, and generate recurring revenue through reagents (consumables) and service contracts.

**Specialty diagnostics**: Instruments for more specialized testing — immunoassay systems for infectious disease, tumor markers, and drug-screening tests; molecular diagnostic systems for pathogen identification; and point-of-care testing devices used in emergency departments and doctor's offices.

**Software and connectivity**: Laboratory information systems (LIS) and data-management software that integrate diagnostic results, help laboratorians track samples, and feed test results into hospital electronic health records (EHRs).

The spinoff was complex because Fortive's infrastructure — finance, HR, IT, supply chain — had to be replicated for Veralto as a standalone company. But the fundamental businesses being transferred were already running and profitable.

## The diagnostic instrument market and Veralto's position

Veralto is now one of the world's largest diagnostics-instrument manufacturers, competing against established players like Siemens Healthineers, Roche Diagnostics, Abbott Diagnostics, and Beckman Coulter. The market for diagnostic instruments is large and structurally attractive: hospitals and clinical laboratories must perform diagnostic testing to treat patients, the regulatory barriers are high (diagnostic instruments must be validated and approved by health authorities), and the installed base of machines creates recurring revenue from reagents and service.

Veralto's strength is breadth — it competes across multiple diagnostic platforms (chemistry, immunoassay, molecular) rather than specializing in one category. This breadth allows the company to place multiple instruments in a single laboratory and to cross-sell new offerings to existing customers. A hospital laboratory that already uses Veralto chemistry analyzers might also adopt Veralto immunoassay systems or molecular testing platforms, reducing the likelihood they switch to a competitor.

The business model follows a predictable pattern: hospitals and laboratories buy the analyzer (a capital expenditure, usually amortized), and then purchase reagents and consumables continuously for the life of the machine — often five to ten years. Gross margins on reagents are very high (often 60–70%) because the customer is locked in and there are no economical alternatives once the analyzer is installed. Service and maintenance contracts add additional recurring revenue.

## Growth drivers and the secular expansion of diagnostics

Veralto benefits from several structural tailwinds. First, the volume of diagnostic testing is expanding, driven by aging populations (older people require more testing), the rise of chronic disease (cancer, diabetes, cardiovascular disease all require ongoing diagnostic monitoring), and the globalization of healthcare. Second, the precision medicine movement — the development of targeted drugs that work only for patients with specific biomarkers — creates demand for more and more specialized diagnostic tests. Third, developing nations are building out healthcare infrastructure, driving investment in hospital labs and diagnostic capabilities.

Within Veralto's portfolio, the specialty diagnostics and molecular platforms are growing faster than core chemistry analyzers, because the total addressable market for specialized testing is expanding and the regulatory barriers are high enough to prevent commoditization.

Software integration is increasingly important. Hospitals and labs want diagnostic data to flow automatically into electronic health records and laboratory information systems, and they want analytics and quality-control software that helps them interpret results and identify trends. Veralto is investing in these capabilities both through internal development and through acquisitions.

## Integration and the challenge of size

As an independent company, Veralto faces the typical challenges of a recent spinoff: building its own corporate infrastructure, establishing independence in supply chains and IT systems, and communicating a clear investment story to capital markets. Fortive had significant operating leverage; Veralto, as a smaller company, must build similar capabilities at smaller scale, which is less economical.

The company is also navigating the post-spinoff integration playbook: dividing shared service agreements with Fortive, migrating systems, and establishing independent governance and reporting. These efforts typically take several years.

Competitively, Veralto must establish itself as a committed investor in diagnostics innovation and acquisitions. The market leaders (Roche, Siemens, Abbott) are much larger and have deeper research pipelines. Veralto's path forward likely involves organic innovation in software and automation, selective acquisitions in specialty diagnostics and molecular testing, and geographic expansion in high-growth regions.

## How to research Veralto as an investment

The annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001967680) will detail revenue by diagnostic platform and geographic region. As a newly independent company, Veralto's first full-year financials will also disribe the stand-alone cost structure — shared services still being provided by Fortive, and the areas where Veralto is building independent capabilities.

Key metrics: organic growth by platform (core chemistry, specialty diagnostics, molecular), the ratio of recurring reagent and service revenue to total (higher is better and more predictable), gross margins on reagents versus analyzers, and backlog or order trends. The company's capital allocation strategy — how it balances investment in research and development, dividends, and acquisitions — will indicate management's conviction about future growth.

Investors also watch diagnostic testing volume trends (available from industry reports and healthcare utilization data) and the pace of adoption of molecular and specialty diagnostic tests, which indicate whether Veralto's higher-growth platforms are accelerating.
