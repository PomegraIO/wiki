Nuveen Select Maturities Municipal Fund trades on the exchange as NIM. It is a closed-end vehicle that buys [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/) — debt issued by states, cities, and other local government entities to fund infrastructure and services. The fund distributes interest income from those [bonds](/bond/) to shareholders on a quarterly or monthly basis; because municipal interest is typically exempt from federal income tax, NIM's distributions carry that same tax advantage. This makes it attractive to high-earning investors in elevated tax brackets for whom the after-tax yield on munis beats taxable bonds, even at lower stated yields.

## The municipal-bond case and who buys NIM

Municipal bonds are fundamentally a tax arbitrage. A muni yielding 3.5% that is tax-exempt is equivalent to a taxable bond yielding roughly 5% for someone in the 35% federal tax bracket. That spread — the value of the tax shield — is the core reason wealthy individuals own munis, and why funds like NIM exist. Schools, roads, water systems, and courthouses need capital; cities and states raise it by issuing bonds and offering a tax incentive to buyers. The buyer takes on [credit risk](/credit-risk/) (can the city or state pay back the bond?), and in exchange captures yield and a tax shelter.

## Nuveen's market position

Nuveen is part of TIAA, a diversified asset manager with a long history in fixed income and municipal bonds. Select Maturities is one of many Nuveen municipal [closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/); others focus on high-yield munis, insured bonds, or specific states. The "select maturities" part of the name signals a laddered or targeted approach to maturities — buying bonds across different date ranges so the portfolio does not depend on any one maturity point. This matters because bond prices move differently depending on how far away the maturity is.

## Credit quality and state/local risk

The fund holds bonds issued by a mix of states, cities, and special-purpose entities like school districts and utility authorities. Munis have [default rates](/default-rate/) far below [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) historically — a municipality's reliance on tax revenue means it treats bondholder repayment as essential — but defaults do happen. Detroit, Stockton, and other cities that faced fiscal crises have seen bondholders take haircuts. NIM must navigate the mix between safer, lower-yielding bonds (general-obligation bonds backed by full taxing authority) and riskier, higher-yielding bonds ([revenue bonds](/revenue-bond/) backed by a specific income stream like airport fees or toll roads). The fund's credit analysis and portfolio construction are the main sources of outperformance or underperformance relative to the muni bond market.

## The distribution puzzle and closed-end mechanics

NIM declares a distribution monthly or quarterly. Because the fund's yield varies with [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and the creditworthiness of its holdings, distributions can move around. The fund may also realize gains when a bond it owns appreciates or is redeemed early. In a falling-rate environment, bond prices rise and the fund captures those gains in distributions. In a rising-rate environment, bond prices fall and the fund may need to distribute less or draw on reserves. Like other closed-end funds, NIM can trade above or below [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), creating mispricings that savvy investors can exploit.

## Competition and pressures

Municipal ETFs — such as iShares National Munis or Vanguard Tax-Exempt — have grown fast and compete on cost and transparency. These [open-end funds](/open-end-fund/) charge lower fees and move to net asset value, not a market-driven premium or discount. NIM's closed-end structure and higher fees must be justified by better credit selection or superior distributions. The broader risk to all muni investors is regulatory change: a federal tax-reform move that caps or eliminates the municipal-bond tax exemption would drain demand and crater prices. That risk is rarely imminent but is never zero.

## Research and evaluation

For a potential investor in NIM, the starting point is the fund's most recent [annual report](/10-k/): the current distribution rate, the portfolio composition by type and credit quality, the maturity schedule, and the fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/). Compare this to the broader muni market — has the fund delivered better distributions than a low-cost muni ETF, or is the fee drag not worth it? Check the fund's discount or premium to net asset value; a deep discount suggests the market is wary of the portfolio or the manager, which may offer entry-point value or may be a warning. Read the prospectus to understand what [credit rating](/credit-rating/) floor the fund maintains and what restrictions apply to portfolio construction. Municipal bonds are often held by investors planning to keep them to maturity; closed-end funds are more suitable for those seeking current income and are willing to trade in and out.
