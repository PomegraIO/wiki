*The **NPF Core [Equity ETF](/equity-etf/)** (NPFE) is a straightforward instrument: a collection of the largest US companies held in proportion to their [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/), tradable as a single fund share. It serves as a core holding for portfolios seeking pure exposure to American equity markets without sector bets, active management, or complex strategies.*

## The market-cap weighted foundation

NPFE tracks a core equity index, typically a broad large-cap benchmark such as the S&P 500 or a comparable measure of the 500–1000 largest US firms by market value. The fund holds each constituent in direct proportion to its weight in the underlying index — meaning that if a company represents 2 percent of the index, it represents roughly 2 percent of NPFE's holdings. This market-cap weighting is the most passive possible approach: the fund requires no stock picking, no sector tilts, and no judgement about which firms are "better" than others.

The resulting portfolio is therefore a snapshot of how the financial markets themselves have priced the largest US corporations. If mega-cap technology stocks dominate the index, they dominate NPFE. If financial or energy stocks are underrepresented, that underrepresentation follows through. The fund does not attempt to correct for what its managers might view as mispricings or imbalances; it is purely a market vehicle.

## Why core equity matters in a portfolio

For most investors, a core equity holding like NPFE should form the equity anchor of a diversified portfolio — the largest piece, the foundation, the part that captures the broad health and growth of the American economy. Everything else added to a portfolio (international stocks, [bonds](/bond/), alternatives, sector tilts) is a satellite position, sized and shaped deliberately. A well-constructed portfolio might be 60–70 percent NPFE, with smaller allocations to other asset classes and geographies chosen to diversify and lower overall portfolio risk.

Because NPFE's goal is to match its index rather than to beat it, the fund's management is laser-focused on minimizing costs and tracking error. Every basis point spent on fees or trading costs is a basis point the fund underperforms its benchmark. This creates strong incentive alignment: the fund manager benefits when costs are low and stays true to the index.

## Expense ratio and cost dynamics

NPFE's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically among the lowest in the ETF universe, often in the 0.03–0.10 percent range annually, depending on the exact benchmark and the [fund family](/fund-family/). For an investor holding NPFE for decades, the cumulative effect of a low expense ratio compounds meaningfully. The difference between 0.05 percent and 0.40 percent may not sound large, but over 30 years, that difference can total 10 percent or more of a portfolio's value in foregone returns.

The fund also has very low turnover — it is not trading frequently in or out of positions. When index components change (a company gets added or removed due to market-cap shifts), the fund adjusts its holdings; otherwise, it sits quiet. This minimises taxable [capital gains distributions](/capital-gains-distribution/) and trading costs, further lowering the true cost of ownership for tax-aware investors.

## Liquidity and tradability

NPFE is a listed product on major US exchanges, meaning it trades continuously throughout the day at market prices set by buyers and sellers. The fund has deep liquidity — trading volume is typically high and spreads (the gap between buy and sell prices) are tight. An investor can buy or sell a modest position in seconds; large institutions can trade significant blocks without material price impact. This liquidity is a genuine advantage over [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) or closed-end structures.

The ability to trade continuously and intra-day is particularly useful for systematic investors who rebalance on a schedule or who need to raise cash for other purposes. For buy-and-hold investors, the daily liquidity matters less but is still a feature.

## Concentration and sector exposure

Because NPFE is market-cap weighted, it reflects the current structure of the market. In recent years, that structure has been dominated by a handful of mega-cap technology firms — companies valued in the trillions. The largest few holdings often represent 8–12 percent of the fund's assets combined, far more than would occur in an equal-weight structure or a structure that tried to cap individual holdings.

This concentration is not a defect but a feature — the fund is designed to be the market, not a modified version of it. If large-cap tech firms genuinely represent 30 percent of the US equity market's value, they should represent 30 percent of a broad US equity fund. An investor uncomfortable with that level of technology exposure should deliberately underweight NPFE and tilt toward value or dividend-focused alternatives, or should add international equities to diversify away from US-centric tech dominance.

## Sector composition and diversification within the fund

NPFE's sector allocation mirrors the S&P 500 or equivalent, typically including meaningful exposure to technology, consumer discretionary, financials, healthcare, industrials, materials, and utilities, with smaller positions in energy, real estate, and communication services. Within each sector, the fund owns dozens or hundreds of individual firms at various scales.

This internal [diversification](/diversification/) is genuine — the fund does not rely on any single company or small group of companies for its behaviour. If a specific firm falters, it is one position among hundreds and barely moves the fund's total return. This is why core equity funds are appropriate for the foundational part of a portfolio: the diversification is thorough, the risk is lowered by sheer number of holdings.

## Why NPFE is not a "growth" or "value" fund

NPFE is deliberately neutral on many dimensions. It does not tilt toward cheap stocks or expensive ones (value versus growth). It does not emphasise [dividend](/dividend/) payers or non-payers. It does not screen for profitability, balance-sheet strength, or earnings growth. Its only rule is market-cap weight. This neutrality is its strength for a core position — it avoids accidentally betting the farm on a single style or theme.

Investors who want a growth tilt, a value tilt, or any other style bet should make that explicitly through a second or third holding, not by modifying their core equity allocation.

## How to research and monitor NPFE

Read the fund's prospectus and fact sheet to confirm the exact benchmark and verify that the expense ratio and tracking error (the actual drift between the fund's returns and the benchmark's returns) are as low as promised. Over a full [market cycle](/market-cycle/), a well-managed core equity fund should track within 0.10–0.15 percent of its benchmark.

Monitor the fund's top ten holdings annually to ensure the fund's composition has not drifted materially from your expectations. Check the [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) to understand what portion of your expected return will arrive as taxable distributions. And at the start of each year, confirm that NPFE still makes sense as the foundation of your equity exposure — if your time [horizon, risk](/horizon-risk/) tolerance, or income needs have shifted, the role and sizing of your core equity holding may need adjustment.

For most long-term investors, NPFE or a direct equivalent is the core position that requires the least attention once it is in place. The work happens elsewhere in the portfolio.
