## What is Nvni Group?

Nvni Group Limited (trading on Nasdaq under NVNIW for warrants and NVNI for common shares) is a software consolidation company, often called a roll-up, focused on the Brazilian market. The company acquires small to medium-sized software-as-a-service companies — businesses that sell software subscriptions to other businesses — and operates them under a single corporate structure. The bet is that by consolidating these fragmented companies, Nvni can drive efficiencies, cross-sell products to existing customer bases, and create a larger, more investable platform than any individual company could be on its own.

The company went public in October 2023 following a merger with Mercato Partners Acquisition Corp, a blank-check company. Nvni Group's headquarters are in Brazil, and its portfolio of software companies are all based in Brazil, primarily serving Brazilian small and medium enterprises that need software tools for sales, marketing, customer relationship management, hiring, and data management.

## What companies does Nvni own?

Nvni's portfolio currently comprises seven software companies: Leadlovers, a marketing automation and email platform for SMEs; Ipê Digital, which offers various services; Effecti, serving specific customer needs; Datahub, providing data services; OnClick, a clickable software product; Mercos, a platform for small business operations; and SmartNX, rounding out the portfolio. Each operates semi-independently but is managed as part of the broader Nvni holding company.

The portfolio is intentionally diverse. Some companies serve overlapping customer bases (so Nvni can cross-sell), while others serve distinct needs. Leadlovers, for instance, is a larger, more established business in the portfolio; some of the other companies are earlier-stage acquisitions whose growth Nvni aims to accelerate by providing better back-office, marketing, and operational support.

## How does a software roll-up make money?

Nvni's revenue comes from the subscription fees charged by each of its portfolio companies to their customers. A typical SaaS company charges a monthly or annual subscription, and the customer uses the software continuously without incurring significant additional costs to the vendor. That recurring, predictable revenue is the appeal of software: once a customer is locked into using your product, the relationship tends to be sticky, churn is often lower than in other industries, and each new customer represents an annuity of future subscription payments.

Nvni's strategy is to acquire profitable or promising software companies at reasonable multiples, consolidate their back-office operations (combining finance, human resources, and infrastructure), use its scale to improve pricing and customer acquisition efficiency, and potentially grow their installed bases through cross-selling. If successful, the combined company's total revenue grows faster than the sum of its parts would alone, and the profit margins improve because of the operational efficiencies.

The capital required to run this business is relatively low compared to hardware manufacturing. Software is mostly intellectual property and people. Nvni must pay its developers, its customer support teams, and its salespeople, but it doesn't manufacture anything or maintain large physical assets. That capital efficiency is one of the structural advantages of the software business.

## What makes this a risky strategy?

A roll-up strategy depends on finding good acquisition targets at reasonable prices. When the price of SaaS companies was high — as it was in 2020 and 2021 — every roll-up had to overpay, which compressed future returns. When valuations are more rational, the acquisition thesis works better. Nvni went public during a period of more normalized valuations, which was better timing than earlier SPACs in the software roll-up space faced.

The second risk is integration and management. Acquiring a company is legally simple; integrating it operationally and culturally is harder. If Nvni's management cannot effectively consolidate back-office operations, maintain founder and key-employee retention, or cross-sell across the portfolio, the acquisition thesis fails. The company becomes simply a collection of separate entities under one holding company, with none of the synergies that justified the acquisition in the first place.

Third, there is the question of market opportunity. Nvni is focused on Brazil, which has a large and growing economy but also regulatory and macroeconomic challenges that differ from North America or Europe. Success requires not just good software companies but good software companies that can thrive in a specific geographic and economic context.

Finally, public-market investors are skeptical of roll-ups after the SPAC boom of 2020–2022 produced mixed results. Many investors view software consolidation as an old story and require stronger proof of synergies and growth before valuing the business attractively.

## How does Nvni fund itself?

Nvni's IPO and subsequent public stock offering raised capital that the company uses for acquisitions and to fund any losses or growth investments in its portfolio companies. The company generates cash from subscription revenue; if revenue growth outpaces operating expense growth, Nvni becomes cash-generative and can self-fund acquisitions or return cash to shareholders. If operating costs grow faster than revenue, the company burns cash and depends on outside funding.

## What's the long-term goal?

Nvni's stated strategy is to become the leading software consolidator in Brazil, then potentially expand into other Latin American markets. The company aims to grow through acquiring additional companies, expanding the customer bases of existing portfolio companies, and improving operational efficiency. Eventually, the ambition is either to build the portfolio into a platform substantial enough to sell to a larger technology acquirer, or to sustain it as a public company that generates increasing profits and cash flow.

## What should investors watch?

The most important metric is whether Nvni's total revenue from all portfolio companies is growing faster than the cost to operate them. If subscriber acquisition cost is declining, churn is stable or falling, and margins are improving, the roll-up thesis is working. If the company is paying more and more to acquire customers, or losing existing ones, the business is not delivering on its promise.

Investors should also track whether new acquisitions are being completed and at what prices, whether key people in the acquired companies are staying, and whether cross-sell between portfolio companies is producing real revenue. These are harder to measure than headline revenue, but they determine whether the consolidated company is more valuable than the sum of its parts. The annual 20-F filing (SEC CIK 0001965143) provides the detailed financials; the quarterly results show whether growth is accelerating or slowing. Nvni trades on Nasdaq at prices set by the market; nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell.
