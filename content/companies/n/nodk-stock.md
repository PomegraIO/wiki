> "Insurance companies exist to collect more in premiums than they pay out in claims, and invest the difference until the claims arrive."

NI Holdings, Inc. is an insurance holding company that writes property and casualty insurance through subsidiary operations. The business is almost childishly simple in its core mechanics: collect premiums from policyholders, invest that money in [bonds](/bond/) and stocks, pay out claims as they arrive, and keep the margin as profit. In practice, the business is far more complex—selecting customers and pricing policies correctly, managing claims efficiently, adhering to state regulations, and navigating the occasional catastrophic loss that can wipe out a year's worth of profits. Yet that core truth—premium in, claim out, invest the float—drives everything.

The insurance industry is a tale of two income streams. The first is underwriting: the difference between premiums collected and claims paid out. A property insurer might collect ten dollars in premiums, pay out seven dollars in claims, and keep three dollars as underwriting profit. That margin depends entirely on accurate risk selection and pricing. If an insurer misjudges the riskiness of the customers it sells to, it can find itself paying out more in claims than it collected in premiums—a recipe for losses that can persist for years.

The second income stream is investment income. An insurance company holds, at any given moment, a large pool of customer premiums waiting to be paid out as claims. That float—technically, policyholders' money held in trust—is invested in bonds, stocks, and sometimes real estate. The interest, [dividends](/dividend/), and capital gains from that portfolio add to the insurance company's earnings. Some years, investment income exceeds underwriting profit. During stock-market crashes or periods of rising [interest rates](/interest-rate/), investment returns can swing sharply and overwhelm the underwriting business.

NI Holdings operates in a fragmented insurance market. It does not have the national scale or brand recognition of Allstate, State Farm, or Progressive. Instead, it targets regional markets and niche segments where it can develop expertise and relationships. This approach trades off scale-driven cost advantages for the ability to dominate specific geographies or customer types. A regional insurer that understands the roofing contractors in its state, or the auto dealers, can underwrite them more accurately than a national competitor parachuting in with generic pricing models.

The insurance business is also regulated state-by-state in the United States. An insurer must maintain minimum capital ratios, follow rate-filing rules, and comply with coverage mandates that vary across states. This regulation protects policyholders from insolvency but it also restricts the flexibility that an insurance company can use to expand, exit markets, or adjust pricing. It means every state offers slightly different profit margins and constraints.

Competition in insurance is fierce and structural. Customers switch policies frequently if they find lower premiums elsewhere. National insurers with superior technology, brand recognition, and scale can undercut regional operators on price. Catastrophic losses—hurricanes, earthquakes, industrial fires—can hit a regional insurer disproportionately hard if its customer base is geographically concentrated. And new competitors with different underwriting models or lower cost structures can erode margins industry-wide.

NI Holdings' strategy hinges on being selective about the business it writes. If the company can identify market segments or geographies where it understands risks better than competitors, it can price policies more accurately and attract better customers. That selectivity keeps loss ratios sustainable and underwriting profitable. But selectivity also means slower growth and smaller scale, which creates pressure to keep operating costs low and to ensure investment returns contribute meaningfully to earnings.

The capital position is paramount for an insurance company. Regulators require insurers to maintain capital ratios that buffer against unexpected losses. A well-capitalized insurer can absorb a large catastrophic claim without going insolvent; an undercapitalized one can be forced to stop writing new business or even face regulatory action. Building and maintaining adequate capital is an ongoing discipline—the company must balance returning capital to shareholders (via dividends or buybacks) against the need to hold reserves for claims and catastrophes.

## How to research NI Holdings as an investment

Start with the SEC filings (CIK 0001681206), particularly the annual 10-K and quarterly 10-Qs. These documents disclose the composition of the insurance portfolio by line of business (auto, homeowners, commercial, etc.), loss ratios and [expense ratios](/expense-ratio/) for each segment, the investment portfolio (which bonds and stocks are held and at what cost), and regulatory capital ratios. The combined ratio—a metric calculated as (claims paid plus operating expenses) divided by premiums—is the most useful summary of underwriting health: a ratio under 100 means underwriting profit, over 100 means underwriting loss.

Quarterly earnings calls often reveal management's view of market conditions, competitive pricing, and whether loss trends are improving or deteriorating. Watch for any commentary on large claims or catastrophic events (hurricanes, floods, or other insured perils) as these are the biggest earnings surprises in insurance.

Also monitor the investment portfolio: bond duration, credit quality, and equity exposure. If interest rates rise sharply, the market value of a long-duration bond portfolio falls. If the company has bought bonds with widening [credit spreads](/credit-spread/), those bonds will also mark down in value. These are not typically losses for an insurer that holds bonds to maturity, but they affect equity value and mark-to-market accounting.

Finally, track the competitive environment for the specific insurance lines NI Holdings writes. [Auto insurance](/auto-insurance/) and [homeowners insurance](/homeowners-insurance/) have very different competitive and claims dynamics. If homeowners claims are deteriorating industry-wide due to weather patterns or construction [inflation](/inflation/), any insurer in that segment will feel the squeeze.
