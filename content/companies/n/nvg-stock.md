The Nuveen AMT-Free Municipal Credit [Income Fund](/income-fund/) is a closed-end investment company trading under the ticker NVG. Its singular purpose is straightforward: to assemble a portfolio of [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/) — borrowings by US states, cities, and local authorities — and distribute the interest income they generate to shareholders in a tax-advantaged form.

Municipal [bonds](/bond/) appeal to investors in high tax brackets because their interest is typically exempt from federal income tax and, if issued in the bondholder's home state, from state tax as well. That tax shelter makes the nominally lower yields on municipal bonds competitive with taxable bonds for those investors. The fund exists to offer that tax efficiency at scale, managed by professionals, to individuals who want exposure to the municipal-bond market without building a portfolio bond-by-bond themselves.

> "A fund trades at a price set by the market, not the value of its holdings — that gap is where risk and opportunity live."

[Closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/) differ from their better-known cousins, open-ended [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/), in a structural way that matters. When you buy shares of an open-ended fund, the fund creates new shares and you own a claim on your slice of the assets. But a closed-end fund has a fixed number of shares that were issued once, at inception, and now trade on an exchange like a stock. There is no redemption — no way to force the fund to buy your shares back at their [net asset value](/net-asset-value/). Instead, the price of NVG shares floats, set by supply and demand in the market, just as the price of any stock does. If the fund's underlying bonds are worth 100 million dollars and there are 10 million shares outstanding, the net asset value per share is 10 dollars. But NVG might trade at 9.50 or 10.50 depending on investor sentiment, market conditions, and the fund's distribution history.

That daily price disconnect is the core mechanism of closed-end funds. A fund can trade at a discount to its net asset value if investors are pessimistic or if the market is risk-averse, or at a premium if investors clamor to own it. The structure creates opportunities for disciplined investors who understand what they are buying, but it also introduces a layer of risk that exists independent of the bonds themselves.

The specific portfolio — the bonds NVG holds — is built and rebalanced by Nuveen's investment team. They select municipal credits they believe will service their debt, assessing the issuer's finances and the bond's seniority in the capital structure. They adjust duration and sector weights to manage risk and capture yield. The fund aims to emphasize bonds with investment-grade ratings, though its charter allows some lower-quality holdings. Because distributions must be paid monthly, the team needs a portfolio that generates steady, reliable income. That discipline shapes the kinds of bonds purchased: typically general-obligation bonds backed by tax revenue, or [revenue bonds](/revenue-bond/) from essential services like water utilities or airports where demand is stable.

Municipal bonds themselves carry distinct risks. A city or state facing a fiscal crisis can default, though outright defaults are rare relative to corporate defaults. More common is gradual deterioration — a municipality that cannot afford to maintain its infrastructure or that must raise tax rates to unsustainable levels. [Concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) matters too: if many bonds in the portfolio are from issuers in the same state or the same sector, a localized shock can hit multiple holdings at once.

The leverage story adds another layer. Many closed-end funds, including those in Nuveen's stable, use leveraged borrowing — they raise money via preferred shares or debt, then invest it alongside shareholder equity to amplify returns. Leverage magnifies both gains and losses. In a benign environment it boosts the distributions the fund can pay, attracting yield-hungry investors. In a stressed environment it can force rapid asset sales or distribution cuts that wound shareholders who bought specifically for income.

Nuveen itself is the investment advisor — a TIAA subsidiary, part of a larger asset-management conglomerate. The firm's ability to source bonds, negotiate pricing, and manage the portfolio shapes what returns are possible. The fee structure — stated as an annual percentage of assets under management — varies by fund, and those fees compound over decades. Even small fee differences stack up.

An investor considering NVG should study the fund's fact sheet and [annual report](/10-k/) to understand the current portfolio composition, the [leverage ratio](/leverage-ratio-forex/), the premium or discount at which it trades, the distribution history and whether those distributions are being paid from income or from capital (a warning sign), and the [management fee](/management-fee/). The prospectus spells out the risks and the fund's permitted strategies. Because the price can diverge from value, comparing the trading price to the net asset value is essential — buying a fund trading at a steep discount is different from buying one trading at a premium, even if the underlying portfolio is identical. And for a fund whose entire raison d'être is tax-efficient income, the actual tax treatment of distributions matters more than the absolute yield.
