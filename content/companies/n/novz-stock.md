*The **TrueShares Structured Outcome (November) ETF** (NOVZ) is built on a simple compromise: reduce the pain of market declines by accepting that not all gains are yours to keep.*

## The tradeoff at the heart of NOVZ

Every structured outcome ETF promises the same bargain, and NOVZ executes it through annual November outcome periods. The fund holds S&P 500 constituents but wraps them in a collar — a financial structure that uses options to cap both potential losses and potential gains. In a rough market year, the collar absorbs declines up to a stated buffer level; in a sharp rally year, the collar keeps anything above a cap. The cost of these protections is baked into the fund's returns and its [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/).

This is not a new idea, but it remains quietly powerful for a narrow audience. Retirees, people living off portfolio withdrawals, or investors who have experienced a financial trauma in past [bear markets](/bear-market/) often find that the peace of mind from a capped loss exceeds the value of the upside they sacrifice. NOVZ and its peers offer that trade at a scale — a full-sized S&P 500 exposure — that makes it digestible for portfolios of any size.

## How the annual reset reshapes the picture

Unlike evergreen [buffer funds](/buffer-fund/) that might operate over three- or five-year periods, NOVZ resets each November, creating a series of one-year snapshots. This structure has an underappreciated virtue: it forces a yearly reckoning. When November comes, an investor holding NOVZ sees fresh data on how last year's buffer performed, what the new year's cap and floor will be, and whether market conditions have shifted enough to warrant a change in strategy.

That annual reset also introduces timing risk. A market decline that occurs in October, as the current buffer is nearly exhausted, offers little protection. A decline in January, fresh after the November roll, gets the full new buffer. Investors cannot time this perfectly, but they can at least monitor the calendar and adjust expectations accordingly.

The repricing of the options collar each November means that volatility, [interest rates](/interest-rate/), and index level all ripple into the fund's new mechanics. In a low-volatility market environment, the buffer protection is inexpensive to provide, so the upside cap can be wider. During periods of high volatility, protection is costly, so the cap narrows. An investor tracking NOVZ over multiple cycles will observe these shifts and gain some insight into how options markets price uncertainty.

## The real cost and what it buys

NOVZ's expense ratio reflects both the cost of the options overlay and the fund's operational overhead. That figure is substantially higher than a plain S&P 500 ETF, which costs pennies annually. An investor comparing NOVZ to a 0.03-percent ETF is essentially paying for insurance and should view it as such. In years when markets fall and the buffer activates, that insurance looks cheap. In years when markets soar and the cap bites, it looks expensive. Over a full [market cycle](/market-cycle/), it should roughly wash out for an investor who was truly at the margin of staying invested — the years the buffer helps nearly offset the years the cap costs them.

For investors who are genuinely confident they can remain patient through any market decline, NOVZ's cost is pure drag. For investors who know from experience or temperament that a big market loss will cause them to abandon their strategy, the cost of NOVZ is the cost of preventing that disaster. That is a legitimate calculation, but it must be an honest one.

## Suitability and concentration risk

NOVZ is appropriate for investors within roughly 10–15 years of needing significant portfolio withdrawals — early retirees, people facing near-term major expenses, or those who have explicit liquidity needs they cannot finance any other way. It is least appropriate for young accumulators, where the compounding cost of capped upside is severe.

The fund is not hedged against the underlying index's concentration or sector risk. Because the S&P 500 currently skews heavily toward mega-cap technology firms, a NOVZ investor still bears that sector concentration fully. The buffer protects against declines from any source — a tech crash, a financial crisis, a geopolitical shock — but it protects against the magnitude of the decline, not its direction. An investor who is uncomfortable with the current S&P 500's composition should address that through a different fund choice, not through NOVZ.

## Liquidity and trading dynamics

NOVZ is a TrueShares product listed on a major exchange and trades with moderate liquidity. Spreads are wider than a flagship S&P 500 ETF, and trading volume is lower, so large transactions may face price impact. For the kind of patient, buy-and-hold investor who should own NOVZ, this is a minor friction. For active traders or those managing large allocations, it is a meaningful constraint.

## The decision framework

Investors considering NOVZ should ask: if the market fell 20 percent next year, would I stay invested or bail out? If the honest answer is "I would bail," then NOVZ or a similar buffer fund may be justified; the cost of the protection is less than the cost of missing the recovery. If the answer is "I would stay the course," then NOVZ is probably overpriced for you — simple [diversification](/diversification/) and a [rebalancing discipline](/rebalancing-discipline/) are cheaper and more efficient. The [buffer ETF](/buffer-etf/) is not an investment; it is insurance against behaviour that you know would hurt you. Price it as such.
