REITs are vehicles for holding and operating real estate. A REIT owns buildings — office parks, shopping centers, warehouses, apartments, data centers — and collects rent from tenants. By law, REITs must pay out most of their earnings as [dividends](/dividend/), making them high-income vehicles. NURE concentrates on REITs whose underlying properties carry shorter lease terms or faster tenant turnover, distinguishing them from funds holding long-duration [commercial real estate](/commercial-real-estate/).

The short-term aspect matters. A REIT holding a portfolio of apartments with year-to-year leases faces more frequent tenant turnover and rent-resetting opportunities; a REIT holding office space under long-term fixed leases locks in revenue for years but cannot easily adjust to market moves. NURE's focus on the former camp — shorter lease durations and quicker repricing cycles — implies higher exposure to near-term economic and occupancy swings but also more flexibility when conditions change.

## What "short-term" means here

Short-term does not mean the REIT itself will be short-lived or unstable. It means the properties held by the REIT tend to have lease expirations within one to three years, or the REIT model relies on frequent tenant turnover for revenue. Examples include apartment REITs, [self-storage REITs](/self-storage-reit/), and data-center REITs with monthly or annual renewal cycles. By contrast, a long-term REIT might hold office or industrial space let on [triple-net leases](/triple-net-lease/) lasting five, ten, or twenty years — much longer commitments between landlord and tenant.

The advantage of short-term leases is responsiveness: when an existing lease rolls, the owner can reset rent to market rates or choose new tenants. The downside is uncertainty — if an economic downturn arrives and occupancy rates fall, short-term REIT rents can fall quickly, and dividends can contract. Long-term REITs cushion this by locking in revenue, but at the cost of forgone upside if the market tightens.

## Interest-rate sensitivity

One of the defining features of short-term REITs is their interest-rate sensitivity profile. REITs typically use leverage — they borrow money to buy more properties, amplifying returns and income. When [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise, the cost of refinancing debt climbs, pressuring earnings and dividends. Long-term REITs with fixed-rate debt are somewhat insulated because their borrowing costs do not move with the rate cycle. Short-term REITs, if they rely on floating-rate debt or face frequent refinancings, feel rate moves more acutely and sooner. This makes NURE more sensitive to [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) moves than a long-term REIT fund would be.

## Holdings and sector mix

NURE holds a diversified portfolio of REITs across various property types — residential, hospitality, self-storage, data centers, infrastructure, and occasionally office or [retail REITs](/retail-reit/) with shorter leases. The number of holdings typically ranges from 40 to 80, depending on the screening criteria and market conditions. Because many REITs are smaller or mid-sized cap names, individual REIT movements can swing the fund noticeably.

## Dividend yield and income

REITs are chosen by income-focused investors for their high distribution yields. NURE's yield varies with market conditions and prevailing interest rates but typically sits well above yields from stock indices. That high income comes with strings attached: it reflects the use of leverage and the requirement to pay out most earnings, which limits capital reinvestment and long-term total-return potential. Some REIT dividends are classified as ordinary income for tax purposes, making them less attractive in taxable accounts for high-earners, whereas others contain [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/) or depreciation-driven return of capital.

## Risks specific to short-term REITs

Economic [recessions](/recession/) hit short-term REITs harder than long-term ones because tenants can leave or renegotiate rent downward quickly. [Inflation](/inflation/) affects operating costs — utilities, repairs, labor — and can squeeze margins if rents cannot be raised immediately. Rising interest rates directly pressure REIT valuations and dividend sustainability. Specific property-type risks also apply: apartment REITs face rent-control regulations in certain geographies; hospitality REITs are sensitive to travel demand and consumer spending; data-center REITs depend on cloud adoption trends and energy costs.

The fund is also subject to liquidity variations — some individual REITs are thinly traded, and in market dislocations, spreads can widen.

## Cost and execution

NURE trades as a listed ETF with a low [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), usually under 0.5 percent annually. Trading volume is typically adequate for retail investors, though not as deep as mega-cap [index funds](/index-fund/).

## Evaluating the fund

Anyone considering NURE should understand the fund's specific REIT holdings and their lease-term profiles. Review the expense ratio and distribution yield, then model what happens to income if occupancy rates fall or refinancing costs rise. Compare NURE's distribution yield to a longer-term REIT fund to ensure you understand the trade-off you are making. Watch sector concentration — if the fund is overweight in one property type like data centers or self-storage, that single thesis carries elevated risk. Finally, consider whether short-term REIT exposure fits your goal: it is a real-estate income play tuned for near-term repricing, not a buy-and-hold long-duration property hold.
