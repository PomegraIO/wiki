Northpointe Bancshares Inc. owns a bank that does something straightforward: it takes money from people and businesses in Michigan and Wisconsin, pays them interest on it, lends that money out to other people and businesses at higher [interest rates](/interest-rate/), and pockets the difference. That is the entire banking business in a sentence. Northpointe is a regional bank, meaning it operates in a specific geographic area rather than nationwide, and it relies on deposits to fund its loans rather than borrowing in wholesale markets. The risks are simple to understand once you know how banks actually work.

## How Northpointe makes money: the interest spread

Picture Northpointe's [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) as two piles of money. On one side are deposits — checking accounts, savings accounts, money market deposits, certificates of deposit — that people and businesses have entrusted to the bank. Northpointe pays those depositors a small amount of interest to keep their money there. On the other side are loans — mortgages on houses, loans to farmers, lines of credit to small manufacturers, auto loans — to borrowers who pay Northpointe a higher rate of interest.

The difference between what borrowers pay and what the bank pays depositors is called the net interest margin. If the bank pays 0.5 percent to depositors and earns 4 percent on its loans, the spread is 3.5 percent. That margin is the profit margin on the bank's core business. Everything else — fees on checking accounts, fees for processing payments, fee income from wealth management — is subsidiary to that spread.

The spread depends on interest rates. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises its benchmark interest rate, both deposit rates and loan rates tend to rise, but they do not move together. For a while, if the Fed raises rates, loan rates go up faster than deposit rates, which is good for the bank — the spread widens and the bank makes more money. But eventually, deposit rates catch up, especially if the bank is trying to keep its money from fleeing to higher-paying alternatives. When rates fall, the opposite happens: loan rates drop faster than the bank can lower deposit rates without losing deposits entirely.

## Why location and deposits matter

Northpointe's location in Michigan and Wisconsin matters because it determines what kinds of borrowers it can lend to and what kinds of deposits it can gather. Michigan has auto manufacturing, agriculture, healthcare, and professional services. Wisconsin has similar sectors. Those are the businesses Northpointe understands, the ones whose credit officers can walk into the bank and talk to a loan manager who knows the industry.

The deposits are what actually constrain the bank. A bank is only as good as the stability of its funding. Northpointe funds its loans with deposits from its local communities, not with borrowing in capital markets. This has an advantage: deposits are stable and relationship-based, and they are cheaper than borrowing. It has a disadvantage: the bank cannot grow loans faster than it can gather deposits, and if economic fear spreads, depositors might move their money elsewhere.

In normal times, deposits are sticky. People do not move checking accounts constantly. But when interest rates rise sharply or when fear spreads (like the 2023 regional banking turmoil), deposits can flee to higher-yielding alternatives or to bigger banks perceived as safer. A bank that loses deposits has to shrink its loan book or raise deposit rates aggressively, both of which squeeze profitability.

## Credit quality and the economic cycle

Northpointe makes loans to people and businesses. Some borrowers will not repay. When times are good and people are employed and businesses are profitable, defaults are rare. When a [recession](/recession/) hits or when unemployment rises in Michigan or Wisconsin, defaults increase. The bank takes a loss on each default. During a severe recession, losses can overwhelm a quarter's profits.

The bank sets aside reserves for expected loan losses. The bigger the reserve, the safer the bank, but the lower the reported earnings. Regulators watch these reserves to make sure banks are not being too generous in assuming their loans will perform. If a recession hits unexpectedly, the bank might face losses larger than its reserves, which would directly reduce shareholder equity.

Regional banks like Northpointe are more vulnerable to economic cycles in their home regions than nationwide megabanks are. A manufacturing slowdown in Michigan or Wisconsin hits Northpointe harder than it hits a bank with customers across fifty states. Concentrated risk is the trade-off for the relationship-based advantage.

## Capital constraints and shareholder returns

A bank must hold capital — actual money and assets — as a cushion. Regulators set minimum capital ratios that every bank must meet. Northpointe's ability to grow loans is constrained by how much capital it holds, because each dollar of capital supports a maximum amount of loans. If the bank wants to grow faster, it either has to raise capital (by issuing new stock, which dilutes existing shareholders) or hold back more of its earnings, reducing the amount available to return as [dividends](/dividend/) or buybacks.

This is the eternal tension in banking: maximize profitability and distribute cash to shareholders, or hold capital for safety and growth. Northpointe probably leans toward a conservative approach as a regional bank, holding enough capital to survive downturns without shocks to shareholders, but not building enormous excess capital that would suggest pessimism about future growth.

## Regulation and the pressure from big banks

Northpointe competes against megabanks like Chase, [Bank of America](/bank-of-america/), and [Wells Fargo](/wells-fargo/), but also against online banks, credit unions, and fintech lenders that are slowly encroaching on the mortgage and small business lending space where regional banks have traditionally operated. A megabank can offer lower rates because of its scale; an online bank can operate with lower costs; a fintech lender can use algorithms to approve loans faster.

Northpointe's advantages are relationship banking and local knowledge. If a farmer in rural Michigan needs a loan, the megabank's automated system might say no, but a local loan officer at Northpointe who knows that farmer and his land might say yes. That relationship advantage is real but is slowly eroding as technology makes banking more commoditized.

Regulation also presses differently on different-sized banks. A megabank faces intense scrutiny and extensive rules. A regional bank faces less intensive oversight but still must meet capital standards, liquidity tests, and stress tests that validate the bank would survive a severe downturn.

## The risks and what to watch

The fundamental risks in a regional bank like Northpointe are:

Interest rates. If rates fall unexpectedly, net interest margins compress, hurting profitability.

Credit quality. A recession or sector-specific downturn in Michigan or Wisconsin increases loan losses.

Deposit stability. If rates rise and depositors defect to higher-paying alternatives or bigger banks, Northpointe has to chase deposits with higher rates, again squeezing margins.

[Capital adequacy](/capital-adequacy/). If losses exceed reserves, capital erodes, potentially triggering regulatory action or the need for a dilutive capital raise.

Competition. Slow erosion of market share to megabanks and fintech as they improve their offerings.

## How to research Northpointe Bancshares

Read the most recent 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001336706). It will tell you the loan portfolio breakdown — how much is mortgages, how much is commercial, how much is consumer — and the deposit base. It lists nonperforming loans, which are the red flag. It discloses loan loss reserves.

Watch quarterly earnings reports for trends: Is net interest margin stable or declining? Are deposits growing or shrinking? Is the nonperforming loan ratio increasing? Are charge-offs (written-off bad loans) rising?

Track Federal Reserve interest rate decisions, because those are the single biggest driver of regional bank profitability in the short term. Watch economic data from Michigan and Wisconsin — employment, manufacturing activity, home prices — because those affect borrower credit quality. Finally, compare Northpointe's profitability metrics, capital ratios, and loan growth to other regional banks to see if it is performing in line with peers or lagging.
