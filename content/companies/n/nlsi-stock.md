*The **NEOS Long/Short Equity [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (NLSI) is an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) that runs a long/short strategy — holding both stocks it believes will rise and short positions in stocks it expects to fall — while targeting a steady stream of [dividend](/dividend/) income to shareholders. Unlike most exchange-traded funds, which track an index mechanically, NLSI gives a portfolio manager discretion to adjust both the long and short sides in response to market conditions, making it a pick for investors seeking downside hedging alongside [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/).*

NLSI sat at the intersection of what was becoming a more competitive corner of the ETF market in the early 2020s: the space where income-seeking investors asked whether a fixed index of stocks really captured their needs, and where portfolio managers argued they could do better by mixing dividend payers with strategic short positions to dampen downside swings. The fund is structured as an open-ended management company under the Investment Company Act — meaning it holds a license to actively manage money in the ETF wrapper rather than passively tracking a benchmark.

The strategy it pursues is conceptually straightforward but operationally nuanced. The long book (the stocks NEOS owns outright) emphasizes equities that throw off meaningful dividends — companies in sectors like utilities, energy, real estate, and financials that have historically paid shareholders cash yields well above the market average. The portfolio manager builds this long side with the aim that its income will fund much of the fund's own distributions without relying entirely on capital appreciation. The short book — positions where the manager has borrowed shares and sold them, profiting if the price falls — targets stocks the manager believes are overvalued or vulnerable to earnings disappointment. Ideally, when the market rises, the long positions gain more than the shorts lose; when the market falls, the shorts cushion the decline.

What distinguishes this from a pure equity fund is that the goal is not maximum upside but *reduced volatility alongside income*. A long/short fund trades some of the excitement of a [bull market](/bull-market/) for the insurance policy of a protected downturn. That is appealing to investors who have reached a life stage where losing money matters more than making it, or who want a bucket of their portfolio that pays them a salary whether markets rise or fall. It is less appealing to younger savers who are making regular contributions and can exploit big declines to buy more shares at lower prices.

The mechanics of [short selling](/short-selling/) inside an ETF create real costs. The manager must borrow shares (paying a borrow fee that can vary depending on how hard the stock is to locate), post collateral, and manage the risk that the short will move sharply against the fund if a stock rallies unexpectedly. These frictions eat into returns. A perfectly hedged long/short fund should lag a rising market because the shorts are a drag, and should outperform in a falling market because the shorts are an anchor. In sideways or choppy markets, the dividend income becomes the return story.

NLSI's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) reflects the cost of active management and short operations — meaningfully higher than a passive [index fund](/index-fund/) or a simple dividend-focused strategy. Investors in the fund are paying for the manager's stock-picking skill and the infrastructure required to maintain a short book. Whether those costs are justified depends entirely on whether the manager's picks add alpha (outperformance above what skill and luck would suggest), and that is always uncertain. Some periods will show the manager adding value; others will show underperformance, and the short book amplifying losses when the manager gets the direction wrong.

The fund is designed for a specific investor profile: someone with a three-to-five-year or longer time horizon who wants exposure to equities but is unwilling to ride out steep declines, and who prioritizes steady cash distributions over growth. It is not suited for market-timing or for investors who need maximum upside in a bull market, nor for anyone who does not understand how short selling works or why a fund called Long/Short can underperform the [stock market](/stock-market/) in a healthy rally. Like all active strategies, NLSI lives or dies on the manager's skill and consistency.

A potential investor researching this fund should start with the prospectus and fact sheet, available through the fund's sponsor. Key numbers to monitor are the fund's actual income distribution history (does the manager deliver on the income promise?), the magnitude of the long and short positions (how much hedging is really in place?), and the tracking record of performance relative to a simple diversified equity income benchmark — say, a dividend-focused stock index. Quarterly fact sheets and the fund's [annual reports](/10-k/) lay out the holdings and the manager's rationale. As always, past returns do not guarantee future results, and the decision to invest must account for the investor's own risk tolerance and financial plan, not the fund's marketing claims.
