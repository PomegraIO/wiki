The developed world extends well beyond the United States. A significant share of the global economy — and the dividend-paying companies within it — trades on exchanges in Europe, Australia, Japan, and Canada. WisdomTree International Efficient Core Fund tracks exposure to that universe through a dividend-weighted index, accepting exposure to the currency fluctuations that come with foreign holdings and applying a weighting scheme that privileges companies returning cash to shareholders.

## The dividend lens on international markets

Most international stock indexes weight holdings by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/): the largest companies get the largest positions. That approach is straightforward but grants outsized influence to whatever mega-cap firms happen to be trading at high valuations in a given year. The index underlying NTSI takes a different route. It selects large-cap and mid-cap companies from developed markets outside the U.S., then weights each holding by the annual [dividend](/dividend/) it pays. A company that yields three percent of its market value carries three times the weight of an otherwise-similar firm yielding one percent.

This dividend-weighted approach has two effects. First, it mechanically tilts the portfolio toward value — toward companies that distribute cash rather than reinvest all earnings or prioritize growth. Second, it concentrates the fund's holdings in sectors and geographies where dividends are culturally common: Western Europe's bank stocks, Australia's resource companies, Japan's consumer firms. A traditional market-cap-weighted international fund would put proportionally less weight on these regions, because European and Australian markets are smaller than the U.S. market in absolute terms, even though they represent the bulk of developed-market stock trading outside North America.

## Currency and the international hold

All of NTSI's holdings are priced in foreign currencies — euros, [British pounds](/british-pound/), yen, [Australian dollars](/australian-dollar/), and the rest. When the fund's share price is quoted in dollars (as it is on U.S. exchanges), the fund owns the underlying currency exposure unless it chooses to hedge. An investor in NTSI is therefore betting on two things at once: the performance of the companies themselves and the movement of their home currencies against the dollar. If the dollar weakens, the same foreign holdings are worth more in dollar terms; if the dollar strengthens, they are worth less. That [currency volatility](/currency-volatility/) is a real source of risk and opportunity, and it cannot be separated from the return on international equities.

Some international funds attempt to hedge away this currency exposure, buying and selling [currency futures](/currency-futures/) to lock in a specific dollar value per foreign holding. NTSI does not; its unhedged structure means investors capture whatever currency gains or losses emerge from the period they hold the fund. Practitioners sometimes distinguish this approach from hedged alternatives by calling unhedged portfolios "currency-exposed" and hedged ones "currency-neutral," though those terms can be misleading — neither truly controls currency; they simply choose different exposure profiles.

## Sector and regional concentration

Because the dividend-weighting scheme overweights sectors that historically pay large dividends, NTSI's holdings concentrate in areas quite different from a broader international equity fund. Banking, consumer staples, energy, and telecommunications typically make up a larger share of the portfolio than they would in a market-cap-weighted international fund. Technology — which is much larger in the U.S. and parts of the developed world but tends to reinvest earnings rather than distribute them — is often underrepresented.

Geographically, the fund tends to carry meaningful weight in the United Kingdom, Continental Europe, Japan, Australia, and Canada. The proportions shift with dividends paid, not with the size of those economies or markets. This can be a feature or a bug: a manager seeking pure [diversification](/diversification/) across all developed markets might find the tilt toward dividend-paying regions too narrow; a dividend-focused investor might find it precisely aligned with their goals.

## Costs and trading

NTSI charges a relatively low annual [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), which is routine for dividend-weighted funds that track published indexes rather than requiring active management. The fund trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) exchange with daily liquidity; it can be bought and sold at transparent prices throughout the trading day. The spread between the bid and ask price is typically tight for a fund of this size, though the effective cost of entry depends on order size and market conditions.

The fund's performance is anchored to an underlying index — the WisdomTree International Dividend Index. Investors can compare NTSI's returns (after costs) against the returns of that index and against its peer funds (other international dividend-weighted or equal-weight products) to assess whether it is tracking accurately and whether its fee is competitive.

## What to watch and research

An investor considering NTSI should start by clarifying what role international exposure plays in their overall portfolio. International stocks offer diversification away from U.S. concentration but introduce both company-specific risk (the quality and valuation of the foreign firms held) and country-level risk (currency, economic cycles, and regulatory changes in other developed nations). The dividend-weighted approach is neither inherently superior nor inferior to market-cap weighting; it is simply a different lens, suited to some investors' goals and at odds with others'.

The prospectus and fact sheet disclose the fund's holdings, sector and country allocation, expense ratio, and tracking error (the degree to which the fund's returns differ from those of its underlying index — a meaningful metric for any passive product). Reviewing these will clarify whether the fund's particular tilt and costs align with your investment thesis.
