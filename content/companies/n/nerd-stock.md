*The **Roundhill Video Games ETF** (ticker: NERD) is an exchange-traded fund that holds equity stakes in companies whose primary business or major revenue stream comes from video games, gaming hardware, or gaming-related services. It offers investors exposure to the global gaming industry without picking individual stocks — a thematic bet on an entertainment sector that spans hardware makers, game publishers, esports operators, and gaming platforms.*

Launched in 2018, NERD is managed by Roundhill Investments and tracks holdings across the entire gaming ecosystem. The fund owns both mega-cap household names and smaller specialist firms. A holdings list typically includes companies like [Nvidia](/nvda-stock/) (graphics processors for gaming), Activision Blizzard and Take-Two (game publishers), [Sony](/sony-stock/) and [Microsoft](/msft-stock/) (console makers with gaming divisions), Roblox (user-generated gaming platform), and ESE Entertainment (esports infrastructure). The exact composition shifts quarterly as the fund's methodology reassesses which firms meet the gaming-revenue threshold.

The fund is thinly capitalized relative to broad market ETFs — its [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is modest but higher than a simple S&P 500 tracker, which is normal for a specialized thematic strategy. Liquidity is reasonable for a niche product; shares trade on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/), and typical spreads (the gap between bid and ask prices) are tight enough for most retail investors. The fund is not leveraged and does not reset daily, so it carries none of the volatility drag that bedevils leveraged or inverse products.

The real question with NERD is whether a "video games" theme is a coherent investment category. Gaming revenue does correlate within the industry — a blockbuster release or major hardware launch can lift multiple holdings at once. But the fund holds such different types of firms (chip makers, entertainment companies, infrastructure operators) that it sometimes behaves less like a pure gaming play and more like a pick-and-shovel bet on consumer entertainment and technology broadly. That mix can be a feature or a bug depending on what the investor is trying to achieve. Someone bullish on gaming specifically might own too much Nvidia (a chip maker with wider uses beyond gaming) and too little of pure-play publishers. Someone hedging a tech portfolio with gaming upside gets more [diversification](/diversification/) but less precision.

Risks worth knowing: gaming is cyclical. When consumer spending contracts, entertainment discretionary suffer first. Hardware cycles are lumpy — a new console generation can drive a single year of outsized sales, then plateau for five years. Regulatory risk looms; regulators worldwide increasingly scrutinize loot boxes, in-game spending mechanics, and the psychological hooks game designers build in. Currency exposure is real: many gaming revenues come from overseas, so dollar strength can shrink reported earnings. Finally, the sector is subject to sentiment swings; perceived moral panics or social-media backlashes can trigger sharp moves unrelated to fundamentals.

To research NERD, start with the [fund prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) and fact sheet on the Roundhill Investments website, which spell out the exact inclusion criteria and holdings rebalance schedule. The fund's reported top ten holdings show the weighting toward major players. Watch quarterly updates to the composition; a shift in who qualifies as "gaming-revenue exposed" can quietly change what you actually own. For context on the underlying industry, follow earnings reports from major holdings (Nvidia earnings calls are especially informative), major game releases, and console sales trends. Gaming forums and industry publications like Gamesindustry.biz often offer useful color on market sentiment and competitive dynamics.

NERD suits investors who believe the gaming industry will outpace the broader economy but prefer not to bet on a single stock, or who want thematic exposure without the burden of picking publishers or hardware makers. It is not a core holding for most portfolios but rather a tactical position for someone with conviction about gaming's long-term reach and willing to tolerate [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/).
