*The **Nelson Select ETF** (NELS) is an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) that holds a portfolio of primarily United States stocks selected through fundamental analysis, aiming to capture companies with enduring moats and attractive valuations.*

## The core strategy: active security selection

NELS is not a tracker fund. A portfolio manager or team at Nelson Capital analyzes individual companies and builds a portfolio based on that analysis. The fund is neither market-cap weighted nor bound by index constraints; the manager has the freedom to overweight companies they believe are cheap and underweight (or avoid) those they think are expensive. This active approach adds opportunity for outperformance if the managers are skilled, but also carries the risk of underperformance if they are not.

The specific criteria Nelson Capital uses are disclosed in the fund's prospectus and marketing materials, typically emphasizing quality — durable competitive advantages (moats), strong cash generation, reasonable debt levels — paired with valuation discipline. The idea is to buy good businesses at fair prices, not bargain-basement shots in the dark or overvalued growth stories. That philosophy has philosophical roots in [value investing](/value-investing/), though the label matters less than the actual process.

## Holdings and portfolio construction

NELS typically holds somewhere between 50 and 150 stocks, concentrated enough that top holdings have meaningful weight but diversified enough that no single stock can crater the fund. The portfolio covers major sectors — technology, healthcare, financials, industrials — rather than picking a narrow niche. That broad [diversification](/diversification/) reflects both the fund's philosophy (find good companies everywhere) and practical realities (a stock fund that avoids entire sectors faces limits on opportunity and liquidity).

The fund's turnover — how frequently managers buy and sell stocks — can be moderate to high depending on how often their views change or market prices move away from their estimates of value. Higher turnover means more trading costs and potentially higher tax consequences for taxable investors. The prospectus discloses annual turnover, which you can compare across actively managed rivals to understand the manager's trade frequency.

## The income angle: dividends and capital returns

Many value-oriented funds harvest [dividends](/dividend/) alongside capital appreciation. NELS likely holds a meaningful proportion of dividend-paying stocks, both because such stocks often trade at reasonable valuations and because dividends provide a cushion in sideways markets. A stock that yields 2 per cent and treads water in price is less painful to own than a non-dividend stock that also treads water. That dividend income can be distributed to shareholders monthly, quarterly, or at year-end, depending on NELS's policy.

The fund may also hold stocks that are returning cash to shareholders through buyback programs. Buybacks reduce the share count, which mechanically boosts [earnings per share](/earnings-per-share/), but they are not income in the sense that dividends are. Both represent capital returns, but dividends are taxable and buybacks are not (until shares are sold).

## The competitive landscape and performance stakes

NELS competes against other actively managed stock funds (which typically charge higher fees) and against passively indexed alternatives (which charge less but offer no active selection). For NELS to justify its [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), it must beat the relevant benchmark — often the S&P 500 or a broad total-market index — by more than the fee difference. If NELS costs 0.50 per cent annually and a passive [index fund](/index-fund/) costs 0.05 per cent, NELS must outperform the index by at least 0.45 per cent per year just to break even. Longer track records tell you whether that hurdle has been cleared.

Nelson Capital's reputation and track record with NELS and any prior funds or accounts it has managed will be crucial signals. A long history of outperformance lends credibility; a long history of underperformance is a red flag.

## The risks of active selection

The core risk is simple: the managers might be wrong. They might overestimate the durability of a company's competitive advantage, underestimate the threat of disruption, or misread the market's valuation of the business. Unlike a market-cap weighted index, which owns the entire market in proportion to the weights that millions of traders assign, NELS is betting that Nelson Capital's concentrated views are better than the market's aggregate view. If they often are, you win; if they often are not, you lose.

There is also the risk of style drift — the fund's managers gradually shifting away from the stated philosophy (e.g., loading up on expensive growth stocks because they are performing well) in a bid to chase short-term returns. The prospectus defines the investment objectives and permitted universe, but enforcement is ultimately on shareholders to notice and act.

## How the fund trades and what to watch

NELS trades throughout the day on an exchange at prices set by buyers and sellers. The share price reflects the value of the holdings inside, though occasionally the fund's market price can diverge slightly from the [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) (NAV) — the precise value of the assets. Large discounts or premiums between price and NAV are worth investigating; they can signal liquidity problems or shifting investor sentiment.

To evaluate NELS, start with the fund's prospectus and [annual report](/10-k/), which disclose the portfolio and the managers' investment approach. Compare its expense ratio to peer funds. Review the track record against the chosen benchmark over trailing periods (one year, three years, five years, since inception) to see whether the active management has been worth the cost. Examine the current holdings to assess whether the philosophy is genuinely being applied or whether it has drifted. Finally, understand your own risk tolerance — active funds can underperform in their less-favoured market environments (e.g., a value-focused fund can lag badly in growth-dominated markets), so make sure you can live with that possibility.
