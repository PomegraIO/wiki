NNN REIT owns and manages single-tenant commercial properties — the individual buildings where retailers and services operate, such as a pharmacy, a dollar store, or a restaurant. The company buys these buildings, leases them to the business operators, and collects rent. Because the company owns the real estate rather than operating the businesses, it earns a steady, recurring stream of income from property rent rather than exposing itself to the ups and downs of retail commerce.

## The landlord model

NNN owns buildings that it leases to operators — the pharmacist who runs the drugstore, the franchisee who runs the pizza restaurant, the manager who runs the dollar store. The rent agreement is structured as a triple-net (or NNN) lease, which means the tenant pays not only the rent but also the property taxes, insurance, and maintenance costs on the building. That shifts responsibility and cost away from the landlord and directly to the business operator. NNN collects rent and mostly stays out of the day-to-day operation of the properties.

This is a fundamentally different business from retail itself. A retailer like a grocery-store chain worries about finding customers, managing inventory, training employees, and managing the profit and loss of the business. NNN worries about finding good tenants, collecting rent, and maintaining the portfolio of properties. If the grocer has a bad quarter because customers are not buying, NNN still gets paid rent. If the grocer's business improves and they expand to ten new locations, NNN can benefit by owning some of those new buildings.

## The portfolio and the tenant mix

NNN owns properties across North America, and the portfolio is diversified across different types of retail and service businesses. The company owns standalone buildings leased to drugstores, grocery stores, dollar stores, fast-casual restaurants, hair salons, insurance agents, and many other kinds of operators. No single tenant typically represents more than a small percentage of NNN's rent, and no single property category dominates. That diversity matters because it means if one category — say, fast-casual restaurants — goes through a tough time, it does not destroy NNN's overall income.

The strength of NNN's portfolio depends on the health of the businesses that occupy it. If the tenants are stable, established businesses with a long track record and loyal customers, the rent will be reliably paid. Dollar-store chains, drugstores, and franchised food operators are typically stable because their business models are predictable and they have proven they can manage many locations. Newer, flashy retailers are more risky — they may not survive a downturn or may go out of business, leaving NNN with an empty building and the need to find a new tenant.

## How NNN makes money

The company's revenue is simply rent. Tenants pay NNN for the right to occupy the buildings. Because the tenants are responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance, NNN's costs are low — mostly consisting of staff to manage the properties and any overhead. That means a high percentage of the rent becomes profit.

The company distributes most of that profit to shareholders in the form of a dividend. In fact, that is the core appeal of owning NNN stock: the reliable, recurring dividend income. Real estate investment trusts (REITs) are legally required to distribute at least 90 percent of their taxable income to shareholders as dividends. NNN typically does this and markets itself to income-seeking investors.

The second source of return is capital appreciation. If NNN buys a building for USD 2 million and the property appreciates to USD 3 million over a decade, the company can sell it and realize that gain. However, most of NNN's return to shareholders comes from the dividend rather than capital appreciation.

## How NNN invests and grows

NNN grows by acquiring new properties. The company has investment teams that scout for buildings meeting its criteria — established, stable tenants; long lease terms; reasonable rental rates; good locations. When it identifies a building it wants to own, it buys it and either signs a new lease with the current tenant or finds a new tenant. Acquisitions require capital, which NNN raises by selling new shares to investors and by borrowing money. The borrowed money is called debt, and the ratio of debt to assets is important because it affects the company's financial stability and the returns to shareholders.

The company also receives cash from rents and can use that cash to buy new properties (or to increase the dividend, or to pay down debt). In growing markets where cap rates (the annual rent divided by the property price) are attractive, NNN will acquire more properties and the portfolio will expand. In markets where prices are high and rents do not cover the cost of purchase, NNN may slow acquisitions and focus on managing the existing portfolio.

## Risks and challenges

The biggest risk is that tenants fail to pay rent or go out of business. If a major tenant goes bankrupt — say a retail chain that occupies several of NNN's buildings — the company faces an immediate loss of rent and the cost and hassle of finding a new tenant. This risk is baked into every REIT; even though the properties have value, the rent stream is only as good as the tenants' ability to pay.

Another risk is interest-rate exposure. If NNN borrowed money at low rates (say, 3 percent) and interest rates rise to 6 percent, refinancing becomes more expensive and the company's interest costs rise. Higher interest costs reduce the earnings available to shareholders.

A third risk is the long-term viability of the retail businesses NNN's tenants operate. E-commerce has shifted shopping away from physical stores, and that trend has not reversed. Drugstores, dollar stores, and grocery stores are more resilient than apparel retailers, and NNN has tilted its portfolio toward those categories. But the existential threat to retail remains. A world with far less physical shopping is a world where fewer buildings are needed for retail, and that could depress property values and rents over decades.

Finally, there is the risk of recession. When the economy slows, retailers struggle, rents may not be paid on time, and tenants may default. NNN's dividend would come under pressure. This is not a risk unique to NNN — it is a general economic risk — but it is worth knowing that in a severe recession, REIT dividends are not immune to problems.

## How to research NNN

Start with the annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0000751364), which lists all of the company's properties, the tenants that occupy them, the rent each generates, and the lease terms (how long the lease is, when it expires, whether the rent is fixed or can be raised). This tells you what income streams are expiring and what the company is relying on.

Watch the earnings reports and the commentary on lease spreads — the difference between the rent NNN charges and what tenants paid at previous locations. A widening spread suggests NNN is acquiring properties at better rental rates. A narrowing spread could mean rising property values or rising rents are making acquisitions harder.

Track the company's debt levels and interest-coverage ratios. High debt makes NNN more sensitive to rising interest rates and leaves less cushion if rents decline. Stable or falling debt is a sign of disciplined capital management.

Finally, pay attention to which kinds of tenants and property categories NNN is adding to its portfolio. If it is moving into faster-growing or more resilient categories and away from weaker ones, that is a positive signal. If the company is getting more aggressive about tenant credit quality or lease terms, that could reflect concern about the retail landscape.
