Most [leveraged ETFs](/leveraged-etf/) are short-term trading vehicles — they reset daily, amplifying returns (or losses) on a single day's price movement, and suffer from volatility decay over longer periods. NTSX is structured differently. It uses equity index futures to achieve approximately 30 percent leverage to a dividend-weighted U.S. large-cap index, then holds that level continuously rather than resetting it each day. The fund is engineered for buy-and-hold investors who accept leverage but want to avoid the drift and drag that plagues daily-reset leveraged products.

## The dividend-weighting scheme

The underlying index selects large-cap and mid-cap U.S. companies — the constituents of the broad U.S. [stock market](/stock-market/) — and weights them by annual [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) rather than by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/). A high-yielding large company gets a larger weight than a low-yielding one of similar size. This tilts the portfolio toward mature, cash-returning firms and away from growth-oriented businesses that reinvest earnings. The bias is systematic: utilities, consumer staples, energy, financials, and [real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/) tend to be overweighted; technology and growth industrials are underweighted.

## How the leverage works

NTSX holds the dividend-weighted stocks directly, but it finances part of that purchase with a loan — effectively borrowing to buy more stocks than the fund's assets alone would permit. Operationally, this is achieved by holding long equity [futures contracts](/futures-contract/) alongside the equity portfolio itself. The futures provide leverage without requiring the fund to carry an explicit margin loan or to execute daily buy-and-sell rebalancing.

This structure differs from daily-reset leveraged ETFs (sometimes called 2x or 3x funds), which use swaps or options and recalibrate daily to maintain a fixed leverage multiple. Daily-reset leverage is corrosive over time because of volatility decay — the fund loses value on downside moves and gains less on upside moves, even if the underlying index ends flat over a long period. NTSX's continuous structure avoids this: it maintains leverage at a steady level, so its long-term return characteristics should hew more closely to what simple mathematics would predict (approximately 130 percent of the index's return, minus costs).

That said, leverage is leverage. If the underlying index falls 20 percent, NTSX will fall approximately 26 percent (30 percent more). If it rises 20 percent, NTSX will rise approximately 26 percent. The magnified volatility is the price of the magnified expected return.

## Interest costs and compounding

The fund pays an interest cost on its effective borrowing — implicitly, through the cost of carrying the futures contracts. That cost accrues continuously and reduces the fund's returns relative to an unleveraged version. In a low-rate environment, this friction is small; in a high-rate environment, it becomes material. The fund's prospectus details the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), which is where this drag appears.

More subtly, leverage compounds over time. If an unleveraged portfolio returns 8 percent annually and leverage is 130 percent, the leveraged fund should return approximately 10.4 percent (8 times 1.3), before costs — but only if the return is consistent. If returns are volatile, the mathematics of geometric returns can cause the fund to trail this simple calculation. This is a form of volatility decay, less severe than in daily-reset funds but still present.

## Who NTSX is and is not for

NTSX is purpose-built for investors who want to tilt their portfolio toward [dividends](/dividend/) and are comfortable accepting magnified volatility in exchange for a shot at magnified returns. It is suitable for:

- Long-term holders (typically those with a multi-year investment horizon) who will not be spooked by drawdowns;
- Investors seeking to increase their equity exposure without adding cash or selling other holdings;
- Those building a diversified portfolio where leverage in one sleeve (funded by borrowing or rebalancing into cash) makes sense.

It is not suitable for:

- Short-term traders looking for daily gains;
- Risk-averse investors who cannot stomach swings much larger than the overall market;
- Accounts with specific margin rules or leverage restrictions.

## Research and due diligence

Before purchasing NTSX, clarify your comfort with leverage and with a dividend-focused tilt. The fund's prospectus, fact sheet, and historical returns versus the underlying index show the cost of leverage and the tilt's historical impact. Compare those returns to the dividend-weighted index itself and to unleveraged alternatives to understand the trade-off between enhanced expected returns and additional risk.

The fund trades on major U.S. exchanges with good daily liquidity. Its price can diverge temporarily from the [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) of its holdings (the "NAV"), though the spread is typically narrow. Understanding NTSX as a single, held position — not as a daily trading tool — is key to using it effectively.
