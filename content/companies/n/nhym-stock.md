*The **Nuveen High Yield Municipal [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (NHYM) is a passive exchange-traded fund that tracks a broad index of high-yield [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/) issued by American state and local governments. It offers investors a way to own a diversified portfolio of [tax-exempt bonds](/tax-exempt-bond/) without buying individual securities, combining the liquidity and low costs of an [index fund](/index-fund/) with income that is free from federal tax — and often state tax as well for residents of states represented in the fund.*

## What high-yield municipal bonds are and why they exist

Municipal [bonds](/bond/) are debt obligations issued by states, cities, counties, and other local government entities to finance infrastructure, schools, hospitals, and other public projects. The "high-yield" designation refers to bonds that carry a lower [credit rating](/credit-rating/) — typically BBB or lower on Standard & Poor's scale — which means the issuer is considered a higher [credit risk](/credit-risk/). Because investors are taking on more risk of non-repayment, these bonds offer higher [interest rates](/interest-rate/) to compensate. The trade-off is simple: greater yield in exchange for greater credit uncertainty.

What makes municipal bonds distinctive is their tax treatment. The interest income from most municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax, and exempt from state and local income tax if the bondholder lives in the state that issued them. For a high-income investor in a high tax bracket, this tax advantage can make a lower-yielding municipal bond actually deliver better after-tax returns than a higher-yielding [corporate bond](/corporate-bond/). NHYM's portfolio captures that advantage across a wide range of issuers and states.

## How NHYM works and what it holds

NHYM is a passive index fund. It does not attempt to pick winning bonds or avoid losers; instead, it replicates the holdings of the S&P High Yield Municipal Bond Index, which is constructed to represent the broad universe of high-yield municipal debt in the United States. The fund holds hundreds of individual bonds, spreading credit risk across many issuers and geographies rather than concentrating it in a handful of bets.

The portfolio is weighted by the size of each bond issue — larger issuers occupy larger positions — so the fund's returns broadly mirror what an investor would get from owning the index itself. When bonds mature or are called (redeemed early by the issuer), they are replaced with other bonds that fit the index definition. Trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) like any stock, so liquidity is immediate at market prices, unlike owning individual bonds where you must find a buyer on the [secondary market](/secondary-market/).

The typical bond in the fund carries a maturity of five to ten years, though this shifts with market conditions and [index rebalancing](/index-rebalancing/). The monthly income distribution reflects the interest the underlying bonds are earning, minus the fund's small operating costs.

## The economics of the fund

NHYM's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is competitive for a bond fund — typically in the range of 0.40% to 0.50% per year. This is deducted daily from the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), so investors do not pay it directly as a trading cost but rather see it embedded in the fund's performance. For an actively managed [municipal bond fund](/municipal-bond-fund/), this fee would be considerably higher, often 0.70% or more, so the passive approach saves meaningful money over time.

The fund trades at a small premium or discount to its net asset value — the actual market value of the underlying bonds — depending on supply and demand. When demand is strong, the premium can widen and the fund's price rises above its NAV. When demand is weak, it may trade at a discount. These fluctuations are temporary and reflect the reality of any liquid security trading in a market; they do not indicate a problem with the fund itself.

[Dividend](/dividend/) income arrives monthly, providing a steady cash flow that appeals to retirees and income-focused investors. The after-tax yield depends entirely on the bondholder's federal and state tax bracket, which is why the same fund may be attractive to someone in the top marginal bracket and unattractive to someone in a low bracket.

## Credit risk and the high-yield question

The defining feature of NHYM is that it holds bonds with lower credit ratings — BBB and below — which means issuers that Standard & Poor's considers at elevated risk of default. This is not an accident or a shortcoming; it is the fund's explicit mandate. Investors in [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) are accepting the risk that some of those issuers may fail to pay interest or principal on schedule, in exchange for the higher yield.

In practice, the average [default rate](/default-rate/) on high-yield municipal bonds is lower than on high-yield corporate bonds, partly because municipal issuers often have the ability to raise tax revenue to meet their obligations. But default does happen — cities have filed for bankruptcy, and some bonds have experienced significant losses. The [diversification](/diversification/) across hundreds of issuers mitigates single-issuer risk, but it does not eliminate it.

During economic downturns or periods of fiscal stress for state and local governments, the value of NHYM's holdings may decline, and some issuers may struggle to pay. The fund's net asset value would fall accordingly. This is the core risk of owning high-yield bonds: you are betting that the income you receive is adequate compensation for the possibility that some principal may never be repaid.

## Interest-rate sensitivity and bond volatility

All bond funds are sensitive to interest rates. When prevailing interest rates rise, existing bonds (which pay fixed interest) become less valuable because they yield less than new bonds being issued. When rates fall, existing bonds become more valuable. NHYM, holding bonds with maturities centered around five to ten years, is moderately sensitive to these moves — not as volatile as a long-duration bond fund, but more volatile than a short-term bond fund.

A 1% increase in interest rates can easily translate to a 5% decline in the net asset value of a fund like NHYM, assuming a typical yield environment. The decline is not permanent if you hold the fund to maturity of its bonds, because you will continue receiving the promised interest payments and eventually get your principal back. But if you need to sell before then, an environment of rising rates means you may face a loss.

This interest-rate sensitivity is worth knowing if you are using NHYM to fund near-term expenses. It is less concerning if you are investing for the long term and can weather periodic value fluctuations.

## How to research and understand NHYM

Anyone considering NHYM should start with the fund's prospectus, available from Nuveen's website, which details the exact index NHYM replicates, the fees, the risks, and the tax treatment. The prospectus also lists the top holdings — which issuers and which bonds make up the largest positions in the fund at any given time.

Beyond the prospectus, watch the fund's yield (often quoted as SEC yield) to understand the income it is currently delivering relative to its price. Compare it to other high-yield municipal bond funds and to taxable corporate bond funds, adjusting for your own tax bracket to see which offers the better after-tax return. The fund's ratio of premium or discount to NAV is also worth monitoring; a persistent wide discount might indicate that the fund is less liquid than usual or that market sentiment has shifted.

Because NHYM holds municipal bonds, it is particularly relevant to investors in high-tax states or high tax brackets who can benefit most from the federal tax exemption. It is less beneficial for tax-deferred accounts like IRAs, where the tax exemption is wasted, and less suitable for investors with short time horizons who cannot tolerate price volatility.
