*The **FT Vest U.S. Equity Max [Buffer ETF](/buffer-etf/) — November** (NOVM) is a [structured product](/structured-product/) wrapper around the S&P 500 designed to cushion losses while capping gains. It holds the 500 largest American firms but embeds a financial hedge that absorbs the first portion of any decline, rolling its protection window annually each November. This is neither a pure equity fund nor a [bond](/bond/) fund — it is a compromise built for investors uncomfortable with the full breadth of stock-market swings.*

## The buffer mechanics

The fund attempts to protect investors from roughly the first 12–15 percent of downside movement in the underlying [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) over a defined outcome period (typically one calendar year, ending in November). Any loss beyond that buffer is not protected; the investor still takes it. Gains above the buffer period's appreciation cap are also forfeited — NOVM is explicitly not designed to capture all of the [stock market's](/stock-market/) upside. It is a tradeoff: reduced downside risk in exchange for capped upside gain.

The protection works through options. FT Vest — the Finite Treasury arm of [Franklin Templeton](/fgdl-stock/) — purchases [put options](/put-option/) on the index to create the buffer floor. These options cost money, which comes from the fund's net returns. When markets rise sharply, the capped gains mean the fund underperforms the S&P 500; when markets decline, the buffer absorbs losses up to its limit. In flat or slightly negative years, the buffer often delivers meaningful outperformance.

## Why November, and what happens at rollover?

Each year the outcome period resets on a maturity date in November. When that date arrives, the prior buffer expires and a new one activates. This means the fund is not a buy-and-hold-forever instrument — it is a series of one-year bets, each with its own outcome mechanics and buffer level. An investor holding NOVM through multiple annual resets will experience a sequence of separate protected periods rather than one continuous protection scheme.

On each rollover date, the fund's buffer level, cap, and option costs are recalibrated based on market conditions and [interest rates](/interest-rate/). If volatility rises, buffer protection becomes more expensive, so the actual downside cushion might shrink slightly. The annualised lens means NOVM investors need to stay aware of their roll dates and the fund's resetting mechanics — passive set-and-forget ownership is not the right posture here.

## The expense-ratio and cost reality

FT Vest funds carry higher fees than plain-vanilla S&P 500 ETFs because the options protection is not free. The stated [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) typically reflects both the annual [option premium](/option-premium/) and operational costs. In quiet markets, an investor may pay notably more for protection they do not use. In turbulent years, that protection pays off in full. The economics of the buffer are most favorable when you actually experience a market drawdown within the outcome period — then the downside cushion justifies its cost.

## Who actually benefits, and who should skip it?

NOVM suits investors with specific constraints: those near or in retirement who cannot stomach the psychological discomfort of a 20–30 percent drawdown, or those who have a stated need to stay invested but cannot tolerate losses below a certain floor. The annual reset also appeals to investors who want to revisit their hedge each November and adjust allocations if circumstances change.

It is poorly suited to long-term accumulators with many years until they need the money. Over multi-decade horizons, the caps on upside are enormously expensive — that forgone outperformance compounds into material shortfalls versus the unhedged S&P 500. Younger investors or those in growth phases should almost certainly stick with plain equity funds and control downside risk through [asset allocation](/asset-allocation/) and rebalancing instead.

NOVM also misses the real nature of stock-market risk. A buffer that covers 12–15 percent of losses is genuine protection for mild [bear markets](/bear-market/) but vanishes in severity. The 2008 crisis saw the S&P 500 fall nearly 60 percent; a 12-percent buffer would have left an investor with devastating unprotected losses. NOVM's protection is contextual — it works well when drawdowns are modest, less so in historic crashes.

## The real risk landscape

Beyond the mechanics: because NOVM is tied to the S&P 500, it carries all the idiosyncratic [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) of that index — if mega-cap technology falters, so does the fund. The options overlay does not hedge any particular sector. The annual reset also introduces timing risk: if a significant market decline happens just after the November rollover, the new year's fresh buffer applies immediately. But if a correction occurs in October, when the old buffer is nearly exhausted, protection is minimal just when it would have been most useful. Timing matters.

Liquidity is generally solid because NOVM is a Franklin Templeton product trading on a major exchange, but trading volumes are far below a standard S&P 500 fund, so spreads may be tighter if an investor needs to exit quickly.

## How to evaluate the fit

Before investing, read FT Vest's fund fact sheet carefully — it lays out the current outcome period's buffer level, cap, and expense ratio plainly. Compare the rolling one-year performance against plain S&P 500 ETFs in the past several years; if the buffer has consistently cost more than it has protected, rethink the purchase. Examine your broader portfolio — if you are already holding bonds, real assets, or other defensive positions, a buffer ETF may be redundant. And ask honestly: would a 12-percent buffer actually prevent you from staying invested in a decline, or is it mere peace of mind? If the latter, consider cheaper risk management through [diversification](/diversification/) instead.
