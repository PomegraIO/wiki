[National Storage Affiliates](/nsa-stock/) Trust's Series B Preferred Shares represent fixed-income claims on a self-storage real estate portfolio. To understand these shares, one must first understand the underlying self-storage business, which has evolved from a commodity real estate play into a competitive, consolidating industry where scale, pricing discipline, and technology determine winners and losers.

## The self-storage business and competitive dynamics

Self-storage facilities—row upon row of climate-controlled or unconditioned units that individuals and small businesses rent by the month—generate revenue from a simple model: collect rent from many tenants, keep the facilities mostly occupied, and limit operating costs through automation and minimal labor. The business is capital-intensive upfront but runs with strong cash margins once the property is built and stabilized.

The competitive landscape in self-storage has changed dramatically over the past 15 years. Once dominated by thousands of independent mom-and-pop operators, the sector is now consolidated around a handful of large public REITs: [Extra Space Storage](/exr-stock/), [Public Storage](/psa-stock/), and a tier of mid-sized consolidators like National Storage Affiliates. Consolidation was driven by the realization that self-storage rewards size: larger portfolios spread fixed costs, allow for better pricing technology, and create scale in customer [acquisition](/acquisition/). A lone operator with five facilities cannot compete on price or brand against a company with 500 facilities and sophisticated revenue-management software.

National Storage Affiliates competes by owning and operating a geographically diversified portfolio of storage facilities, primarily in secondary and [tertiary markets](/tertiary-market/) across the United States. The company avoids the most expensive coastal markets, where land and development costs make it harder to achieve target returns. Instead, it focuses on markets with steady population inflows and balanced supply, where rental rates can grow with [inflation](/inflation/) without severe competition from new supply.

## Revenue model and the preferred shares' claim

National Storage Affiliates generates revenue from monthly rental contracts. The typical customer signs a lease for a storage unit at $X per month, with the ability to leave with notice. There is high customer churn—people use storage temporarily, for relocation, renovation, or seasonal goods—but it averages out such that a well-operated facility achieves stable occupancy rates of 80 to 90 percent and can raise rates as demand supports it.

The company's preferred shares entitle the holder to a fixed quarterly [dividend](/dividend/), paid from the cash flow the company generates. That cash flow comes from rental income minus operating costs (utilities, repairs, insurance, property taxes, management) and debt service. If the company has excess cash after meeting these obligations, it distributes some to both preferreds and common shareholders.

The hierarchy is critical: in [liquidation](/liquidation/) or distress, preferreds are paid before common equity but after debt holders. This seniority makes the preferred shares less volatile than the [common stock](/common-stock/) but far riskier than the company's [bonds](/bond/) or bank debt.

## Occupancy, pricing, and the competitive moat

The self-storage sector's profitability depends fundamentally on occupancy rates and the ability to raise rents. When occupancy is high and new supply is limited, owners can push rents up annually without losing tenants. When new storage facilities are being built, occupancy falls temporarily, and rents flatten or decline until the new supply is absorbed. National Storage Affiliates, like its peers, must navigate these cycles.

The competitive pressure varies by market. In a market with healthy inflow and constrained supply, National Storage Affiliates can raise rents at 3, 4, or 5 percent annually for years. In a market flooded with new supply, occupancy pressure forces discounting, which erodes margins. The company's ability to pick markets wisely—acquiring or developing facilities in places where supply remains disciplined—is a core competitive skill.

The era of extremely high rents and occupancy is finishing. In the mid-2010s through the early 2020s, demographic trends and pandemic disruption created unusually strong demand for storage, and prices rose sharply. That period attracted capital and new development, which is now showing as elevated supply in many markets. The sector is moving toward more normal, competitive conditions, which should moderate rental growth.

## Debt, leverage, and the preferred dividend's safety

National Storage Affiliates funds acquisitions and operations through a combination of debt and equity. The company borrows from banks and the bond markets, then uses that leverage to multiply returns on its equity base. Leverage amplifies gains when properties appreciate and rents rise, but it magnifies losses if rents stall or properties must be sold at lower valuations.

The preferred shares' dividend is safer than the common stock's but depends on the company maintaining enough cash flow to cover both debt service and the dividend. If the self-storage market enters a sustained contraction—low occupancy, rent pressure, rising operating costs—the company's [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) tightens. At that point, management faces a choice: cut the common dividend, cut the preferred dividend, or draw down debt capacity. Most REITs prioritize preserving their preferred dividend above all, viewing it as a covenant with income investors who took subordination risk on the assumption the dividend would be stable. But that assumption can be tested in severe downturns.

The company's debt levels and the principal repayment schedule matter. A highly leveraged company with significant debt maturities approaching must refinance in whatever [interest rate](/interest-rate/) environment prevails at that time. If rates remain high, refinancing costs squeeze the cash available for dividends.

## Portfolio composition and tenant concentration

National Storage Affiliates owns facilities spread across the United States, which provides [diversification](/diversification/) against regional [recessions](/recession/) or local supply gluts. However, the company's portfolio will reflect whatever market thesis management has adopted. If management believes strongly that [secondary markets](/secondary-market/) offer better growth than [primary markets](/primary-market/), the portfolio will be weighted accordingly—and if that thesis is wrong, returns suffer.

Unlike [net-lease REITs](/net-lease-reit/), which depend on individual tenant solvency, self-storage relies on the aggregate demand from thousands of individual renters. A single customer default matters little; what matters is occupancy trends and pricing power across the whole portfolio.

## Comparing NSA preferreds to peers

National Storage Affiliates' preferred shares compete for investor capital against preferreds issued by Extra Space Storage, Public Storage, and other REIT preferreds broadly. A larger peer with higher credit quality may be able to issue preferred shares at a lower yield, signaling lower perceived risk. If NSA preferreds trade at a materially higher yield than peers, it either reflects genuine credit concerns or represents a pricing opportunity for yield-seeking investors willing to accept the extra risk.

## Monitoring and research guidance

An investor in NSA preferred shares should review the company's quarterly earnings reports and 10-K filing for several key metrics. Occupancy rates by property and by region reveal pricing power and competitive pressures. Rent growth trends show whether the company can push pricing or is facing headwinds. Debt-to-EBITDA and [interest coverage ratios](/interest-coverage-ratio/) indicate financial stability and how much room exists for unexpected shortfalls.

Watch for commentary on new supply in key markets. If management indicates that major competitors are building new facilities nearby, expect near-term occupancy and pricing pressure. Conversely, if the company is seeing consistent occupancy strength and healthy rate growth, the dividend is more secure.

Monitor the capital allocation discipline. Does management pursue acquisitions at reasonable prices, or has it paid elevated multiples chasing growth? Does the company return excess cash to shareholders or hoard it? A management team that raises the dividend steadily while maintaining stable occupancy and debt levels is demonstrating prudence; one that cuts the dividend while raising leverage is signaling distress.
