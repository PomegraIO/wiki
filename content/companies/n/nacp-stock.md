*The **Impact Shares NAACP Minority Empowerment ETF** ([NASDAQ](/nasdaq/): NACP) selects US-domiciled companies that meet environmental, social, and governance standards defined in partnership with the NAACP, emphasizing diverse leadership, equitable hiring, and community investment.*

Impact Shares partnered with the NAACP to create a screening methodology that identifies publicly traded companies aligned with the civil rights organization's principles. The fund does not hold NAACP shares or proprietary NAACP products; rather, it applies a set of metrics around leadership diversity, workforce composition, supplier diversity, and board representation to select from the broader universe of US equities.

The screening process is distinct from typical ESG funds. Standard ESG indices often emphasize environmental management and governance structure (executive compensation, board independence, [audit committee](/audit-committee/) strength). The NAACP framework adds explicit focus on racial and ethnic diversity at leadership levels, equity in hiring and advancement, community engagement in neighborhoods where the company operates, and measurable commitments to diversity in procurement. A company might score well on classical ESG (low carbon, strong audit committee) but not pass NAACP's screens if it lacks diverse board representation or has poor labor-relations history in minority communities.

The resulting portfolio tends to hold mid-cap and large-cap companies that have made high-profile commitments to diversity and social responsibility — consumer brands, financial services, industrials, and technology firms where demographic composition of leadership and workforce is visible and material. The fund avoids sectors with historically lower diversity (extractive industries, certain financials). Because the screens are qualitative and subjective, two analysts reviewing the same company might rank it differently; the fund's methodology document spells out the criteria, but real-world application involves judgment calls.

Holdings concentration matters. NACP's eligibility universe is narrower than a broad-market index, so it typically holds sixty to eighty stocks rather than hundreds. This makes it more volatile than a diversified large-cap index; a few names' earnings misses can move the fund noticeably. The current holdings skew toward large-cap consumer and tech names where diversity announcements are frequent and scrutinized, which also means NACP tends to move with broad-market rallies and declines.

Turnover is another practical consideration. As companies' leadership changes, workforce metrics shift, or new diversity commitments come and go, the index methodology is rebalanced. High turnover creates transaction costs and generates short-term capital gains that may not be tax-efficient for taxable accounts. The fund's prospectus discloses the historical turnover rate and whether gains are typically short or long-term.

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) for NACP is in line with or slightly above typical thematic equity funds, reflecting the custom index construction and ongoing monitoring required to maintain the NAACP partnership.

A key question for any investor: Does holding a fund that applies social criteria deliver financial returns competitive with broad-market alternatives? Over extended periods, the answer depends partly on whether the companies meeting these criteria also have strong fundamentals — good returns on capital, sustainable competitive positions, reasonable valuations. If diversity correlates with better management and lower downside risk, NACP should outperform. If the screens select for popular names at elevated prices (a "diversity bubble"), NACP will underperform. The historical data is still limited; the fund has been available for less than a decade.

For someone seeking exposure to US equities aligned with social-impact criteria, NACP offers a simple, liquid vehicle. For someone indifferent to impact and focused solely on returns, a broader, lower-cost index would typically be preferable. For someone motivated by impact but skeptical of the NAACP's specific criteria, direct engagement with companies or investment in smaller, private diversity-focused vehicles might be more effective.

The fund's stated objective is to track an index, so fees are modest and tracking error (difference between fund and index return) is typically small — the fund is liquid and efficiently managed. Research should focus on whether the underlying index criteria align with your values or impact thesis, and whether the fund's historical returns justify the cost relative to broad-market alternatives.
