*The **Tradr 2X Long NBIS Daily ETF** (NEBX) is a leveraged exchange-traded fund that seeks to amplify daily returns of the NBIS index by a factor of two, using financial instruments to create that leverage within a structural constraint: the amplification resets every trading day.*

## The leverage structure and daily reset

NEBX holds a basket of securities and derivatives — typically futures, swaps, and options — to create a portfolio that aims to move twice as much as the NBIS index each trading day. If the index rises 1 per cent, NEBX targets a 2 per cent gain; if the index falls 1 per cent, NEBX targets a 2 per cent loss. This amplification happens within a single trading day. At the market close, the fund rebalances its holdings to reset the leverage and pursue the same two-to-one multiplier for the next day.

That daily reset is the crucial distinction between NEBX and a traditional leveraged position. Leverage itself — borrowing to magnify returns — has been used for centuries. But a day-trading [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) is a creature unique to modern finance: the fund must mathematically separate its returns into discrete daily units. This creates a surprising consequence: in markets that gyrate sideways or sideways-and-down, NEBX can lose money even if it gets the direction right over a longer span.

## Volatility decay and the long-term hazard

Imagine a market that falls 10 per cent on Day 1, then rises 10 per cent on Day 2, returning to where it started. NEBX, on Day 1, targets a 20 per cent loss, leaving the fund down 20 per cent from opening. On Day 2, it targets a 20 per cent gain off that diminished base, recovering roughly 16 per cent of assets. The fund is left down about 5 per cent even though the underlying index is flat. This is volatility decay — the mathematics of compounding daily resets over time. The more volatile the underlying index and the longer you hold NEBX, the more pronounced the decay becomes.

This is not a secret or a design flaw; it is baked into the product. NEBX's prospectus warns plainly that the fund is not suitable for [buy-and-hold investing](/buy-and-hold/). It is engineered for traders who expect a sharp, directional move over a period of days or weeks, not months or years. A trader who correctly bets on a 15 per cent index surge over two weeks might capture close to a 30 per cent gain in NEBX (minus costs). That same bet held for eighteen months, even if it ultimately proves correct, will likely underperform because of the drag from daily rebalancing through volatile stretches.

## Cost structure and who should use this

Leveraged ETFs typically carry higher [expense ratios](/expense-ratio/) than their unleveraged cousins, reflecting the cost of maintaining the leverage through derivatives and frequent rebalancing. NEBX trades throughout the day on an exchange, so you can enter and exit at market prices with a small [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/). The liquidity is there, but the fund is narrowly targeted: traders who want a 2X amplifier for NBIS exposure and nothing more.

The audience for NEBX is not the traditional buy-and-hold investor. It is the trader who believes the NBIS constituents will rise sharply over the next few weeks and wants to capitalize on that move with extra leverage. It is the hedger seeking to amplify a short-term directional position without opening a separate futures account. It is the sophisticated investor who understands the daily-reset mathematics and is willing to use that constraint as a feature rather than stumbling into it as a trap.

## Risks beyond the obvious

Leverage amplifies losses as much as gains. A 10 per cent index decline becomes a 20 per cent hit to NEBX, and a 50 per cent index crash becomes catastrophic. Volatility decay, discussed above, is a mathematical certainty over time — holding NEBX across volatile markets will drag on absolute returns. The daily reset also means NEBX is not a true leveraged position for any [holding period](/holding-period/) longer than one day; it is a series of day-long bets. If you hold NEBX expecting a simple 2X return over six months, you will likely be disappointed.

The underlying NBIS index itself carries risks — sector concentration, stock-specific volatility, and the particular timing of the index's composition. Because NEBX amplifies whatever happens in NBIS, those risks are simply magnified.

## How to research NEBX

Read the fund's prospectus carefully, paying close attention to the daily-reset and volatility-decay warnings. Understand exactly which securities and derivatives NEBX holds to create leverage. Use the fund's fact sheet to see the daily volatility of the underlying NBIS index — higher volatility means faster decay. Model out a few scenarios: a 20 per cent index move over two weeks, a sideways 20 per cent rally and decline over two months, and a steady climb over a year. See how NEBX performs in each case relative to simply holding NBIS with margin. That comparison will clarify whether NEBX's leverage structure suits your trading horizon.
