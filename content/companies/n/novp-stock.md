## A defined loss, not pure equity

NOVP is Prudential's entry in the growing cohort of buffered outcome ETFs — funds that trade unlimited upside for a guaranteed floor. Where a traditional S&P 500 ETF exposes an investor to every swing of the 500 largest American firms, NOVP absorbs the first 12 percent of any decline and offers an upside cap to compensate for that protection. The hedge rolls each November, creating a repeating cycle of one-year outcome periods.

The mechanics are straightforward in principle: the fund holds the underlying S&P 500 constituents but overlays [protective put](/protective-put/) options that activate if the market falls. Those puts cost money — extracted from the fund's net return — and are priced fresh each year according to prevailing volatility and [interest rates](/interest-rate/). In low-volatility years, the protection is comparatively cheap, so the upside cap can be wider. When volatility spikes, protection becomes expensive, shrinking the cap and the net potential gain.

## Who this serves, and why

NOVP appeals to a specific investor: someone with a long-term commitment to equity markets but who cannot psychologically or financially withstand a steep decline. A retiree who cannot afford to see a portfolio drop 30–40 percent has a genuine risk-management problem that simple [asset allocation](/asset-allocation/) (stocks and [bonds](/bond/)) might not solve elegantly. A [buffer ETF](/buffer-etf/) offers a middle path — they keep the expected equity returns but trade away the catastrophic [tail risk](/tail-risk/) and the very largest gains.

The annual reset is a feature for investors who are willing to revisit their strategy each year. If market conditions shift materially — if volatility falls, for instance, or if a [recession](/recession/) becomes evident — a new outcome period gives them a moment to assess whether the protection is still fitting. This is not buy-and-forget; it is structured alpha for thoughtful investors.

By contrast, younger accumulators with many decades until retirement should almost never use NOVP or funds like it. The compounding effect of capped upside over 30 or 40 years is brutal. The S&P 500 has historically returned roughly 10 percent annually in nominal terms; NOVP caps that return annually, with the actual cap varying year to year. Over three decades, that foregone compound growth represents hundreds of thousands of dollars in opportunity cost. For long horizons, [diversification](/diversification/) and patience are cheaper insurance.

## The annual repricing cycle

Every November, NOVP's outcome period matures and a new one begins. On that roll date, the fund's writers (using options markets) reprice the buffer and cap based on current market levels, volatility estimates, and [risk-free rates](/risk-free-rate/). A spike in [implied volatility](/implied-volatility/) makes protection more expensive, potentially narrowing the upside cap. A drop in volatility can widen it. An investor holding NOVP across multiple roll cycles will experience different hedge ratios and cap levels, creating a series of distinct one-year bets rather than one continuous protection umbrella.

The timing of rolls matters. If a major market decline begins in the weeks after November, the fund is starting its journey with fresh protection. If volatility spikes just before the roll, the incoming outcome period may have a tighter cap. An investor cannot time these mechanics perfectly, but awareness that the protection is periodic, not permanent, is essential to managing expectations.

## Cost and concentration

NOVP's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) includes both the cost of the options protection and the fund's administrative overhead. Compared to a vanilla S&P 500 ETF at 0.03–0.07 percent, NOVP typically runs 0.30–0.50 percent or higher, depending on the price of the underlying protection. That is a meaningful headwind over time, payable whether or not the buffer ever activates. Only in years where the market decline is genuine and sharp does that cost feel justified.

The fund is not hedged against the S&P 500's [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) — the recent dominance of a handful of mega-cap technology names, for instance. The buffer protects against index declines, whatever their source. Sector-specific crashes (a tech correction, for example) can still inflict heavy losses on NOVP, just not losses worse than roughly 12 percent below the period's starting level.

## Liquidity and trading

NOVP is a PGIM product on a major US exchange and thus has reasonable trading liquidity, though far lower than a standard S&P 500 [ETF. Bid-ask spreads](/etf-bid-ask-spread/) are wider and trading volumes lower, which means that investors transacting in size may face price impact. This is not a fund for frequent trading; it is designed as a multi-year holding through several annual cycles.

## Suitability checklist

Consider NOVP only if: you plan to stay invested in equities for at least three to five years, you cannot tolerate index-level declines of more than 12–15 percent without abandoning your strategy, you understand that upside is capped and will be lower than the market in good years, and you have carefully compared the total expected cost (the higher expense ratio compounded over your [holding period](/holding-period/)) against the psychological benefit of the buffer. If any of these fails, a plain S&P 500 fund combined with bonds or other defensive assets will likely serve better.
