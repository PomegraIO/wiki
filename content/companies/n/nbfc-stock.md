Neuberger Flexible Credit [Income ETF](/income-etf/) (ticker: NBFC) is an exchange-traded fund that specializes in credit exposure—the debt securities issued by corporations and other borrowers. Unlike a pure government-bond fund, NBFC seeks income by holding a mixture of [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) across the credit spectrum, from safer investment-grade debt to higher-yielding securities with more risk. The fund pursues flexibility in both its holdings and its approach to duration, meaning it can tilt toward shorter or longer maturity [bonds](/bond/) depending on where the fund manager sees opportunity or risk.

## What the fund holds

NBFC constructs a portfolio primarily of corporate debt—bonds issued by companies, often referred to as corporate credit. This includes conventional bonds (which pay fixed coupons on a set schedule), [floating-rate notes](/floating-rate-note/) (where the coupon adjusts periodically with [interest rates](/interest-rate/)), and other debt instruments like secured loans to private companies. The fund is not restricted to traditional public markets; Neuberger's approach typically includes a mix of bank loans, [preferred stock](/preferred-stock/), and other structured fixed-income assets. The name "flexible" signals that the fund does not lock into a specific maturity range: the manager can overweight shorter-term bonds in one environment and longer-dated debt in another, adjusting the fund's interest-rate sensitivity as conditions warrant.

## Why this fund exists

Credit-focused funds like NBFC appeal to investors seeking yield that exceeds government bonds, often called Treasuries. When Treasury yields are low, corporate bonds offer a broader spread—a higher interest rate to compensate for the added risk that a company might fail to repay. For investors who cannot afford to hold individual corporate bonds (due to minimum sizes, research costs, or the need for [diversification](/diversification/)), an ETF like NBFC offers pooled access to dozens or hundreds of credit securities, managed by professionals. The income-focused design targets investors who want cash flow from their holdings rather than pure capital appreciation, and the flexible structure appeals to managers who believe their timing and selection skills can improve returns by moving along the maturity spectrum.

## How it competes

Corporate credit is a crowded arena. NBFC faces competition from specialized [fixed-income ETFs](/fixed-income-etf/) run by iShares, Vanguard, SPDR, and dozens of others, each with a slightly different tilt—some favor short-maturity, low-risk debt; others target higher-yield [junk bonds](/junk-bond/); still others focus on floating-rate notes. Neuberger Berman brings a decades-long reputation as a fixed-income specialist and places its bet on active management and tactical flexibility. The fund's competitiveness depends on whether the manager's flexibility decisions—when to move up the credit spectrum toward riskier borrowers, when to extend duration—add value above the [management fee](/management-fee/). In a rising-rate environment, a fund that kept duration too long will lag; in a falling-rate environment, one that stayed too short will miss gains. The flexibility cuts both ways.

## What can go wrong

Credit funds live or die by the health of their underlying borrowers. In a [recession](/recession/), corporate defaults spike, bond values fall, and the spreads that make corporate debt attractive widen further, meaning existing bonds lose mark-to-market value before companies even miss payments. NBFC's willingness to hold lower-rated debt—the higher-yielding bonds issued by weaker borrowers—means it is more exposed to this risk than a fund holding only investment-grade debt. Rising interest rates also hurt credit funds, because they lower the market price of existing bonds (a bond paying 4% is less attractive if new bonds now pay 6%). Because NBFC is flexible on duration, it is not immune to rate moves, only less mechanically bound to a single maturity profile. The fund's complexity—holding bank loans, preferred stock, and [structured products](/structured-product/) alongside traditional corporate bonds—also requires constant attention to liquidity and valuation, since not all of these instruments trade as actively as large-cap corporate bonds do.

## How to research NBFC

Start with the fund's prospectus and factsheet on Neuberger Berman's website. The prospectus sets out the fund's mandate, fee structure, and what securities it is permitted to hold. The factsheet shows the current portfolio composition: the breakdown by maturity (how much is short-term versus long-term), by credit quality (how much is investment-grade versus high-yield), and by sector (finance, energy, consumer, industrials, and so on). Monitor the fund's monthly or quarterly holdings reports to understand what the manager is actually buying and selling. [Credit spreads](/credit-spread/)—the yield difference between corporate bonds and Treasuries—are the most important frame for evaluating whether credit is attractive. Widening spreads suggest the market is nervous about credit quality; tightening spreads suggest appetite for risk. A fund that holds NBFC should compare its performance and volatility against simpler alternatives, like a broad investment-grade corporate [bond ETF](/bond-etf/), to evaluate whether its flexibility and expertise justify the fees.
