MFS Charter Income Trust is a [closed-end fund](/closed-end-fund/) — a type of investment company that pools capital from shareholders and deploys it into a portfolio of bonds, [preferred stocks](/preferred-stock/), and other fixed-income securities. Unlike an open-end mutual fund, a closed-end fund has a fixed number of shares outstanding, and those shares trade on an exchange like a stock. The fund's primary purpose is to generate current income from interest and dividends and distribute that income to shareholders regularly, typically monthly or quarterly.

## The mechanics of a closed-end fund

A closed-end fund operates differently from the open-end [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) most investors encounter. When a closed-end fund is first offered to the public, it raises a set amount of capital through an [initial public offering](/initial-public-offering/) of shares. Once that capital is raised, the number of shares is fixed — investors cannot cause the fund to grow by buying new shares from the fund manager (as in an [open-end fund](/open-end-fund/)), nor cause it to shrink by redeeming shares. Instead, shares trade on an exchange, and the price is set by supply and demand among investors, not by the [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) of the underlying portfolio.

This structure has important implications. Because the fund can borrow money at the time of formation and use leverage to increase its income, a closed-end fund can often offer higher distributions to shareholders than an open-end fund with the same investment portfolio could offer. Leverage amplifies returns in both directions; in a rising market, it boosts gains, but in a falling market, losses are magnified. The tradeoff is explicit: higher income in exchange for higher risk.

## What MFS Charter Income Trust invests in

MFS Charter Income Trust invests in a diversified portfolio of fixed-income securities. This typically includes investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), government bonds, preferred stocks, and sometimes lower-rated (high-yield) bonds. The fund's managers make decisions about how much to allocate to each sector and credit quality based on their views of market conditions and relative value.

The goal is straightforward: find securities that generate income today. Interest on bonds, dividends on preferred stocks, and other current cash flows are collected and distributed to shareholders. The fund does not primarily aim to generate capital appreciation; it aims to provide a high [current yield](/current-yield/), and that income is the main reason shareholders own the fund.

## The income distribution and the leverage question

MFS Charter Income Trust typically distributes cash to shareholders monthly, and the distribution is fixed or semi-fixed at a rate chosen by the board and the manager. This predictability is part of the appeal to income-focused investors. However, the distribution can be deceptive. In a low-interest-rate environment, the yields available from bonds may be insufficient to pay the advertised distribution rate. To bridge that gap, the fund may use reserve capital — in effect, paying out some shareholders' principal back to them as income. This is known as a return of capital, and it is distinct from income generated by the portfolio.

Leverage is often part of the fund's strategy. By borrowing money at one rate and investing it at higher rates, the fund can amplify its income and its distributions. But this leverage introduces risk: if the portfolio declines in value, the leverage magnifies the loss, because the borrowed money must still be repaid. A sharp sell-off in the bond market can force the fund to liquidate holdings at poor prices or reduce its leverage, which reduces the distribution.

## The risks specific to closed-end bond funds

The core risk is interest-rate sensitivity. When [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise, the value of existing bonds falls (because newly issued bonds offer higher yields). If a shareholder buys MCR shares and interest rates rise sharply, the value of the fund's portfolio declines, and the market price of MCR shares typically falls. If the shareholder needs to sell before the bonds mature, they may realize a loss.

A second risk is market price versus net asset value (NAV). Because MCR trades on an exchange like a stock, its market price can diverge from the underlying value of its portfolio. Sometimes the fund trades at a discount to NAV (investors are pessimistic and willing to buy shares for less than the portfolio is worth), and sometimes at a premium (optimism drives the price up). If you buy at a premium, you are paying more than the portfolio is worth, which is a headwind on returns.

Credit risk is a third concern. If the bonds or preferred stocks the fund holds deteriorate in credit quality, their value falls. A recession or sector downturn can hurt the issuers the fund has lent to, and in the worst case, default and principal loss are possible.

Finally, there is distribution risk. The fund's ability to pay its stated distribution depends on both the income from its portfolio and its ability to access leverage and manage its [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/). If spreads widen dramatically or credit conditions tighten, the fund's cost of leverage can rise, squeezing the income available for distribution. In extreme cases, the board may reduce the distribution to preserve capital.

## How to research and track a closed-end income fund

Investors in MFS Charter Income Trust should start with the fund's [annual reports](/10-k/) and fact sheets, which spell out the portfolio composition, the current yield, the [leverage ratio](/leverage-ratio-forex/), and the distribution history. The SEC filings provide complete transparency about holdings.

Key metrics to track are the discount or premium to NAV (buy near a discount, be wary of buying at a premium), the yield relative to comparable funds and to the broader bond market, and the trend in leverage. A rising leverage ratio in a rising-rate environment is a warning sign.

Listen to management commentary on the interest-rate outlook, the credit environment, and whether the fund's distribution is sustainable from current income or is drawing down reserves. A fund whose distribution exceeds its portfolio income for several quarters is gradually eating its seed corn.

Because MFS Charter Income Trust is part of a larger family of funds and a professional asset manager (MFS), the manager's track record, stability, and consistency in deploying capital matter. A change in management or a shift in investment philosophy can alter the fund's risk-return profile.

## The appeal and the caveat

Closed-end bond funds like MCR appeal to investors seeking higher income in a low-rate environment or to those seeking exposure to leverage to amplify income. The distributions are often higher than what an individual could assemble buying bonds directly. However, that higher income comes with higher risk and the possibility of principal loss. The steady distribution can mask the fact that the underlying asset base is shrinking if distributions exceed income, or that leverage is increasing market sensitivity. A careful investor should understand whether the distribution is sustainable and whether the price they are paying for the fund's shares reflects fair value or includes an inflated premium.
