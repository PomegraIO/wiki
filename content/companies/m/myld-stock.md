*The **Cambria Micro and SmallCap Shareholder Yield ETF** ([NASDAQ](/nasdaq/): MYLD) is a concentrated equity fund that targets micro-cap and small-cap US firms that reward shareholders through [dividends](/dividend/) and [share buybacks](/share-buyback/), with a leverage overlay that increases the fund's exposure during market upswings and reduces it during downturns.*

The fund marries three ideas: that small and micro-cap stocks — companies with market values below roughly $3 billion — offer growth opportunities that larger firms cannot match; that firms actively returning cash to shareholders through buybacks and dividends tend to be better managed and more resilient; and that a dynamic leverage strategy (borrowing to invest more when conditions favour it, and delevering when they do not) can amplify returns without adding permanent fixed costs.

## How MYLD works

The fund starts by selecting from the universe of US micro-cap and small-cap equities — roughly two to three hundred companies at any time — those with the highest shareholder yield. Shareholder yield is the sum of a firm's [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) and the annualized rate at which it buys back its own shares, expressed as a percentage of the stock price. A firm that pays a 2% dividend and repurchases 3% of its shares annually has a 5% shareholder yield. This metric appeals to value-oriented investors because it captures all the ways a company returns wealth to shareholders, not just the visible dividend.

Once the holdings are selected, MYLD overlays a leverage strategy tied to market momentum. When the market is trending upward, the fund borrows money to amplify its stock positions — turning a $100 investment into, say, $110 or $120 of actual stock exposure. When market conditions deteriorate or momentum fades, the leverage unwinds, dampening the fund's sensitivity to further declines. This is not a fixed 2x or 3x leverage like some Direxion or ProShares products; it flexes based on technical signals and volatility.

The effect, in theory, is to buy more exposure when conditions favour it and retreat when they do not. In practice, leverage adds two distinct costs: the interest expense of borrowing, and the drag from being wrong — if momentum signals flip unexpectedly or if the market falls sharply while the fund is still leveraged, those magnified losses can quickly erase gains.

## The shareholder-yield lens through cycles

During expansions, when corporate profits are rising and management teams are confident enough to accelerate buybacks, shareholder-yield stocks tend to perform well. The combination of growing earnings and shrinking share counts lifts per-share metrics and can drive outperformance. Micro and small-cap firms are particularly sensitive to economic cycles — they have less financial cushion than blue-chips and more exposure to [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and credit availability — so rising rates or slowing growth can swiftly reverse their appeal.

Downturns expose the fund's vulnerabilities. Firms that were repurchasing shares aggressively during good times often halt buybacks immediately when earnings weaken, removing a support for the stock price. Some cut dividends as cash reserves dry up. And because MYLD holds smaller, less liquid companies (compared to broad small-cap indices), any repricing can be sharp; spreads widen, and getting out of positions becomes expensive. The leverage overlay, if still engaged during early-stage declines, compounds losses.

## Costs and fit

The fund charges an above-average [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) for an [equity ETF](/equity-etf/) — reflecting both the cost of the strategy and the limited scale it has accumulated relative to flagship small-cap indices. The leverage is not free; interest costs accumulate even when the market is flat. Distributions tend to be ordinary income (from dividends and interest) rather than long-term capital gains, making MYLD less tax-efficient in taxable accounts than a buy-and-hold broad-market fund.

The [holding period](/holding-period/) matters. MYLD is designed for investors who believe small-cap stocks will deliver outsized returns over full [market cycles](/market-cycle/) and are comfortable with the heightened volatility and drawdown risk that come with both the leverage and the small-cap universe itself. It is not a core position for long-term retirement accounts; it is a tactical stake for someone with conviction about micro and small-cap outperformance and the stomach to sit through the inevitable deep declines without panic-selling.

## How to research it

Start with the fund's fact sheet and prospectus (available on the issuer's website), which lay out the methodology for selecting holdings and the mechanics of the leverage policy in detail. Track the fund's actual [leverage ratio](/leverage-ratio-forex/) over time — whether it is growing, stable, or declining tells you how the portfolio managers are reading current market conditions. Watch the portfolio turnover, which indicates how frequently positions change; higher turnover means more transaction costs and less tax efficiency. Compare MYLD's performance against both broader small-cap indices (like the Russell 2000) and other small-cap value and dividend-focused competitors to see whether the shareholder-yield lens and leverage strategy are adding value net of fees.
