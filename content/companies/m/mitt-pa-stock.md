What is it? A REIT — [real estate investment trust](/real-estate-investment-trust/) — that buys and holds residential mortgages and mortgage-backed securities. The company was formerly called AG Mortgage Investment Trust; it rebranded to [TPG Mortgage Investment](/mitt-stock/) Trust in December 2025 as part of an affiliation or ownership shift. The preferred [share class](/share-class/) MITT-PA represents senior preferred equity in the company.

Business segments — three of them. Securities (residential mortgage-backed securities and [bonds](/bond/)). Loans (whole mortgages, either non-agency, agency-eligible, or home equity). [Single-family rental](/single-family-rental/) properties. Revenue comes from net interest income — the spread between what the mortgages yield and the cost of funding them — plus investment gains when the company sells securities or loans at a profit.

The mortgage market matters here. Non-agency mortgages are loans that do not meet the standard criteria that government agencies ([Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/), [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/), Ginnie Mae) require. Borrowers might have lower credit scores, larger loan sizes, or property types agencies will not back. Non-agency loans are riskier but yield more. Agency-eligible mortgages meet agency standards but the company keeps them off-balance-sheet rather than selling them. [Home equity loans](/home-equity-loan/) are second mortgages or lines of credit on owner-occupied homes.

## Funding and leverage

A [mortgage REIT](/mortgage-reit/) does not lend from capital; it borrows short-term money ([repurchase agreements](/repurchase-agreement/), or "repos"), uses that money to buy mortgages that yield more, and pockets the difference. Mortgage origination companies do the same thing. The REIT is basically a leveraged mortgage investor. In 2025, the company executed ten [securitizations](/securitization/) — pooled mortgages that it sold to investors — which is the primary way mortgage REITs fund their portfolios and lock in funding costs.

The leverage works in the company's favor when the spread widens (mortgages yield a lot more than repo funding costs). It works against the company when the spread compresses or when the value of the mortgages it holds falls (because [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise). A mortgage REIT in 2022, when the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raised rates sharply, suffered significant losses as the mark-to-market value of held mortgages plummeted. The company manages this risk by matching the duration of its funding to the maturity of its mortgages, by hedging with [interest rate swaps](/interest-rate-swap/), and by adjusting its portfolio allocation based on interest rate outlook.

## The mortgage REIT sector

Mortgage REITs exist to exploit the spread between mortgage yields and funding costs. When that spread is wide, returns are strong. When it is narrow, returns compress. The spread is determined by macro conditions: the Federal Reserve's policy rate, the [yield curve shape](/yield-curve-shape/), and [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) (how much more borrowers pay above risk-free rates). A REIT manager's skill is partly in predicting those spreads and allocating capital accordingly, partly in managing portfolio duration and [credit risk](/credit-risk/), and partly in executing securitizations and funding at favorable costs.

Peers include New York Mortgage Trust, Two Harbors, INVESCO Mortgage Capital, and others. All are trying to harvest the spread between mortgages and funding. Differentiation comes from the portfolio allocation (what types of mortgages they buy), the funding strategy (how much leverage and what type), and the operator's cost of capital (larger, better-capitalized REITs can fund more cheaply). TPG Mortgage Trust has diversified into non-agency loans and single-family rentals, which offers some variation in return drivers compared to pure mortgage-backed securities investors.

## Credit and interest rate risk

Credit risk is the chance that borrowers stop paying. Non-agency mortgages carry more credit risk than agency mortgages (which are implicitly backed by the government). The company manages this by buying mortgages with substantial down payments, strong borrower credit, and performing loans (not delinquent ones). In a [recession](/recession/) with sharp unemployment, even credit-quality borrowers stop paying. The company underwrites assuming unemployment rises and rates rise, and it prices mortgages to absorb some loss.

Interest rate risk is the risk that the value of held mortgages falls because rates rise. A mortgage yielding 4 percent is less valuable if new mortgages pay 6 percent. The company hedges this with swaps and by matching the maturity of its funding to its mortgages, but perfect hedges are expensive or unavailable. This is the main risk a mortgage REIT faces in a rising-rate environment.

## Preferred shares and MITT-PA

MITT-PA is a preferred share class of TPG Mortgage Investment Trust. Preferred shares rank above common equity in claims on cash and assets but below debt holders. The dividend on preferred shares is typically fixed and paid quarterly. If the REIT generates enough income, the preferred dividend is paid in full. If the REIT is struggling, the preferred dividend might be deferred (though usually not suspended without serious distress).

Investors buy preferred shares for the higher yield than [common stock](/common-stock/) and the senior claim on dividends and assets in a [liquidation](/liquidation/). The trade-off is limited upside — if the REIT thrives, preferred holders still get the fixed dividend and little more. In a downturn, preferred shares can fall in value, though they should outperform common in a bankruptcy.

## Dividend and income

A mortgage REIT distributes most of its taxable income to shareholders as a requirement of REIT status. For income-focused investors, the dividend is the main return. Over the past year, TPG Mortgage paid dividends that supported a return exceeding 42 percent when combined with stock price appreciation. That level of return is not sustainable — it reflects a favorable 2025 for mortgage spreads and markets. Investors should assume a normalized return of 6 to 10 percent if dividend stability holds and rates remain stable.

## Operational notes

The company runs securitizations regularly to manage portfolio liquidity and funding costs. Watch for regular securitization activity; a slowdown signals trouble accessing capital markets. Pay attention to the company's portfolio composition: what percentage is non-agency versus agency-eligible, how much is home equity, what is the average mortgage rate and maturity. Shifts in composition signal changes in the company's risk appetite or view on the market.

Credit losses matter. Any quarter with reported loan losses indicates stress in the borrower base. Rising delinquency rates (mortgages 30+ days late) are a leading indicator of future losses.

## Metric dashboard for monitoring

**Dividend yield:** The preferred dividend divided by the price per share. Compare to other preferred shares and Treasury bonds; a widening spread signals higher perceived risk.

**Book value per share:** The company's net asset value, adjusted for mark-to-market of held mortgages. A decline in book value signals portfolio losses or unrealized mark-downs.

**Net interest margin:** Net interest income divided by average earning assets. A decline signals shrinking spreads and profitability pressure.

**Loan loss provisions:** Reserves set aside for expected defaults. Rising provisions suggest deteriorating credit conditions.

**Securitization volume:** Quarterly securitizations and the funding costs achieved. A slowdown or rising costs signal market stress or company-specific issues.

**Leverage ratio:** Total assets divided by equity. Higher leverage magnifies both returns and losses. Extreme leverage is a warning sign if combined with rate or credit stress.

## Perspective

Mortgage REITs are financial engineering vehicles — they exist to exploit the spread between mortgages and funding costs, not to create value by building businesses or managing assets productively. The returns depend almost entirely on macro conditions, the yield curve, credit spreads, and the Fed's policy rate. Management skill matters, but it is secondary to the environment. In a favorable environment, the REIT returns attractive income. In an unfavorable one, shareholders suffer losses and dividend cuts. This is not a buy-and-hold-forever business; it is a tactical investment tied to macro outlook.
