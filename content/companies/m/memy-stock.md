> "The fund attempts to profit from both the stock appreciation and the volatility of meme names themselves — a bet that the crowd and the options market will keep them lively."

Tuttle Capital Meme Stock Income Blast ETF (MEMY) is an optionality strategy fund: it holds an index of meme stocks and simultaneously sells [call options](/call-option/) against those holdings, collecting [option premiums](/option-premium/) as income. This is known as a covered-call strategy.

The meme-stock universe is the same as the concept tracked by other meme-themed ETFs — equities that have attracted outsized retail community enthusiasm and trading volume. The innovation in MEMY is the income overlay. The fund's managers sell call options on the meme holdings, meaning they accept the obligation to sell shares at a specified price above the current stock price. The buyer of that call option — typically an options trader seeking upside exposure — pays a premium for this right. MEMY pockets that premium as current income, reducing the fund's distribution yield to shareholders.

The covered-call trade-off is straightforward: you keep the premium paid by the [option buyer](/option-buyer/), but you cap your upside. If a meme stock rallies sharply above the [strike price](/strike-price/) of the sold call, the shares are called away at that price and you miss the additional gain. The covered-call strategy works best in range-bound or moderately rising markets where volatility is elevated enough to make options expensive, so the premium collected is meaningful, but the underlying stocks do not spike dramatically higher.

Because meme stocks are volatile, the options written against them carry high premiums. This makes the income collection attractive — the call options are valuable because the market expects these stocks to move. It also means the capped-upside risk is real. If a meme name suddenly soars on renewed community enthusiasm, MEMY holders will not fully participate.

The fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) includes the cost of the frequent option-writing activity and the rebalancing required as the underlying meme-stock index turns over. Turnover in MEMY is higher than in a simple meme-stock holding because the option positions must be managed continuously — old calls expire and new ones are written, strikes are selected, and the overall portfolio must be adjusted to manage risk.

The risks are compounded. Meme stocks are volatile by nature; [covered calls](/covered-call/) dampen that volatility but at the cost of upside. If the portfolio experiences a sharp decline, the short call options provide no downside protection — the call seller is not insured against large losses in the underlying stock, only obligated to deliver shares if called away at a profit. In a severe market downturn, MEMY could experience steep losses while still being obligated to sell shares to call holders at pre-determined prices, an unfavorable outcome.

The [dividend](/dividend/) or distribution from MEMY will be higher than from a simple meme-stock fund because of the premium collection, but that distribution is partly return of capital rather than earned income. The true economic benefit of the covered-call strategy depends on the realized volatility of the underlying stocks relative to the [implied volatility](/implied-volatility/) priced into the options.

To research MEMY, examine the prospectus to understand the option-writing frequency and strike-selection methodology. Check the historical income distribution relative to the underlying meme-stock index and relative to simpler meme-stock funds. Review the fund's performance in both rising markets (where call selling has hurt) and falling markets (where the premium cushioned losses). Look at the expense ratio and turnover. Monitor how the covered-call strategy has performed during periods of extreme meme-stock volatility versus periods of calm. Any investor considering MEMY should understand that they are not simply holding meme stocks but making a specific bet that the covered-call income stream will exceed the opportunity cost of capped upside.
