*The **NEOS MLP & Energy Infrastructure High [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (MLPI) is an actively managed portfolio of master limited partnerships and energy-infrastructure equities, designed to deliver high current income from distributions and capital appreciation.*

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Strategy** | Active selection of MLPs and energy-infrastructure equities for income and growth |
| **Yield target** | High current distribution, typically in the high single-digit to low double-digit range |
| **Sector focus** | Midstream energy (pipelines, storage, logistics), energy services, infrastructure operators |
| **Management approach** | Discretionary stock selection by NEOS investment team, not index-tracking |
| **Tax treatment** | MLP distributions taxed as ordinary income; may include K-1 forms |
| **Typical holding period** | Long-term, with attention to distribution sustainability |

MLPI differs from passive MLP [index funds](/index-fund/) in that a dedicated management team actively selects holdings based on their assessment of company quality, distribution safety, and growth potential. This is not a passive track-an-index strategy; it is a bet on the skill and judgment of the fund managers. The idea is that careful security selection can identify MLPs with above-average distribution growth, stronger [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/), and better resilience through energy cycles compared to the broader peer group.

The management team conducts bottom-up analysis of individual MLPs and energy-infrastructure companies, evaluating factors like distribution-coverage ratios (is the partnership generating enough cash to safely maintain or grow its payout?), leverage (how indebted is the partnership relative to its cash flow?), contract quality (are revenues from long-term offtake agreements or volatile spot markets?), and management track records. The goal is to own the best operators in the space — those most likely to preserve distributions during downturns and grow them during upswings.

## The cyclical bet embedded in active MLP selection

An actively managed MLP fund's performance relative to passive MLP funds hinges on how well the manager navigates energy cycles. In a strong energy environment, with high utilization and capacity constraints, almost all MLPs do well, and the active manager's edge is modest. In a deteriorating energy environment, selective stock picking becomes critical. A manager who avoids highly leveraged partnerships before a downturn, who gravitates toward those with long-term contracts that lock in volumes despite price softness, and who exits deteriorating credits early will outperform a passive index fund. A manager who gets these calls wrong will underperform.

The 2016 oil-price crash provided a test case. Passive MLP indices suffered significant losses as some partnerships cut distributions, and the sector went through a brutal reset. Active managers who had favoured defensive names with shorter leverage and contract-backed cash flows outperformed those holding the full sector cap-weighted. Since then, energy has recovered in fits and starts, and the relative performance of active MLP managers has been mixed.

## Distribution safety and leverage

A critical skill in managing an MLP fund is assessing whether a partnership's distributions are truly sustainable or at risk of cuts. A partnership that pays out 8% but generates only 5% in [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) is using balance-sheet cash or debt to fund the payment — an unsustainable trajectory. During booms this can persist; during busts it fails. MLPI's managers should be analyzing coverage ratios (cash flow divided by distributions paid) and trend, asking which partnerships have the financial flexibility to maintain or grow distributions if volumes or prices soften, and steering the portfolio toward the defensible names.

## Active management fees and the hurdle rate

Because MLPI is actively managed, its [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is higher than a passive MLP index fund. The fund's managers must clear a hurdle: they must outperform a passive MLP index by enough to justify the extra fees. Over long periods (10 years or more), many active managers fail this test. The cost of security selection, trading costs, and [management fees](/management-fee/) often exceed the value created by superior stock picking. For MLPI to be worth owning, the managers must have demonstrated a genuine edge — either through a published track record or through a clear, repeatable investment philosophy that has worked in the past.

## Energy transition and the long-term backdrop

Like all MLP-focused funds, MLPI faces the structural headwind of energy transition. As renewables grow and hydrocarbon demand potentially plateaus or declines, the long-term demand for fossil-fuel transport and storage diminishes. An active manager might try to navigate this by tilting toward energy-infrastructure partnerships that are diversified into renewable-energy logistics, hydrogen transport, or carbon-capture infrastructure — emerging segments that could grow. A manager stuck in pure traditional midstream faces a slow-motion decline in the underlying market.

## Practical considerations for investors

Anyone considering MLPI should ask three questions. First, what is the fund's track record versus a passive MLP index? Does MLPI's outperformance exceed its fee premium? Second, is the management team stable, or have there been significant departures? A manager's edge often travels with the person. Third, what is the fund's exposure to energy-transition risks? Does the portfolio hold partnerships that are adapting to decarbonization, or is it purely traditional midstream?
