The MFS Government Markets Income Trust (MGF) is a closed-end fund — a pool of money managed by professionals and traded on an exchange, much like a stock. The fund exists to hold and generate income from bonds issued by the U.S. government and government-related entities. Its customers are individual investors seeking a steady stream of dividend payments backed by the creditworthiness of the U.S. government.

## What exactly does MGF hold?

MGF's portfolio consists almost entirely of bonds. As of its most recent holdings report, the fund held approximately 99% in bonds: roughly 94% in U.S. government bonds and government-related securities ([Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/), agency bonds from entities like Fannie Mae or [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/), and similar instruments), and about 5% in asset-backed or securitized securities tied to government programs. The remaining 1% is typically cash or very short-term holdings. There are no stocks in the portfolio. The fund is purely fixed income.

The bonds in the fund mature over various horizons, from near-term (months away) to multi-year maturities. MFS actively manages the portfolio, deciding what maturity ladder to build, whether to favor longer bonds (which pay more interest but move more in price when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) change) or shorter bonds (which are more stable but pay less), and which government-related securities offer the best value. These decisions drive the fund's performance.

## Why would an investor buy MGF instead of Treasury bonds directly?

A retail investor who wants to own U.S. Treasury bonds can buy them directly from the government through TreasuryDirect or from a broker. Why buy through a closed-end fund instead? There are several reasons. First, professional management: MFS has expert bond analysts who can assess whether longer-term bonds are fairly valued, can navigate complexities around government-related securities and mortgage-backed paper that the average investor might not understand, and can actively adjust the portfolio in response to changing interest-rate conditions. Second, instant [diversification](/diversification/): buying MGF shares gives you exposure to a large basket of government securities with a single trade, rather than buying individual bonds. Third, a trading mechanism: MGF shares trade continuously on the [New York Stock Exchange](/new-york-stock-exchange/), so you can buy or sell your position instantly at market prices, whereas buying actual Treasury bonds directly requires opening an account and navigating auctions or the [secondary market](/secondary-market/).

Fourth, and not incidentally, MGF pays distributions to shareholders. Like other [closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/), MGF collects the interest income from its bonds and distributes much of it to shareholders monthly or quarterly. Many individual investors value this predictable income stream, especially retirees looking for cash flow. The yield on MGF (the annualized distribution divided by the share price) can be higher than what an investor would earn buying Treasury bonds directly, especially if MFS is investing in longer-maturity bonds that pay higher interest.

## What is the relationship between MGF's price and the value of the bonds inside?

MGF trades on the [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/), and like all closed-end funds, its share price can diverge from the [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) (NAV) of the bonds it holds. If sentiment toward bond funds is bullish, MGF shares might trade at a premium to NAV — meaning you pay more per share than the bonds inside are mathematically worth. If sentiment sours, shares might trade at a discount — you buy a share for less than the bonds are worth, which from a pure value perspective is attractive. This premium-discount dynamic is unique to closed-end funds; they do not automatically rebalance their share count the way open-ended [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) do. Sophisticated investors monitor MGF's premium or discount to NAV as part of their decision to buy or sell.

## What drives MGF's returns?

Returns come from two sources. The first is the interest income the bonds generate, which MGF distributes to shareholders. If you own the shares for a year and collect all the distributions, that is your current-yield return. The second is capital appreciation or [depreciation](/depreciation/) based on the price of the bonds themselves. When interest rates fall, existing bonds (which pay fixed interest) become more valuable — investors are willing to pay more to own them. When rates rise, bonds fall in value. An investor who buys MGF at $10 per share and sells it a year later for $11 per share, plus collected distributions of $0.50, has realized a total return of 15%. But if rates rose and pushed bond prices down, MGF shares might be trading at $9 instead, leading to a capital loss despite the distributions.

## Is MGF appropriate as a complete portfolio?

No. MGF is a single-asset, single-country bond fund focusing entirely on government-related securities. A complete investment portfolio typically includes stocks (for growth), bonds (for stability and income), and diversification across geography, sector, and credit quality. MGF covers only one corner of that landscape. It is best thought of as a satellite position — part of a broader bond allocation — rather than a core holding. An investor holding MGF alongside equity funds or diversified funds achieves broader diversification.

## How to research MGF

Start with the fund's most recent monthly or quarterly fact sheet, available on the MFS website, which shows the current NAV, share price, premium or discount, the [current yield](/current-yield/), and a snapshot of holdings. The SEC filing called a NPORT-P (filed quarterly) provides detailed holdings and portfolio breakdowns. Watch the distributions over time: a rising distribution usually signals increasing yields or extending maturity in the bond portfolio, while a flat or falling distribution suggests lower yields or defensive positioning. Track MGF's share price relative to its NAV to get a sense of whether the fund is trading at a bargain or at an inflated price. Finally, remember that MGF's share price moves with interest-rate expectations: falling rates support capital appreciation for bond holders, and rising rates work against them. Understanding where interest rates are expected to go is central to understanding MGF's outlook.
