*MPRO is an exchange-traded fund that tracks the ProCap Mega Cap and Micro Cap Value Index, a US equity strategy engineered to blend exposure to the largest 200 companies in the market with a tilt toward small, undervalued stocks—a bet that value is accessible across the size spectrum.*

## The ProCap index logic

The index that MPRO tracks is not one of the household-name benchmarks (like the S&P 500 or the Russell 2000); it is a purpose-built strategy from ProCap Advisors, designed to capture a particular view of market opportunity. Its core idea is that compounding returns sit at both ends of the size spectrum: the most liquid, network-rich mega-cap stocks benefit from moat durability, while micro-cap companies offer asymmetric upside for investors patient enough to hold illiquid positions. The index weights the top 200 companies by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) and then layers in a basket of micro-cap stocks, each weighted by market cap within its group. This bimodal construction differs from a straight market-cap-weighted index (which would be dominated by mega-cap names) or a traditional size-based index (which would segment large and small into separate buckets). MPRO's weighting scheme aims to marry the stability of mega-cap exposure with the asymmetric return potential of smaller names.

## How the index construction changes with markets

The 200 mega-cap holdings adjust as market values shift and companies enter and exit the top tier. Micro-cap holdings are eligible based on size and value criteria, which means the constituent list is dynamic. Quarterly or semi-annual rebalancing snaps the fund back to the target weights, a process that can entail buying stocks that have fallen (and become cheaper) and selling those that have surged. This [rebalancing discipline](/rebalancing-discipline/) is the primary source of the fund's turnover and its [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) in real trading. During periods when mega-cap stocks and micro-cap stocks move in sync, rebalancing friction is minimal; when they diverge sharply (as happens during rallies that lift the largest names while depressing small-cap valuations), turnover increases and the fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) becomes a larger drag relative to the index itself.

## Sector and style drift in ProCap construction

The index does not impose sector constraints, so its holdings gravitating toward certain industries reflects organic market concentration rather than design. In periods when mega-cap technology stocks dominate the market's gains, MPRO tends to be overweight there. Similarly, the micro-cap tilt has historically carried an exposure to value stocks (low price-to-book, low price-to-earnings names), though value is not the explicit rule—it is a tendency within the micro-cap universe that the index captures. Investors should not mistake MPRO for a pure [value fund](/value-fund/); it is a size-blended fund that happens to tilt that way. The fund's performance will differ materially from a pure large-cap or pure small-cap strategy, and it will tend to underperform in rallies driven purely by mega-cap growth stocks while it may outperform when smaller companies stage recoveries.

## Costs and liquidity

MPRO's expense ratio is among the lower costs in the actively managed index space, pricing the fund competitively against custom indices and traditional [index funds](/index-fund/). The fund trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) with typical spreads that narrow or widen depending on intraday volume. On a heavy volume day a typical investor might see a bid-ask spread of 0.05 percent or less; in thinner market conditions the spread can widen to 0.20 percent or more. For investors making large purchases or selling positions suddenly, this cost can be material. The fund holds enough assets and daily volume to make it liquid for retail and institutional trades under normal market conditions; extreme stress scenarios (pandemic-era [trading halts](/trading-halts/), flash-crash conditions) have occasionally driven wider spreads across all exchange-traded products, but MPRO's blend of large and small holdings generally provides some resilience.

## Research and fit

An investor considering MPRO should first understand what the ProCap Index itself is designed to do. The fund's prospectus lays out the construction rules, the eligible universe, and the rebalancing frequency. Comparing MPRO's performance to a straight market-cap-weighted index (such as the S&P 500 or the total US market index) over a full [market cycle](/market-cycle/)—including years when large caps lead and years when smaller names rebound—shows how much of the fund's return comes from the size tilt versus fees. The fund's [annual report](/10-k/) discloses the top ten holdings and sector weights, and watching how those shift each quarter reveals whether the index is staying true to its design or drifting into unintended tilts. For anyone deploying significant capital, consulting the full prospectus and holding-level detail from the fund sponsor is the starting point; no ETF summary can substitute for direct scrutiny of what you own and how it is weighted.
