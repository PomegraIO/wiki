*The **Motley Fool Innovative Growth ETF** (ticker MFIG) is an actively managed exchange-traded fund built on the investment philosophy of Motley Fool, a financial education and stock-research platform. Rather than passively tracking a predetermined index, MFIG's managers curate a portfolio of companies they believe have sustainable competitive moats and significant room to compound earnings over many years. The fund targets investors who want professional stock selection focused on quality growth, with the daily liquidity and tax efficiency of an ETF.*

## The Motley Fool investment philosophy

Motley Fool's framework emphasizes several foundational principles. The first is competitive moat — the company should have a defensible advantage rooted in brand loyalty, switching costs, network effects, or economies of scale that allow it to earn attractive returns on capital for years. The second is management quality — a capital-allocation mindset that prioritizes long-term shareholder value over quarterly earnings. The third is recurring revenue, which provides visibility and reduces business volatility. The fourth is exposure to secular tailwinds: cloud computing, digital payments, aging demographics, renewable energy, or other structural trends that will drive demand for decades.

This framework is fundamentally different from indexing. An index fund owns all the companies that meet a mechanical rule (by market cap, sector weight, or membership in a published index). MFIG's managers make judgment calls about which companies deserve space in the portfolio based on proprietary research. If they believe a large-cap technology company is overvalued despite strong competitive position, they may avoid or underweight it. If they identify a smaller company with an emerging moat and secular tailwind, they may give it meaningful weight. This discretion introduces both upside and downside risk — upside if the managers' judgment is sound, downside if it is not.

## How MFIG differs from growth indices

A growth-focused index fund — tracking the NASDAQ or a growth slice of the S&P 500 — holds a fixed, formula-driven list determined by size and category. MFIG holds a discretionary selection that can differ significantly in sector exposure, company size, and growth themes. The fund may be underweight software and overweight healthcare innovation if the managers believe the latter has better long-term economics. Holdings are not static. As companies mature, as competitive conditions shift, or as new opportunities emerge, the managers rotate in and out of positions.

## Portfolio composition and sector exposure

MFIG typically holds 30 to 50 stocks, with weights determined by the managers' conviction in the business quality and growth runway rather than by market-cap rules. The portfolio spans technology, healthcare, consumer discretionary, industrials, and communications sectors — anywhere quality and durable growth can be found. The specific names change over time as managers adjust their views on competitive position, management execution, and valuation.

## Costs and trading mechanics

The expense ratio is higher than a passive growth index fund would be, reflecting active research, stock analysis, and portfolio management. It is typically lower than a traditional growth mutual fund because the ETF structure is cheaper to operate. METU trades on an exchange throughout the day at market-set prices with good liquidity, meaning investors can buy and sell at tight spreads without significant friction.

## Active management risks

The primary risk is that the managers' stock picks simply underperform a passive growth index, after fees, over a long period. This is the central challenge of active management: most active funds fail to beat their benchmarks on a net-of-fees basis over decades, either because fees are high or because outperforming the market consistently is genuinely difficult.

A second risk is concentration. Highly selective managers may overweight their best convictions, creating a portfolio that is more volatile and less diversified than a broad growth index. A downturn in those specific themes or companies hits harder.

Third, growth stocks as a category are sensitive to interest rates and inflation expectations. In periods of rising rates or high inflation, growth falls out of favor relative to value stocks or dividend payers, and MFIG may underperform significantly.

Finally, active management involves portfolio turnover, which generates tax consequences in taxable accounts. MFIG in a tax-deferred retirement account is cleaner than in a standard brokerage account.

## Who MFIG suits

The fund appeals to investors who believe Motley Fool's stock-picking philosophy is sound, who trust that skilled active managers can identify long-term winners before the market does, and who prefer the simplicity and tax efficiency of an ETF structure to a mutual fund. It also suits investors who follow Motley Fool's published research and stock recommendations and want to align their portfolio with that approach.

## Research and evaluation

Start by reviewing the fund's current holdings and Motley Fool's published thesis for each position — the firm publishes extensive stock research. Compare MFIG's returns to passive growth benchmarks (NASDAQ-100, S&P 500 Growth) over one-, three-, and five-year periods, net of fees. Look at turnover and portfolio changes to understand how actively the managers trade. Most importantly, evaluate your own conviction about active management. If you believe Motley Fool's research process beats passive indices over time, MFIG is a natural vehicle. If you are skeptical of active management generally, a low-cost passive growth fund will likely serve you better.
