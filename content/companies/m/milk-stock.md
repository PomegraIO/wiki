*The **Pacer US Cash Cows [Bond ETF](/bond-etf/)** (ticker MILK) is a [bond](/bond/) fund that invests in corporate debt issued by U.S. companies selected for strong, stable cash generation — a "cash cow" being a business that reliably converts revenue into cash. Unlike a standard [corporate bond](/corporate-bond/) fund that weights issuers by market value or [credit rating](/credit-rating/), MILK weights them by their [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/), tilting the portfolio toward companies most likely to sustain interest payments through economic cycles.*

## The origin: cash flow as a bond quality signal

Pacer Advisors, an options-focused quant manager, created MILK around 2016 to test a specific thesis: that bondholders should prefer companies generating abundant free cash flow, because cash-rich companies are less likely to default and more likely to maintain or grow their [dividends](/dividend/) and buybacks even in downturns. This idea is not new — value investors have always looked to cash generation — but applying it directly to bond selection is a narrower angle. Traditional bond funds weight companies by total debt outstanding (the biggest borrowers get the largest weightings) or by credit rating; MILK instead asks: which issuer has the widest moat, the most resilient business, and the fattest cash machine?

## How free cash flow selects for quality

Free cash flow is operating cash flow (the cash the business generates from its core operations) minus capital expenditures (the cash spent on equipment, facilities, and other upkeep). A company with $10 billion in annual operating cash flow and $2 billion in capital spending has $8 billion in free cash flow — the money left over to pay shareholders, pay down debt, or invest in growth. A company with $10 billion in operating cash flow and $9 billion in capital spending has only $1 billion in free cash flow, even if both show the same operating cash.

The premise underlying MILK is that companies with high free cash flow relative to their debt load are safer bets. They can miss a quarter and still cover interest. They can maintain distributions during weak years. They are less likely to cut their coupon or default. The fund screens the universe of investment-grade and high-yield corporate issuers, ranks them by free-cash-flow strength, and constructs a portfolio tilted heavily toward the top cash generators.

## The portfolio construction: tilt, not extreme

MILK does not hold only the cash-cow extreme — that would mean holding a tiny handful of mega-profitable companies and ignoring [diversification](/diversification/). Instead, it weights each holding by the issuer's free cash flow, so cash-rich companies get larger positions and cash-poor companies get smaller ones (or are excluded entirely). This tilt approach keeps the fund reasonably diversified across sectors and issuers while still favoring the financial quality the strategy targets.

The fund typically holds 100 to 200 corporate bonds across sectors: financials (banks and insurance), industrials (manufacturers and heavy equipment), utilities (which generate enormous cash), oil and gas (cyclical but cash-generative in good years), and consumer staples (like food and household goods). The tilt toward utilities and stable industrials is pronounced, because these sectors are defined by cash generation. The tilt away from high-burn-rate sectors like software and biotech is equally pronounced.

## Cash flow as a risk hedge

The free-cash-flow tilt carries an implicit bet: that in downturns, companies with strong cash generation will hold up better than the broad market. This played out during the 2020 pandemic shock when many corporate bonds fell sharply, but companies with fortress [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/) and free-cash-flow strength recovered quickly. It also held during the 2008 financial crisis, though less obviously, because credit stress was systemic and even strong companies saw cash flow pressured by [recession](/recession/).

The strategy is not foolproof. A company can have exceptional free cash flow in good times and see it evaporate in severe recession — energy companies experienced this in 2014 when oil crashed, erasing cash generation despite decades of prior strength. The tilt toward cash generation helps but does not eliminate default risk.

## Costs and yields

MILK's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is slightly higher than a passive corporate bond [index fund](/index-fund/), reflecting the cost of the quantitative free-cash-flow screening and the ongoing rebalancing as companies' cash generation changes. The fund's yield (the income paid monthly or quarterly) varies with [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) but is typically in the range of investment-grade corporate bonds — usually 4 to 6 percent depending on the rate environment.

## Interest-rate sensitivity and credit risk

MILK carries the same two primary risks as any corporate bond fund. First, [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/): when Treasury yields rise, existing bonds decline in value. The fund's duration (sensitivity to rate changes) is typically moderate, around 5 to 6 years, so a 1 percent increase in yields costs MILK about 5 to 6 percent of value. Second, [credit risk](/credit-risk/): if the economy deteriorates sharply, issuers may struggle to generate cash and may default. The free-cash-flow screen reduces this risk but does not eliminate it. In a severe downturn, even cash-rich companies face headwinds.

A third consideration is [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/): MILK's tilt toward utilities and stable industrials means it is more exposed to regulatory changes, interest-rate sensitivity (utilities suffer when rates rise sharply), and sector-specific shocks than a truly diversified corporate bond fund would be.

## Evolution and positioning

Since its launch, MILK has positioned itself as a differentiated bond fund for income investors who want to sleep at night — people who value cash-flow strength and stability over maximum yield. The fund gained attention during volatile periods (2018 when credit spreads widened, 2020 when the pandemic shock hit) because the free-cash-flow tilt did seem to offer some downside cushion. But in long [bull markets](/bull-market/) (such as 2017 or 2021), when credit spreads compress and investors can reach for yield without much risk, MILK often underperforms broader corporate bond funds because it stays away from the cheapest (and riskiest) borrowers.

## How to research MILK

Start with the fund's prospectus and current holdings, available on Pacer's website, to understand how the free-cash-flow ranking is calculated and which companies currently make up the portfolio. Compare MILK's returns and volatility to a plain investment-grade corporate bond index fund over multiple years, paying particular attention to downturns — does MILK indeed hold up better? Review the current sector weightings: are utilities and stable industrials overweight, and is there meaningful exposure to other sectors? Look at the [current yield](/current-yield/) and ask whether it compensates you for holding corporate credit risk, or whether [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) or a diversified bond fund offers better value. Finally, stress-test the thesis by considering a severe recession scenario: which companies in MILK's portfolio would see their free cash flow evaporate, and how much of the portfolio would they represent?
