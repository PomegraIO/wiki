*MMMA is an exchange-traded fund that holds a diversified portfolio of [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/) across multiple maturity ranges and issuer types, designed to deliver federally tax-exempt income while spreading risk across the full spectrum of the municipal [bond](/bond/) market.*

## The municipal bond spectrum

Municipal bonds come in many shapes, and they do not all behave the same way. A five-year bond issued by a stable city has almost nothing in common with a 30-year bond issued by a struggling municipality except that both are issued by local government entities and both are tax-exempt. The yield difference between them can be enormous — the long-term bond might pay 5% while the short-term bond pays 3%, a difference that reflects both the extra time the lender is taking on and the extra [credit risk](/credit-risk/).

MMMA attempts to capture the whole spectrum at once. Rather than specialising in one maturity bucket or one bond type, it holds a mix: some short-term bonds for stability and liquidity, some intermediate bonds for balanced yield, and some longer-term bonds for maximum income. It holds both general-obligation bonds (backed by the full taxing power of the issuer) and [revenue bonds](/revenue-bond/) (backed by specific revenue streams like toll roads or water fees). That [diversification](/diversification/) is the fund's central premise.

## The allocation across maturities

The fund's specific allocation to different maturity buckets is set by the fund's rules and adjusts over time based on market conditions and the managers' assessment of value. On a typical day, MMMA might hold:

- **Short-term bonds** (up to 3 years): These offer low yield but high stability and liquidity. If the fund needs to raise cash or bond values fall, the short-term position cushions the decline.
- **Intermediate bonds** (4–10 years): These represent the core of the fund and balance yield against duration risk. Most of the income comes from this bucket.
- **Long-term bonds** (10+ years): These offer the highest yields but are most sensitive to interest-rate changes. They amplify principal fluctuations when rates move.

The exact weights depend on the managers' view of the muni market and [current yield](/current-yield/) curves, but the idea is always to find the sweet spot between income and stability.

## General-obligation versus revenue bonds

A general-obligation bond is backed by the full faith and credit of the issuer — the city or state has committed its entire tax base to paying the bond. These bonds are generally safer because the issuer can raise taxes to pay them if needed. A revenue bond, by contrast, is backed by a specific revenue stream: tolls from a highway, fees from a water system, parking meter income. If that revenue stream dries up, the bond is at risk.

MMMA holds both types because they serve different purposes in a diversified portfolio. General-obligation bonds from stable jurisdictions offer safety; revenue bonds from well-managed toll roads or utilities offer higher yields. Combined, they create a risk-return profile that is neither too conservative nor too aggressive.

## The tax-exempt advantage

All municipal bonds held by MMMA are exempt from federal income tax, which is the principal reason to own them. For an investor in the 32% federal tax bracket or higher, a 4% tax-exempt muni yield is effectively worth more than a 6% taxable bond yield. That makes MMMA particularly valuable for taxable accounts — if you hold it in an IRA or 401(k), you lose the tax advantage entirely.

The fund distributes its income monthly, and that income is federally tax-exempt. However, depending on where you live and where the bonds are issued, you may still owe state or local tax. Some funds focus on bonds issued in a single state to maximize state-level tax exemption; MMMA is national, so it does not provide that benefit.

## Navigating the yield and duration tradeoff

MMMA's overall duration — its sensitivity to interest-rate changes — falls somewhere between a short-term bond fund and a long-term bond fund. In a rising-rate environment, the fund will lose value, but not as dramatically as a pure long-term muni fund would. In a falling-rate environment, it will appreciate, but not as much as a long-term fund.

That middle ground is a feature for some investors and a drawback for others. If you are convinced rates will fall, you might prefer a longer-duration muni fund to maximise gains. If you think rates will rise, a shorter-duration option might be better. If you are uncertain, MMMA's blend hedges the bet.

## Credit risk and diversification

MMMA mitigates credit risk through sheer breadth. It holds bonds from dozens of different municipalities across many states, so no single issuer's default meaningfully impairs the portfolio. The fund typically holds only [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/) — the safer tier — which further reduces the default risk.

However, municipal bonds are not risk-free. A severe [recession](/recession/) could impair the finances of multiple municipalities simultaneously, as happened during the 2008 financial crisis. Even investment-grade bonds can see ratings downgrades and principal declines. MMMA's diversification limits that risk but cannot eliminate it.

## Using MMMA in a portfolio

For a high-income investor in a taxable account who wants meaningful tax-exempt income and is willing to accept some principal fluctuation in exchange for a diversified muni portfolio, MMMA offers a simple, liquid vehicle. It is not for investors seeking perfect stability — hold [Treasury bills](/treasury-bill/) for that — but for those who want the tax advantage of municipals without concentrating in any one maturity or issuer type.
