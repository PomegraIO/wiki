*Most real estate finance companies either originate loans and sell them immediately, or hold a portfolio and depend on spreads between borrowing costs and loan yields. **MacKenzie Realty Capital (MKZR)** operates as a lender-and-holder—keeping its mortgages on the books to harvest the margin between what it costs to fund itself and the interest charged to developers and property owners. This asset-and-spread model is straightforward but sensitive to cost of capital and credit quality.*

## The Core Economics of Spread-Based Lending

MacKenzie's primary profit engine is the [gross-profit-margin](/gross-profit-margin/) between its funding cost and its loan yield. If the company borrows at 6 percent through debt and equity capital and lends at 8.5 percent, the 250-basis-point spread (before servicing costs) is where earnings come from. This model requires:

1. **Access to capital** at reasonable cost—achieved through debt issuance, [preferred-stock](/preferred-stock/) sales, or retained earnings.
2. **A portfolio of loans** that perform as expected—credit quality depends on underwriting discipline and favorable real estate conditions.
3. **Scale** sufficient that the fixed costs of underwriting, compliance, and servicing are absorbed across enough loans to generate acceptable returns on [equity](/common-stock/).

A 250-basis-point spread sounds thin, and it is. MacKenzie's profitability depends on keeping its cost of capital low and its default rates manageable.

## Where the Money Goes

Operating expenses consume a meaningful slice of the spread. Loan servicing (collecting payments, managing escrows, pursuing defaults) costs money. Underwriting new deals requires personnel and time. Compliance with banking regulations, even for a non-bank lender, is expensive. Default losses—when a borrower walks away or a foreclosure yields less than the loan balance—directly reduce earnings.

The company also faces mark-to-market exposure if any of its loans become trading securities or if it sells loans prior to maturity. In a rising-rate environment, the economic value of fixed-rate loans already held falls, even if no accounting loss is recognized immediately.

## Portfolio Quality and Interest-Rate Risk

MacKenzie's earnings stability hinges on two risks: **credit risk** (borrowers default) and **duration risk** (interest rates move against the portfolio's fixed-rate holdings). The company's 10-K filing discloses non-performing loans, loan-loss reserves, and the composition of the loan portfolio by property type and geography. Reading that filing reveals whether MacKenzie is concentrated in one real estate sector (office, retail, multifamily) or geographically diversified. A lender concentrated in downtown office buildings faces different headwinds than one funding multifamily or industrial warehouses.

Because MacKenzie is not systemically regulated like a bank, its [balance-sheet](/balance-sheet/) reserves for losses are discretionary. Some lenders run lean reserves, recognizing losses only as borrowers default; others build larger allowances proactively. This choice directly affects reported earnings but may reflect management's pessimism or optimism about portfolio quality.

## Origination vs. Holding vs. Selling

MacKenzie could theoretically shift its model by originating loans and selling them to other investors (capturing an origination fee but no ongoing spread), or by building a larger portfolio and leveraging it more heavily. Comparing the loan portfolio size to total assets and to equity shows the company's leverage decision. Higher leverage amplifies returns on equity if spreads stay positive, but it also magnifies losses if credit quality deteriorates.

## How Funding Costs Change the Playbook

When the cost of borrowing rises—through higher market interest rates or reduced access to debt capital—MacKenzie's economics compress immediately. If the company funded itself at 4 percent and lent at 7 percent, a five-year fixed mortgage held to maturity was profitable. But if refinancing at maturity requires funding at 6 percent against a 7 percent loan, the spread shrinks. This is why real estate lenders are sensitive to the shape and level of the yield curve. A steep curve (long rates much higher than short rates) is favorable; an inverted curve starves profitability.

## The Role of Economic Cycles

MacKenzie's performance is cyclical. In expansions, real estate values rise, borrowers can refinance or prepay without loss, and defaults are rare. Spreads may narrow as competition heats up, but volumes rise and losses stay low. In downturns, property values fall, borrowers face stress, defaults rise, and the company must charge larger loss allowances. Additionally, borrowers will prepay loans if refinancing becomes cheap, forcing MacKenzie to redeploy capital at lower yields—a problem if rates have fallen and new loans offer lower spreads than old ones.

Long-term earnings stability depends on maintaining underwriting discipline through cycles and accepting lower volumes in booms rather than chasing marginal credits that blow up in the next recession.
