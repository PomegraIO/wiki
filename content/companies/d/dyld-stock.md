*The **LeaderShares Dynamic Yield ETF** (ticker **DYLD**) is built around a single promise: capture income from US stocks that are likely to pay, and grow, their [dividends](/dividend/). Rather than holding a fixed list of dividend payers, DYLD rebalances monthly using a proprietary model that scores stocks on factors like [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/), dividend growth history, and [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/). The result is a constantly shifting portfolio meant to chase yield in a way that adapts to market conditions, quarter by quarter.*

## The philosophy: hunting for rising dividends

[Dividend investing](/dividend-investing/) has many flavors. Some funds chase the highest yield (the biggest payout relative to stock price), which can be risky because unsustainably high yields often signal trouble ahead. Other funds hold blue-chip dividend aristocrats—companies with decades of rising dividends—but these tend to be concentrated in utilities and consumer staples, lacking growth. DYLD stakes a middle ground: it hunts for stocks that are likely to raise their dividends, combining yield with the prospect of growth.

The "dynamic yield" in the name refers to the fund's adaptive rebalancing schedule. Once per month, LeaderShares runs its selection algorithm across the US equity universe, scores stocks on dividend metrics, and adjusts holdings. This is more frequent than the quarterly or annual rebalancing typical of many dividend funds, which makes DYLD responsive to changing fundamentals and market conditions.

## How the scoring model works

LeaderShares has not disclosed every detail of its algorithm—proprietary methods are a source of competitive advantage—but the [fund prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) outlines the key criteria. The model examines dividend yield, the growth rate of dividends over recent years, the free cash flow available to sustain or grow dividends, and the balance-sheet strength of the issuer. Stocks scoring high on these metrics are eligible; those that score poorly are excluded. The fund then weights holdings based on the scores, giving more portfolio weight to stocks with higher expected dividend growth.

In essence, the model is trying to predict which companies will raise their dividends in the coming months and years. It is not always right—a company can cut its dividend due to a downturn, a management change, or a strategic shift—but the systematic approach lets DYLD adapt faster than a static dividend index would.

## The composition and sector bias

Because dividends are more common in mature, profitable sectors than in growth-oriented ones, DYLD typically holds a meaningful allocation to financials, utilities, consumer staples, real estate (REITs), energy, and telecommunications. Technology and discretionary sectors are usually smaller. The exact sector mix shifts with the algorithm, as high-dividend stocks in tech (which are rare) or defensive names in utilities (abundant) gain or lose favor.

The fund usually holds 50 to 100 stocks, a moderate portfolio size. The overlap with other [dividend-focused ETFs](/dividend-focused-etf/) is significant—the best dividend stocks tend to appear across many dividend funds—but DYLD's monthly rebalancing and growth-focused screening give it a distinct tilt compared to pure yield-chasing competitors.

## Monthly rebalancing and transaction costs

The monthly rebalancing schedule is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it allows the fund to respond quickly when a company's dividend trajectory changes. On the other hand, rebalancing costs money. The fund incurs trading costs ([bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/), commissions) and generates taxable capital gains that flow to shareholders. For a buy-and-hold investor in a taxable account, these frequent turnover events are a drawback. In a tax-advantaged account (IRA, 401k), they matter less.

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) captures the costs of running the algorithm and trading, and it is moderate for an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/)—higher than a passive dividend-index fund but reasonable for the monthly strategy.

## Income sustainability and reinvestment

DYLD's dividend payments are not guaranteed, and they fluctuate. In a strong earnings environment, many of the fund's holdings will raise dividends, which lifts DYLD's payout. In a [recession](/recession/), dividends often get cut, and DYLD's yield falls. This is true of all dividend funds, but it is particularly relevant to DYLD because its focus on dividend growth means it is exposed to the cyclicality of earnings.

Most income-focused investors treat dividends as reinvestment capital, buying more shares with the payout. DYLD's monthly distributions make this straightforward in a brokerage account set to automatically reinvest dividends (DRIP). The compounding effect of reinvestment is part of the long-term return story.

## Comparison to alternatives

A passive dividend-index fund (such as one tracking the S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats) is simpler and cheaper, but it holds only companies with 25+ years of rising dividends—a stricter, more backward-looking filter. A [high-yield bond fund](/high-yield-bond-fund/) captures income from debt instead of equity, introducing [credit risk](/credit-risk/). A diversified dividend-focused active fund with quarterly or annual rebalancing (such as those offered by some traditional asset managers) may be more tax-efficient but slower to adapt to changing fundamentals.

DYLD sits in a specific niche: investors who want dividend income with growth potential, prefer frequent rebalancing to catch momentum shifts, and are willing to tolerate the transaction costs of monthly turnover.

## Performance and dividend track record

DYLD's track record shows periods when the dividend-growth tilt worked beautifully (when growth faltered and quality dividend stocks outperformed) and periods when it lagged (when high-growth tech dominated). The fund's performance is highly dependent on the earnings cycle and market leadership. In a rising-rate environment, dividend stocks often suffer because yields on [bonds](/bond/) become more competitive; in a falling-rate environment, dividends become more attractive. DYLD cannot predict these shifts, only react to them.

The dividend payment history is public and can be found on LeaderShares' website. It has been substantial in some periods and modest in others, reflecting the underlying business conditions of its holdings.

## Risks and considerations

The primary risk is that the algorithm's selection rules break down. If dividend growth is not as predictable as the model assumes, or if the scoring criteria fall out of favor, DYLD's outperformance will evaporate. There is also the risk that a major holding cuts or eliminates its dividend—a sudden shock that affects both the return and the income distribution.

A second risk is sector concentration. Because dividends cluster in defensive and cyclical sectors, DYLD will always be tilted away from growth. In a technology-driven [bull market](/bull-market/), this tilting will be a drag on returns. The trade-off is explicit: you accept growth drag in exchange for income and potential dividend growth.

Currency and [market timing](/market-timing/) are not concerns (DYLD is all-US equities), but [inflation](/inflation/) is. If inflation erodes purchasing power, a dividend stream that does not keep pace becomes less valuable. The fund's reliance on dividend growth helps with this—raising dividends is one way companies fight inflation—but it is not a perfect hedge.

## Who it is for

DYLD suits an investor who: (a) wants current income from stocks, (b) believes dividend growth is a meaningful return driver, (c) can tolerate monthly distributions and the tax consequences of frequent rebalancing (in taxable accounts), and (d) is comfortable with a defensive, income-tilted portfolio that will lag in growth-driven bull markets. It is particularly relevant for retirees or near-retirees seeking sustainable cash flow from equities, and for those using a core-plus income strategy where DYLD forms the income engine.

It is less suitable for growth-focused investors, those in early-career accumulation phases, or anyone seeking maximum tax efficiency or the lowest possible fees.

## How to research it

Visit LeaderShares' website to review the fund's factsheet, prospectus, and historical dividend-payment history. Compare DYLD's yields and total returns to competing dividend-focused products. Examine the current holdings to see the sector tilts in action. Run your own dividend forecast: if dividend growth slows, would the yield still be attractive relative to alternatives (bonds, other equity funds, cash)? Finally, understand your own tax situation. In a taxable account, the monthly rebalancing and distributions will create frequent tax events; in a retirement account, they do not matter.
