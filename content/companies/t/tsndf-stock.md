## Entry and early positioning

TerrAscend emerged during the era when cannabis legalization began to transition from prohibition to regulated commerce. The company positioned itself to participate in the adult-use and medical cannabis markets as jurisdictions moved to legalize or decriminalize the plant. The regulatory landscape for cannabis is fragmented — federal prohibition still obtains in the United States (though enforcement priorities have shifted), while a growing number of states have legalized cannabis for medical and adult use. Canada fully legalized cannabis for adult use in 2018, creating a different opportunity set north of the border. TerrAscend's initial strategy involved building operations across this patchwork of markets, capitalizing on the opportunity to establish supply chains, retail footprints, and brand recognition as the market opened up.

The cannabis industry differs from most other consumer goods precisely because of [regulation. A](/regulation-a/) cannabis retailer cannot simply open a store; it must operate within the framework of state or provincial rules about who can grow, who can process, who can sell, and in many cases, where. Many jurisdictions limit the number of licenses issued, making a license itself a valuable asset. TerrAscend's competitive position depended not just on operational skill but on securing licenses and navigating the regulatory approval process in each jurisdiction where it wanted to operate. That approval process is often slow, costly, and political.

## Growth through licensing and market expansion

As cannabis regulations evolved and more markets opened, TerrAscend expanded its footprint. The company pursued a strategy of acquiring or obtaining licenses to grow, process, and retail cannabis in multiple jurisdictions. Cultivation facilities vary significantly by location: some are indoor operations using sophisticated lighting and climate control; others are outdoor or greenhouse grows leveraging natural light and weather. Indoor grows demand more capital and energy but allow year-round production and quality control. Outdoor grows are cheaper to operate but are seasonal and weather-dependent.

TerrAscend's cultivation operations supply its internal processing and retail divisions, and may also supply wholesale to other retailers. Processing involves transforming raw flower into concentrates, edibles, vape cartridges, and other product forms — the higher-margin segments of the cannabis market. Retail involves selling directly to consumers through branded storefronts or licensed dispensaries, depending on the jurisdiction's rules. Each layer of the value chain — cultivation, processing, retail — carries different margins and risks.

The geographic strategy matters because regulation is local. A license to grow in one state is worthless in another. Opportunities differ by jurisdiction: some have issued many retail licenses, saturating the market; others have issued few, making retail access scarce. Wholesale prices for cannabis flower and products vary dramatically by market — a pound of cannabis that might fetch $800 in an oversupplied market might fetch $2,000 in an undersupplied one. TerrAscend's ability to identify and enter high-margin markets, and to operate efficiently in commodity markets, determined much of its financial performance.

## The Canadian and U.S. market context

Canada's legalization of cannabis in 2018 opened a large market with a unified regulatory framework. The Canadian cannabis market moved quickly from launch, establishing supply chains and retail distribution across the country. TerrAscend's Canadian operations benefited from early positioning and scale. However, the Canadian market became increasingly competitive, with large multistate operators, provincial retailers, and craft producers all competing on price and product quality. Pricing in Canada compressed as supply ramped up, putting pressure on margins.

The U.S. market is more fragmented because cannabis remains federally illegal. States that have legalized cannabis regulate it independently, creating dozens of separate markets with different rules, tax structures, and competitive conditions. This fragmentation creates opportunity for operators who can navigate multiple state rules and manage operations across different regulatory regimes, but it also creates complexity and risk. A change in any state's enforcement priorities or a shift in political winds can disrupt a market where a cannabis company has invested. Additionally, federal prohibition creates banking barriers: many banks will not work with cannabis businesses, forcing them toward cash operations or non-traditional banking, which increases fraud risk and operational complexity.

## The supply-demand dynamic

Cannabis demand in newly legal markets typically starts with an initial surge as users who were previously buying on illegal markets switch to legal sources. Over time, that initial boom moderates as the addressable market reaches saturation and growth depends on expanding the overall market or gaining share from competitors. Pricing dynamics shift as markets mature: in newly opened markets, limited supply can support high margins; in mature, competitive markets, supply increases and prices decline sharply.

TerrAscend's profitability depended on managing this cycle well. That meant maintaining cost advantages in commodity cultivation (growing flower cheaply in bulk), capturing margin in higher-value processing (turning bulk flower into edibles and concentrates), and securing scarce retail licenses in markets where retail access was controlled. It also meant managing the time-lag between investing in cultivation facilities and realizing revenue — growing and selling cannabis takes months, so cash flow timing is critical.

## Brand and product positioning

In a newly legal market, brand recognition and product quality matter. Some cannabis consumers have preferences for specific brands or product types — some prefer flower, others edibles or vape products; some prioritize potency, others seek specific cannabinoid profiles or flavor profiles. Retailers often feature prominent brands. TerrAscend's brands, whatever their names and positioning, competed for shelf space and consumer attention. The company's ability to develop recognizable brands and to differentiate its products (on quality, variety, or price) determined its competitive strength at retail.

The company also faced the reality that cannabis is rapidly becoming a commodity. In mature markets, price competition is fierce and brand differentiation is limited. Many consumers view cannabis as cannabis and shop primarily on price and convenience. In that commodity environment, margins are thin and scale matters.

## Challenges and regulatory uncertainty

TerrAscend faced several headwinds as the cannabis market matured. First, federal illegality in the United States created banking and compliance challenges that competitors in other sectors do not face. The company faced constraints on banking, difficulty raising capital, and tax treatment (federal tax code Section 280E) that prevents deductions of ordinary business expenses for trafficking in controlled substances. This tax treatment significantly erodes profitability compared to legal businesses.

Second, oversupply emerged in mature markets. Canada legalized too much production capacity, and many U.S. states followed suit, creating commodity-like pricing pressures. TerrAscend had to constantly adjust its cost structure and portfolio strategy to stay profitable as prices compressed.

Third, the regulatory environment remained unpredictable. A change in a state governor or an enforcement crackdown can disrupt a state market. Federal policy shifts could move cannabis further toward legalization or could reverse recent trend toward enforcement leniency, creating political risk.

Fourth, the competitive landscape included both specialized cannabis operators and large alcohol and tobacco companies positioned to move into cannabis if federal prohibition lifted. TerrAscend competed for market share against other cannabis operators and faced the looming threat of much larger corporations entering the space if regulations permitted.
