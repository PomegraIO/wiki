The iShares 3-Month [Treasury Bill](/treasury-bill/) ETF (TBIL) is an exchange-traded fund managed by [BlackRock](/blk-stock/) that holds a continuously rolling portfolio of United States Treasury Bills maturing in approximately 90 days or less. TBIL is not a vehicle for picking winners among Treasury securities or timing the Treasury market. Instead, it is a passive, transparent way to own the shortest-term segment of US government debt — the portion that finances federal day-to-day needs and carries zero default risk. For an investor treating TBIL as a liquidity reserve or a cash substitute, the fund operates almost like a bank account denominated in Treasury bills: money flows in, the fund holds the bills to near-maturity, and principal returns on schedule.

## How Treasury bills work

When the US government needs short-term cash—for payroll, operations, or refinancing—it issues Treasury bills. These are zero-coupon securities: the Treasury announces a maturity date (13 weeks, 26 weeks, 52 weeks out), investors bid at an auction, and the government accepts bids at the lowest price (highest implied yield). The investor buys at a discount and receives full face value at maturity. That difference between purchase price and face value is the investor's yield—achieved purely through the mathematics of discount and maturation, not through [coupon payments](/coupon-payment/).

A three-month bill is the shortest major Treasury issuance. The government auctions new 13-week bills weekly, so TBIL's portfolio continuously turns over as maturing bills exit the fund and newly issued bills enter. This rolling mechanism is the heart of why TBIL functions as a liquid cash tool: bills always mature very soon, and the fund reinvests the proceeds immediately in fresh issuance. There is no bond-price volatility from interest-rate swings, because the time to maturity is so short that rate changes have a vanishingly small impact on price.

## The fund's index and tracking approach

TBIL tracks the Bloomberg 3-Month Treasury Bill Index, a rules-based benchmark that includes all outstanding US Treasury bills with remaining maturity between 1 and 90 days. Because the government issues bills continuously on a weekly schedule, the index universe is constantly refreshed. The index is weighted by market value—larger-denomination bills or those with more value outstanding are held in larger proportion. The fund itself holds the indexed bills in near-proportional amounts, with minimal active discretion. When bills mature, redemption proceeds are reinvested to maintain the target portfolio structure.

Tracking is mechanical and free of timing bets. TBIL does not attempt to favor bills that will perform well or avoid bills that will underperform—a meaningless distinction anyway, since all bills repay at face value on a known date. The fund simply mirrors the index, minimizing tracking error through scale and low turnover costs.

## Returns, yields, and interest-rate sensitivity

TBIL's return in any period comes from the discount earned as bills accrue to maturity plus any change in the price of bills held during the period. Because bills are so short-dated, price movement from interest-rate changes is minimal. A 1% move in [interest rates](/interest-rate/) moves a three-month bill's price by roughly 0.25%, whereas the same move shifts a 10-year [bond's](/bond/) price by several percent. This insensitivity to rate swings makes TBIL extraordinarily stable—principal loss is not a practical concern for any [holding period](/holding-period/), even if interest rates surge during ownership.

The yield available to TBIL investors is set by the [Federal Reserve's](/federal-reserve/) near-term policy rate and the market's near-term expectations. When the Fed has tightened and holds rates high, three-month bills yield more, and TBIL's distribution to shareholders is larger. When the Fed is in an easing cycle and rates fall, bill yields fall in lockstep, and TBIL's yield falls. The fund has no control over this; it is simply a mirror of Treasury-bill market rates. For an investor seeking a high absolute yield, TBIL is only attractive in a high-rate environment—a limitation that is unavoidable, not a fund-management failing.

## Fee structure and the cost advantage

TBIL's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is among the lowest in all of ETF markets, measured in basis points (hundredths of a percent). Because the strategy requires no security research, no [market timing](/market-timing/), and no turnover beyond the mechanical rolling of maturing bills, costs are trivial. This is a significant advantage over traditional bank money-market funds, which charge higher fees and offer less transparency. For an investor choosing between TBIL and a bank money-market account, the ETF typically wins on yield after fees.

## Who owns TBIL and for what purpose

TBIL serves several constituencies. Individual investors use it as a holding tank for cash awaiting deployment elsewhere—a retiree managing a bucket of near-term spending money, or a trader holding proceeds before reinvesting. Large institutional investors (pension funds, endowments, corporates) use TBIL for liquidity and operational reserves—capital that must be safe, accessible, and earning a modest yield. Some investors treat TBIL as a bond-portfolio ballast, replacing the cash position of a traditional savings account. The fund's scale and tight [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) also make it liquid enough for tactical trading, though that is not its primary use case.

## Default risk and inflation risk

TBIL carries effectively zero credit or default risk. The full faith and credit of the United States government backs every bill in the portfolio. Bankruptcy, business failure, and insolvency are not realistic scenarios. This is the security dimension of Treasury bills—investors sacrifice yield for certainty.

The real risk in TBIL is [inflation](/inflation/). If inflation rises faster than the yield TBIL is collecting, the investor's real purchasing power shrinks even though the dollar amount is protected. A three-month bill held in an inflationary environment may repay full principal but deliver a negative real return (purchasing power declines). This is unavoidable for any short-term fixed-income fund and is not specific to TBIL. It is why TBIL is not a wealth-preservation vehicle for long holding periods—it is a temporary parking place for cash that will be deployed or spent in the near term.

## Researching and monitoring TBIL

The fund's prospectus and fact sheet (available via BlackRock's iShares website) detail the fund's methodology, current holdings, and expense ratio. The Bloomberg 3-Month Treasury Bill Index is published daily; monitoring the index composition shows which bills are in the portfolio, their maturity dates, and their value. The current three-month Treasury yield is published weekly by the US Treasury. In normal conditions, TBIL's yield should be very close to that Treasury yield, minus the fund's small expense ratio. Any sustained large gap between TBIL's yield and the raw three-month Treasury rate suggests either a data error or an operational issue worth investigating.

TBIL trades on [NYSE Arca](/nyse-arca/) with deep liquidity and minimal spreads. The fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) and market price are reported continuously during market hours. For most investors, the amount of ongoing research required is minimal—TBIL is operationally transparent, holdings change automatically through the rolling mechanism, and the risk profile is static. The main monitoring task is confirming that the yield remains aligned with three-month Treasury rates.
