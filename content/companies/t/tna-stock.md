*The **Direxion Small Cap Bull 3X ETF** ([NASDAQ](/nasdaq/): TNA) tracks small-cap stock performance with 3X daily leverage — meaning it aims to gain 3% for every 1% gain in the underlying [Russell 2000 Index](/russell-2000/). It is a trading vehicle, not a holding-period investment. Buyers should expect to exit within days or weeks, not months or years.*

## What it holds and what the leverage mechanism does

TNA holds or synthetically gains exposure to the smallest 2,000 publicly traded U.S. companies by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) (the Russell 2000 Index). These are companies typically below five billion dollars in market cap — regional banks, specialty retailers, niche manufacturers, startups on the verge of breaking into mid-cap territory. The small-cap universe is more volatile and more economically sensitive than large-cap stocks, which already makes it a higher-octane bet. TNA amplifies that volatility 3X through daily leverage.

The mechanics are critical. Each day, Direxion rebalances the fund to maintain exactly 3X leverage. This is not a simple buy-and-hold multiplier. It compounds daily. On a day when the Russell 2000 rises 1%, TNA should rise roughly 3%. The next day, leverage resets. The month after, leverage resets again. This daily reset means the fund's longer-term returns do not simply track 3X the index's longer-term return — especially if the index is volatile.

## When this works

TNA shines during strong bull runs. In a month when small-caps rise 10%, TNA might rise 25–35%, depending on daily volatility. During a week of positive data, risk-on sentiment, and momentum buying, TNA can deliver outsized gains that reward traders who got the timing right. Professional traders or tactical hedge-fund managers use TNA to express high conviction during windows when small-caps are clearly outperforming and momentum is building.

In these windows, the leverage is a feature: it lets a trader put smaller capital at risk and achieve larger notional exposure. Alternatively, it lets a trader put a modest position size into TNA and free up capital for other trades or hedges. The 3X mechanism works exactly as intended when the underlying index is rising cleanly with low volatility.

## The hidden cost: volatility decay

The trap is longer [holding periods](/holding-period/) and choppy markets. Imagine small-cap stocks swing up 5% one week and down 5% the next. Over two weeks, the index is flat. What happened to TNA? The first week it rose roughly 15%. The second week it fell roughly 15%, but that 15% loss applies to a higher base (the new TNA price after week one). The result is that TNA ends below where it started — even though the underlying index is exactly where it started. This is volatility decay, and it is baked into any leveraged product in a choppy market.

The severity of the decay depends on the underlying asset's realized volatility. Small-caps are already volatile. When you add 3X leverage and daily rebalancing, volatility decay can be brutal. A trader who bought TNA expecting small-caps to trend upward but found them instead bouncing sideways has watched the fund's value leak away through pure mathematics, not directional loss.

The cost is highest during uncertain environments — when [central banks](/central-bank/) are signaling but not yet acting, or when economic data is genuinely mixed. These are exactly the environments where traders are often most tempted to use leveraged products, because conviction is high but direction is ambiguous.

## Costs and trading friction

Direxion's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is reasonable by leveraged-fund standards, but it is not the fund's biggest cost. The real costs are the borrowing rate (the cost of leverage itself), the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) when trading, and the volatility decay mentioned above. A trader paying a half-cent spread to enter and another half-cent to exit, combined with the overnight borrowing rate, can see transaction costs and decay eat a percentage point or more of returns before the underlying trade even plays out. These frictions matter more in TNA than in a plain [index fund](/index-fund/).

## What kind of trader should own this, and for how long?

TNA is for traders with a specific, time-bounded bullish thesis on small-caps. Maybe the Fed has just signaled a rate-cut cycle starting, and small-caps historically outperform in falling-rate environments. A trader confident in that thesis might hold TNA for two to four weeks, expecting small-cap outperformance and watching daily to exit if that thesis breaks. This is a reasonable use.

What is not reasonable is buying TNA and forgetting about it for a year, expecting to ride it to the moon. Small-cap [bull markets](/bull-market/) do not compound in straight lines. They oscillate. Volatility decay will grind away value, and the trader who had the right direction but the wrong patience will end up with less than they bargained for.

## Research and reality-checking

Before buying TNA, run the numbers. If you are betting small-caps will rise 15% over the next three months, TNA might deliver 30–45% in that ideal scenario — but that assumes steady upward motion with minimal pullbacks. In a more realistic scenario where small-caps make that 15% gain with typical weekly volatility, TNA might deliver 20–25%. The gap between the ideal and the realistic is volatility decay.

Check Direxion's prospectus and fact sheet. Download the fund's past three-month, six-month, and one-year returns and compare them to the Russell 2000 Index's returns for the same periods. You will see that TNA's long-term returns have not been 3X the index's long-term returns because of volatility drag. That is not a failure — it is a feature of daily-reset leverage.

Finally, use proper [position sizing](/position-sizing/). A trader buying 10% of portfolio capital in a 3X small-cap leveraged fund is taking risks equivalent to 30% in unlevered small-caps. Some traders are comfortable with that. Most should not be. TNA is a tactical tool. Treat it as such.
