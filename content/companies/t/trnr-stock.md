Interactive Strength assembles a portfolio of premium fitness-hardware companies: Wattbike (stationary bikes with performance analytics), Ergatta (gamified rowing machines), CLMBR (vertical climbers), and FORME (smart mirrors and cable-resistance systems). The business model is two-legged: sell physical equipment to home users and commercial gyms, then earn recurring revenue from digital memberships — classes, coaching, and app content. Equipment sales are lump-sum transactions sensitive to upswings in consumer wellness spending and confidence; membership revenues are recurring but require a large installed base of active, paying subscribers. The company recently pivoted from organic growth to acquisition-driven expansion, buying established brands rather than building new ones. This is a portfolio play on the boom-and-bust cycle of home fitness spending.

## The boom-bust rhythms of home fitness

Connected-fitness hardware spending exhibits pronounced cyclical behavior. During pandemic lockdowns and recovery booms, consumers funneled capital into home gyms, viewing fitness equipment as durable goods in the wellness category. Bike and rower sales exploded, membership churn fell, and companies like Peloton and Beachbody soared. That cycle has reversed: as gyms reopened and novelty wore off, equipment sales plateaued and subscription cancellations accelerated. Interactive Strength's strategy — acquiring established brands with installed bases rather than building products from scratch — reflects this learned lesson. Existing users have switching costs (invested in that ecosystem, their data, their routines) and existing membership revenue provides a floor. Still, the company inherits the churn risk: if subscribers leave when economy contracts, recurring revenue does not sustain through downturns.

The pattern matters because home fitness is not a recession-proof category. When unemployment rises or wage growth stalls, consumers defer purchases of premium fitness equipment and cancel memberships to protect cash. A buyer with a Wattbike or Ergatta in the living room still has to decide monthly whether to renew; a [recession](/recession/) strengthens that cancellation impulse. This is why large equipment makers prioritize retention, new content, and engagement — keeping churn low is the sole lever to stabilize recurring revenue in downturns. Wattbike's positioning as a professional and athlete device offers some protection, since elite training budgets are less price-sensitive. FORME, CLMBR, and Ergatta, which target mainstream home users, face greater churn risk in contractions.

## Wattbike: the accuracy flagship

Wattbike is the company's crown jewel, known for biomechanical precision and elite athlete endorsement. It pairs a stationary bike with real-time power and cadence measurement, marketing itself to serious cyclists, teams, and sports scientists. This premium positioning means higher price-per-unit but narrower addressable market. Wattbike's revenue is less consumer-discretionary than, say, a Peloton, because serious cyclists and professional teams buy on performance data rather than aspirational marketing. Yet a recession still slows upgrades and new purchases.

## Ergatta, CLMBR, and FORME: widening the portfolio

Ergatta brings gamified rowing (players compete in virtual races against on-screen opponents) to the home. CLMBR is a vertical climbing machine targeting full-body conditioning. FORME offers a smart mirror with on-demand classes and cable-based resistance (similar to Lululemon's Mirror product). These brands serve different workout preferences and price points, spreading risk across equipment categories. None has Wattbike's prestige, but each has enthusiast communities. Ergatta, in particular, has carved out a niche in rowing because it combines a strong aesthetic design with social-competition features.

## Revenue guidance and financial headwinds

Interactive Strength has guided for more than thirty million dollars in pro forma group revenue for 2026, driven by a full year of Wattbike and Ergatta following those [acquisitions](/acquisition/). That revenue target assumes sustained equipment sales and stable membership bases. However, the company faces material financial headwinds: it carries significant debt, reports ongoing operating losses, and has issued going-concern warnings in recent filings. This is typical for young roll-ups scaling through debt-financed M&A. The risk is that membership churn accelerates or hardware sales disappoint, compressing margins before the company reaches profitability. The company's ability to service debt depends on hitting revenue and churn assumptions.

## Manufacturing and supply-chain flexibility

Interactive Strength outsources hardware production to third-party manufacturers, allowing the company to avoid fixed capital commitments and shift production geographically if needed. This is an asset-light model common in hardware roll-ups. The downside is reduced control over quality, lead times, and cost — particularly if supply chains seize up or component costs spike.

## Tracking Interactive Strength as a cyclical equity

Watch quarterly membership churn and the net number of active subscribers across all brands. Churn accelerates in recessions and during product-quality issues. Monitor equipment sales by brand to understand which portfolio companies are gaining or losing market share. The company's quarterly earnings calls should discuss pricing power (whether membership prices are sticking), subscriber-acquisition costs (trending up is a red flag), and debt metrics (the company must refinance or reduce leverage). Because Interactive Strength is debt-funded and growth-dependent, it is sensitive to [interest rates](/interest-rate/): higher rates increase financing costs and slow consumer borrowing for discretionary purchases. 

One specific metric to track is the proportion of revenue from equipment sales versus membership recurring revenue. A high equipment-sales ratio indicates cyclical exposure; a high recurring-revenue ratio reduces volatility but also requires large installed bases to support. The ten-K (SEC CIK 0001785056) outlines all risk factors; watch especially for gross-margin compression, any dilution of brand positioning through acquisitions, and warnings about customer concentration or large-customer churn. Given the company's debt load and growth ambitions, management commentary on refinancing plans and debt covenants is also critical.
