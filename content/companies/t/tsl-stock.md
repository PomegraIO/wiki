**Key facts:**
- **Fund type:** Daily leveraged long ETF issued by GraniteShares
- **Underlying:** Tesla Inc. stock (TSLA)
- **Leverage:** 1.25x daily reset
- **Mechanism:** Rebalances daily to deliver 1.25x of Tesla's next-day movement
- **Best for:** Short-term tactical positions measured in days or weeks
- **Main risk:** Volatility decay erodes returns in choppy markets or over longer holding periods

TSL is a leveraged fund designed for traders, not buy-and-hold investors. It aims to deliver 1.25 times the daily return of Tesla stock. If Tesla gains 4% in a single trading day, TSL should gain approximately 5%. If Tesla falls 4%, TSL should fall 5%. This 1.25x leverage is modest compared to more aggressive 2x or 3x leveraged ETFs, suggesting it is intended to be held for periods of hours to weeks by investors or portfolio managers seeking tactical amplification of Tesla exposure.

The daily reset mechanism is central to understanding how TSL actually behaves. Each trading day, the fund rebalances its holdings—usually through equity index futures, swaps, or options contracts—so that it holds the exact leverage needed to deliver 1.25x of the next day's Tesla price movement. This is not a one-time loan that compounds over time. Instead, the fund restarts each day from a fresh starting point. Over a single trading day, this mechanism works as advertised. Over weeks and months in a volatile market, the daily resets create a drag on returns relative to a theoretical 1.25x simple multiplication of total return. This drag is volatility decay.

Consider a concrete example. Suppose Tesla stock starts at $100 and over two weeks rises 10% to $110—a 10% total return. A 1.25x leveraged position should theoretically deliver 12.5% ($2.50 gain on a $20 starting position equivalent). But if Tesla bounces up and down along the way—rises 3%, falls 1%, rises 2%, falls 0.5%, and so forth—the daily resets mean TSL locks in losses on down days (when it falls 1.25% instead of 1%) and only partially captures gains on up days (when it rises 1.25% instead of 1.5%, but the pool of capital to apply 1.25x to is smaller due to the previous day's loss). The compounding of these daily resets across volatile price action typically results in TSL delivering less than 1.25x of the total return, sometimes significantly less.

This is not a flaw or a market failure—it is simple mathematics. Any leveraged product with daily reset will see its cumulative return diverge from the leverage ratio applied to the cumulative underlying return. The further apart the leverage ratio is from 1.0x, the faster and larger the decay in choppy markets. TSL's 1.25x is relatively conservative, so the decay is slower than 2x or 3x products would experience, but it is real and measurable.

Who should own TSL? Primarily, portfolio managers and traders executing a specific tactical view. An investor who believes Tesla will rise 8% to 12% within the next two to three weeks might use TSL to amplify the bet. A hedge fund using TSL as part of a broader long-short portfolio might hold a position for days, then exit. An investor who buys TSL expecting to hold it for a year to capture Tesla's long-term growth is making a mistake; they would be better served by owning Tesla stock directly or using a non-leveraged fund.

The fund's expense ratio should be reviewed in its prospectus and fact sheet. For leveraged products, the ongoing cost of daily rebalancing is embedded in performance; the stated annual fee typically ranges from 0.50% to 0.95%, though TSL's specific rate should be verified. Beyond the stated fee, the real cost is opportunity loss through volatility decay. An investor holding TSL through extended periods of choppy trading will almost certainly find that the actual return underperforms the theoretical 1.25x calculation.

Research TSL through its prospectus and fact sheet, paying close attention to the daily reset mechanism and the limitations section. Compare TSL's actual historical returns against 1.25x the returns of Tesla stock over various time periods—one week, two weeks, one month—to observe decay in action. Understand the fund's role in your portfolio: if you are using it as a tactical amplifier for a near-term view, it can serve that purpose. If you are treating it as a long-term holding, you are likely better served by other instruments.
