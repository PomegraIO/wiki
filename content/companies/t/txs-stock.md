> *A single index fund can turn a state into a portfolio.*

TXS is a passively managed ETF that tracks a custom index of large and mid-cap publicly traded companies headquartered in Texas. It is a regional concentration play — a way for an investor to take a deliberate bet on Texas's economy and the companies that have chosen to base themselves there. The fund includes well-known names in energy, technology, finance, and consumer goods, alongside smaller industrial and service companies.

## The Texas economic footprint

Texas is the second-largest state economy in the United States, with a workforce and tax environment that have attracted major corporate headquarters. The publicly traded companies domiciled in Texas span several dominant industries: energy (oil majors and suppliers), technology (microprocessor designers, software firms), financial services (regional and national banks), consumer goods, industrial manufacturing, and real estate. That diversity is one appeal of TXS — it is not a bet on a single sector, but rather on whether Texas's regional economy will continue to grow and companies based there will prosper.

Energy is the single largest weight in the index, reflecting both the historical oil-industry roots in Texas and the ongoing significance of oil majors and energy-infrastructure companies that have chosen to keep headquarters in the state. Technology is the second-largest, anchored by companies in semiconductors, software, and data processing. Finance, consumer goods, industrial goods, and services round out the portfolio. The exact composition shifts with market-cap movements, but the regional character remains constant.

## How TXS differs from a broad market index

A standard large-cap [equity ETF](/equity-etf/) like one tracking the S&P 500 owns thousands of companies across all fifty states and many countries. TXS is far more concentrated — it holds the Texas subset, perhaps 50–150 companies depending on the index definition and market-cap criteria. That concentration means TXS will outperform a broad index if Texas companies and the Texas economy do better than the national average, and it will underperform if they lag.

The historical record suggests Texas companies have kept pace with the broader market over most decades, but periods arise when regional differences widen. An energy-sector boom (rising oil prices) lifts TXS disproportionately. A slump in energy or a shift in investment away from Texas-based tech or finance can drag it down. The fund is genuinely leveraged to the fortunes of a single state in a way that broad [diversification](/diversification/) is not.

## Costs and trading

The fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically 0.45–0.60 per cent per year, slightly higher than a broad [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) fund but in line with other specialty regional or sector indices. The cost reflects the work of maintaining a custom index and the reduced scale compared to multi-trillion-dollar broad-market ETFs. Liquidity is modest compared to giant funds but generally sufficient for retail traders; spreads are a few cents per share on a typical day.

[Dividends](/dividend/) from the holdings are reinvested or paid out depending on the [share class](/share-class/). Because the portfolio includes significant energy and finance weightings, [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) is often slightly higher than a pure-tech index but in line with broad market exposure.

## The risks of concentration and sector bet

The primary risk is concentration. TXS is not diversified across the whole country; it is a bet on Texas. If you already own a broad U.S. equity index, TXS is a redundant and overlapping holding unless the intent is to overweight Texas. If held as a sole U.S. equity holding, TXS exposes the portfolio to Texas-specific risks: a prolonged energy downturn, a tech-sector rout that disproportionately hits semiconductor and software companies, or a shift in corporate migration patterns away from Texas.

Energy exposure is the second risk. A large portion of TXS is weighted toward oil and energy infrastructure. If you are already using a separate energy ETF or direct oil investment, TXS is doubling down. If you are trying to reduce fossil-fuel exposure, TXS will make that harder.

Sector concentration is real too. Technology and energy together typically make up more than half of the index. A market correction that hits those sectors hard will hit TXS harder than a broad index.

## Who buys TXS and how to research it

TXS appeals to investors who believe the Texas economy will outperform the rest of the United States, or who have a personal reason to invest in Texas companies (home-state pride, local knowledge, business connections). It also serves as a convenient bucket for investors who want to make a deliberate overweight bet on the state's corporate base.

To research TXS, start with the fund's fact sheet and the index methodology. Look at the top 10–20 holdings to understand which companies are driving it. Read the prospectus for the expense ratio and any tax implications. Watch Texas economic data — employment, population migration, energy prices, and tech-sector news — to sense whether the underlying state economy is strengthening or weakening. Track energy prices, because the oil and gas weighting makes TXS sensitive to crude and [natural gas](/natural-gas/). Finally, compare TXS's performance to both the S&P 500 and a pure-Texas tech index to understand where the outperformance or underperformance is coming from. If you own TXS, ask whether you are making a deliberate, time-limited bet on Texas, or whether you are accidentally overweighting a single region by mistake.
