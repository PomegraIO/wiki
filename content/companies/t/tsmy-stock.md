*The **YieldMax TSM Option Income Strategy ETF** (TSMY) is a single-stock ETF that holds shares of [Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing](/tsm-stock/) Company (TSMC) and systematically sells [call options](/call-option/) against that position to generate monthly distributions—a mechanical yield amplification strategy that appeals to income investors but carries hidden costs and daily-reset mechanics that can erode value in volatile markets.*

TSMY sits at the intersection of index investing and options trading: the core holding is TSMC stock, one of the world's most important semiconductor manufacturers, but the structure wraps that holding in an aggressive income-harvesting overlay that makes the fund behave quite differently from owning the stock directly. The fund sells short-dated call options on its TSMC shares, collecting premiums that are paid out to shareholders as distributions. In quiet markets with steady TSMC stock price movements, that premium collection can feel like found money—extra yield extracted from volatility. In sharp rallies or crashes, the mechanics unwind.

## The strategy: synthetic yield through option sales

TSMY's income generation comes from a covered-call strategy: the fund owns TSMC shares and sells call options on those same shares, typically on a rolling monthly basis. When investors buy call options, they pay the fund a premium—the price for the right to buy the shares at a future price. The fund keeps that premium, which gets distributed to shareholders. The trade-off is that if TSMC's stock rallies sharply above the [strike price](/strike-price/), the shares are called away (sold), and the fund captures only up to the strike price, missing upside. If the stock falls, the fund still owns the shares but shareholders lose value alongside the wider market—the income distributions do not offset losses in a declining market.

This is not free income; it is a conscious sacrifice of some upside potential in exchange for premium collection in sideways or slowly rising markets. The funds that popularized this structure—the CBOE's "buffer" or "accelerated" ETFs—were originally designed for domestic large-cap indexes (SPY, QQQ) and worked passably because those broad portfolios are difficult to call away and have long earnings histories that support options liquidity. A single-stock version like TSMY faces steeper challenges: TSMC is a real business with genuine earnings, but it is also a Taiwan-listed chipmaker with concentrated geopolitical risk, and its stock can gap sharply on supply-chain news or earnings surprises.

## Why the distributions feel so high—and why they actually aren't

TSMY's headline yield looks attractive because the fund publishes a projected distribution rate that includes both the call premiums *and* any [dividends](/dividend/) from the underlying TSMC shares. On the surface, this sounds like the fund is generating returns beyond what the stock naturally provides. In reality, those premiums are being pulled forward from future stock price appreciation. If TSMC rallies strongly, the fund's cap gains are capped; if TSMC falls, the income distributions do little to cushion the blow. The total return—distributions plus price change—will tend toward what TSMC itself delivers, minus the fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) and the daily reset costs built into the options mechanics.

Daily rebalancing in options-overlay structures introduces friction that is often invisible but real. Each day, the fund's position shifts slightly in relation to the underlying option contracts, and that daily turnover—rolling positions, resetting hedges—incurs transaction costs and slippage that compound over a year. Investors see the distributions landing in their account, but they are not seeing the erosion happening beneath the surface in the form of tracking error (the gap between what the fund returns and what TSMC stock returns). In many options-based [income ETFs](/income-etf/), that tracking error is material.

## TSMC's place and TSMY's risk

The strategy's value hinges on TSMC itself. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company is the world's leading foundry—a manufacturer that builds chips designed by other companies, from Apple's processors to Nvidia's GPUs. TSMC has few direct competitors at the high end of the market, which gives it genuine pricing power and a fortress [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/). Its margins are strong, and the business is relatively durable because it is essential to the entire electronics industry.

However, TSMC is not a boring utility stock. Demand for semiconductor manufacturing swings sharply with the chip cycle, and TSMC's valuation tends to move with it. The company also operates in Taiwan, which introduces geopolitical risk that has only sharpened in recent years—any escalation between Taiwan and China would be catastrophic for the company and the broader tech supply chain. TSMY's covered-call structure does not hedge this risk; it merely collects premiums against it. If the geopolitical situation deteriorates and TSMC shares crater, the income distributions will feel thin consolation.

Within the options world, the level at which TSMY sells its call strikes matters enormously. If strikes are set conservatively (far out of the money), the fund lets upside run but collects smaller premiums and feels more like pure TSMC exposure with a small yield top-up. If strikes are set aggressively (closer to the money), the fund collects fatter premiums but gets called away more often, capping gains. The prospectus and fact sheet specify the strike methodology; investors should read it closely.

## Distribution and structure

TSMY distributes income monthly, which appeals to retirees and income-focused traders who want cash flow rather than price appreciation. The distributions are paid from the premiums and any dividends from the TSMC shares the fund holds. Because TSMC is a mature company, its [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) is modest—typically in the single digits—so most of the distributions come from the call premium harvesting. That means distributions are highest in high-volatility environments where options fetch bigger premiums, but they can also compress rapidly if volatility drops or if TSMC itself becomes less tradeable.

The fund is traded on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) like any other ETF, so investors buy and sell TSMY shares at market-determined prices throughout the day. The expense ratio is published and generally in line with other options-overlay funds—higher than a simple TSMC tracker but reasonable for the active management involved.

## What to watch

Anyone holding TSMY should monitor three things: TSMC's operational health (read earnings calls and supply-chain commentary), the geopolitical temperature around Taiwan (news on US-China relations and semiconductor policy), and the rolling strike levels at which the fund is selling calls (available in the fund's prospectus or fact sheet). The distributions themselves should not be the only measure of success—check the total return (price plus distributions) against holding TSMC stock directly over a year or more. If TSMC rallies strongly and TSMY gets called away, the fund will have underperformed, and that is by design, not by accident. For investors comfortable with capped upside in exchange for monthly cash flow and lower volatility, that trade-off can make sense. For buy-and-hold investors expecting TSMC to compound steadily, a simple TSMC or semiconductor-sector ETF is likely cleaner.
