*The **Thrivent Small-Mid Cap [Equity ETF](/equity-etf/)** (ticker TSME) holds a portfolio of hundreds of small and mid-sized American companies — the companies that fall between the Fortune 500 behemoths like [Apple](/aapl-stock/) or [Microsoft](/msft-stock/) and the [penny stocks](/penny-stocks-investor/) no one has heard of. It is a diversified bet on the idea that these companies, which still have room to grow and lack the scrutiny of mega-cap firms, offer better returns than the largest companies, at the cost of higher volatility.*

TSME is not a bet on any single company. It is a basket of companies, each with a [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) generally between $2 billion and $30 billion (the exact band moves with market conditions, but small-cap and mid-cap are always the two tiers below large-cap). The fund typically holds 600 to 900 stocks, meaning no single holding represents more than 1% or 2% of the total portfolio. This breadth of holdings makes TSME a pure-play small/mid-cap exposure: it rises and falls with the fortunes of that market segment as a whole, not with the performance of any particular business.

## The market segment and the bet

The small-cap and mid-cap segment of the US equity market is fundamentally different from large-cap. Large-cap companies (Apple, Microsoft, [Berkshire Hathaway](/brk-b-stock/)) are mature, profitable, global, and built to last. They grow slowly, pay [dividends](/dividend/), and are followed by hundreds of analysts. Small and mid-cap companies are less mature. Some are founders still running their own companies. Many are still investing heavily in growth rather than paying dividends. They are less liquid — easier for an institutional buyer to get a large position in, harder for that buyer to exit without moving the price. Many are not tracked by as many analysts, which means less-efficient pricing and occasional opportunities for informed investors.

The historical return profile is clear: small and mid-cap stocks have delivered higher average returns than large-cap stocks over long periods. The catch is volatility. Small and mid-cap portfolios swing much more sharply through [market cycles](/market-cycle/). A 20% drop in the broad market might mean a 30% drop in a small-cap fund. A 30% rally might include a 50% surge in small-caps. This volatility is both the draw and the risk: the potential for outperformance comes with real downside risk in bad years.

## Composition and diversification

TSME's portfolio is rebalanced and adjusted monthly or quarterly to maintain the small/mid-cap exposure. The fund does not cherry-pick winners; it aims to hold a representative cross-section of the market segment. The holdings span technology, healthcare, industrials, financials, consumer goods, and every other major sector. Because of that breadth, TSME is not a bet on a single industry's health — it is a bet on the broad small/mid-cap segment and, by extension, the US economy's ability to grow.

The fund is weighted by market capitalization, meaning larger companies within the small/mid-cap band carry more weight. So a mid-cap at the top of the range — say, $25 billion in market cap — will be a larger piece of the portfolio than a small-cap at $2 billion. This is the opposite of equal-weighting, which would give every holding the same size. Market-cap weighting is passive and requires less rebalancing, keeping costs down.

The churn in the portfolio is notable. As companies grow, they eventually "graduate" out of the small/mid-cap range and into large-cap, and the fund sells them. As other companies shrink or new companies go public in the small/mid-cap band, they are added. This natural churn creates turnover — buying and selling costs that are embedded in the NAV and expressed in the fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/). TSME's turnover is typically 15% to 25% annually, higher than a large-cap fund but lower than an actively managed small-cap fund.

## Costs and fees

TSME's expense ratio is the annual fee Thrivent charges to run the fund, as a percentage of assets. For an ETF, small-cap and mid-cap exposure tends to be modestly more expensive than large-cap index exposure because the smaller and less-liquid stocks require more careful trading to avoid moving the price. TSME competes in a field of similar funds (Vanguard, iShares, and others all offer small/mid-cap ETFs), so the fee is competitive but not the absolute cheapest on offer. The difference between a 0.40% fee and a 0.50% fee, compounded over decades, is material; an investor should review the current expense ratio before buying.

## Tax efficiency and distributions

TSME distributes dividends and capital gains to shareholders, typically annually or semi-annually. The distribution is not large relative to most dividend-focused funds; small and mid-cap companies reinvest more earnings into growth than into dividends. Capital-gains distributions occur when the fund rebalances or when its holdings rise in value and the fund sells to trim positions. For a taxable account holder, these distributions create annual tax bills. For a tax-deferred account, they are irrelevant.

## Risk and volatility over time

The volatility of small-cap and mid-cap funds in a market downturn is the single biggest risk. In the financial crisis of 2008, small-cap stocks fell 50% or more, far worse than the broad market decline. In a prolonged [bear market](/bear-market/) or deep [recession](/recession/), the risk of permanent loss in a small-cap portfolio is real. Companies in the small-cap band are more vulnerable to credit crunches, disruption, and competitive pressure because they lack the resources and moats of larger firms.

Conversely, in a recovery or a [bull market](/bull-market/), small-caps often outperform. They have more room to grow, better returns on new projects, and less regulatory pressure. The last decade has been unusual in that mega-cap tech stocks have driven returns, leaving small-caps in the dust — but that outperformance is not a permanent feature of the market, and it may reverse.

## Who TSME suits

TSME is appropriate for investors with a long time horizon (ten years or more), who can tolerate volatility, and who believe in the long-term growth of the US economy. It is a natural complementary holding alongside a large-cap US equity fund or a broad total-market fund, adding small-cap exposure to a portfolio. It appeals to investors in accumulation phase, still earning salary and able to ignore market swings, or to those who have a specific bucket of assets they can afford to see swing by 20%, 30%, or 40% in a bad year.

TSME is a poor fit for anyone who needs the money soon (within five years), who cannot tolerate volatility, or who thinks the US economy is headed for structural decline. It is also less suitable as a core holding in a defensive or income-focused portfolio; small-caps are growth-oriented, not stability-oriented.

## Researching TSME

Thrivent publishes a fact sheet showing the portfolio's composition, the current expense ratio, the recent performance, and the [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/). The holding list is available online, usually in a downloadable spreadsheet. For a deeper dive, TSME's prospectus outlines the fund's mandate, the index or strategy it follows, and the fees. A simple comparison — TSME's performance over the past 5, 10, and 20 years versus a broad small-cap index or its peers — shows whether Thrivent's execution and costs have been competitive.

TSME is a vehicle for taking on small-cap exposure systematically and at low cost. It is not a pick-your-winners vehicle and not a hedge against large-cap decline; it is simply a liquid, diversified way to own the segment of the equity market that sits between the giants and the micro-caps.
