*Small-cap stocks — companies valued between roughly $300 million and $2 billion — sit at an awkward intersection: too small to interest most institutional analysts, yet large enough to offer real businesses with transparent financials. The **Motley Fool Small-Cap [Growth ETF](/growth-etf/)** (TMFS) targets that niche, holding a basket of small growth companies chosen by The Motley Fool's analysts for their fundamentals, moats, and growth potential.*

> "Ignore the crowd and research the individual business."

## Why small caps matter

Small-cap stocks have a reputation for outsized returns, but also for extreme volatility and hidden risks. The logic is straightforward: a small company with a decade of 15% earnings growth ahead of it will compound far faster than a mature large-cap adding 3–5% annually. But small caps are also where information asymmetry peaks — fewer analysts cover them, less institutional capital chases them, and bad news can wipe out shareholders faster because no one is watching closely.

TMFS exists at the sweet spot where The Motley Fool's research process adds value. The firm employs analysts to read earnings reports, management letters, and SEC filings for overlooked small-growth stories that institutional money hasn't yet noticed. At this company size, careful fundamental work can genuinely uncover mispricing; at the mega-cap level, thousands of quants do the same analysis simultaneously, erasing advantages.

## Portfolio structure and approach

TMFS typically holds 50–80 individual small-cap stocks, enough for meaningful [diversification](/diversification/) within the small-cap universe but concentrated enough that individual winners drive performance. The portfolio might include an undersea cable-laying company with high barriers to entry, a regional healthcare business expanding into adjacent markets, a software-as-a-service vendor growing 30% annually with improving margins, or an industrial supplier capturing share in a [fragmented market](/fragmented-market/).

The fund rebalances as positions move in size (a winner that doubles moves toward large-cap territory and may be trimmed) or as the analysts' conviction changes. Turnover is typically moderate for an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) — not buy-and-hold passive, but not a trading operation either. The investment time horizon is genuine compound growth, not quarterly momentum chasing.

## Small-cap growth volatility and timing risk

Small-cap stocks amplify market moves in both directions. In a growth-friendly environment with low [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and appetite for risk, TMFS can deliver spectacular returns; in a downturn when investors flee to safety and earnings-multiple compression hits growth hardest, small-cap losses can be severe. An investor must be comfortable with drawdowns potentially 30–40% worse than the broad [market.

Timing](/market-timing/) also matters. Small-cap growth tends to underperform large-cap growth for years at a stretch (as happened 2015–2023), then suddenly outperform (as occurred in early 2024 and early 2025). A fund like TMFS rewards patient capital and punishes those who buy near peaks and panic-sell into troughs. For this reason, it suits longer time horizons (10+ years) and investors who can ignore short-term noise.

## Active selection advantage and limits

The Motley Fool's value proposition is research depth. Full-time analysts reading regulatory filings can spot secular growth trends and competitive moats before consensus catches on. This advantage is real at the small-cap level, where institutional research is scarce and Wall Street analysts do not cover most names. But small-cap fundamentals are also inherently more fragile — a single leadership change, a lost customer contract, a supply-chain shock, or a faster-moving competitor can rapidly destroy value. No amount of prior analysis prevents those surprises.

Over long periods, active small-cap managers show higher variability in outcomes than large-cap managers — a few deliver substantial outperformance, but the median underperforms passive benchmarks after fees. TMFS's success depends on whether The Motley Fool's analysts are in the top tier of that distribution.

## How to evaluate TMFS

Start with long-term performance: How has TMFS returned versus the Russell 2000 Growth Index (the standard small-cap growth benchmark) over rolling 5-year and 10-year periods, net of the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/)? The fund's factsheet breaks down the top 10 holdings — do they look like credible small-growth stories, or gimmicks?

Read The Motley Fool's published investment theses for the biggest holdings. Does the reasoning hold up to your own scrutiny? Can you understand why each holding matters for long-term growth? If the logic is transparent and you believe in the analyst team's judgment, TMFS is a reasonable vehicle to outsource small-cap research. If you suspect you could find the same stocks yourself through a discount [broker](/broker/) and diversify yourself, the fund's active fee becomes harder to justify.

Small-cap [growth investing](/growth-investing/) demands emotional discipline — tolerating sharp drops and resisting the urge to sell after severe underperformance. TMFS is not a tactical timing tool; it is a long-term partnership bet on The Motley Fool's ability to spot genuine small-cap winners.
