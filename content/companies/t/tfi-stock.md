## What municipal bonds are and who issues them

A municipal bond is a debt security issued by a state, city, county, or other public entity to raise money for a public project — a school system, a highway, a water treatment plant, an airport. When you buy a muni bond, you are lending money to that entity in exchange for regular interest payments and the return of principal at maturity. The critical distinguishing feature is the tax treatment: the interest paid on most municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax, and often from state and local income tax as well if you are a resident of the issuing state.

This tax exemption is a direct cost to the U.S. Treasury — it is, in effect, a subsidy to local governments in the form of lower borrowing costs. In return, state and local governments can borrow at lower [interest rates](/interest-rate/) than they would otherwise need to offer, making infrastructure funding more affordable. For the investor, the trade-off is clear: you accept a lower interest rate (compared to a taxable bond of the same credit quality and maturity) in exchange for tax-free income.

## How TFI works and what it tracks

TFI is an exchange-traded fund, which means it holds a portfolio of hundreds of individual municipal bonds and can be bought and sold on the [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) throughout the trading day, like a stock. The fund tracks the ICE BofI Municipal Bond Index (or a related variant), which attempts to represent the broad U.S. municipal-bond market — most states, most credit qualities within investment-grade, and a range of maturities from short to long.

The fund's holdings include both general-obligation bonds (backed by the full taxing power of the issuer — a state's general revenues) and [revenue bonds](/revenue-bond/) (backed by revenues from a specific project, like toll roads or hospital revenues). It excludes bonds below investment grade, so the portfolio is tilted toward borrowers with strong [credit ratings](/credit-rating/), though the index includes smaller cities and smaller issuers that may not be widely followed on Wall Street.

State Street, which runs the fund under the SPDR brand (the company's stock-trading ETF line), rebalances TFI quarterly to stay aligned with the index. The fund does not attempt to outperform the index through security selection or [market timing](/market-timing/) — it is an index tracker, so its results should closely mirror the index, minus a small [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) and any minor tracking error.

## Who pays tax-free interest and who does not

The tax-free status of municipal-bond interest requires careful reading, because it is not universal. Interest on most munis is exempt from federal tax, but some older bonds and bonds issued for certain purposes (like funding stadiums or other private-use facilities) may be "taxable munis" that pay federal-tax-free interest but not state tax-free interest, or even interest subject to both. Most of what TFI holds should be the standard "triple tax-free" (federal, state, and local income tax-free for residents of the issuing state), but the fund's holdings can include exceptions.

This matters for the after-tax return. To an investor in a high federal-income-tax bracket, a 3.5% tax-free muni yield is equivalent to a much higher taxable yield (the exact calculation depends on the investor's marginal tax rate). But for someone in a lower bracket, or in a tax-deferred account like a 401(k) or IRA, where the tax exemption provides no benefit, a taxable bond might offer better after-tax returns. TFI is primarily a tool for investors who can capture the tax advantage.

## Credit risk and the muni-bond market

Munis have a reputation for safety, and it is mostly deserved — defaults among investment-grade municipal bonds are rare. The issuers have stable revenue streams, strong legal protections for bondholders, and strong political incentives to pay. But safety is not the same as absence of risk.

Some municipal issuers have faced genuine financial stress. Pension liabilities, declining tax bases, economic shocks (like the shutdowns of 2020), and deferral of maintenance spending have pressured local budgets. A few cities have filed for bankruptcy, though even in bankruptcy, general-obligation bondholders have usually recovered most or all of their money. The risk that TFI faces is that one or more of the bonds it holds will see its issuer struggle, which could mark down the bond's value before maturity or (very rarely) fail to pay altogether.

Beyond [credit risk](/credit-risk/), there is [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/). When prevailing interest rates rise, the value of bonds paying lower rates falls. If TFI holds a 2.5% muni bond and interest rates rise so that new munis pay 4%, the existing bond becomes less attractive and its price drops. TFI's intermediate maturity means its interest-rate sensitivity is moderate — it will fluctuate less than a long-term bond fund but more than a short-term fund.

## Costs, liquidity, and taxable-account considerations

TFI's expense ratio is typically in the range of 0.05–0.10%, which is very low and reflects the fact that municipal-bond indexing is a relatively straightforward, non-active endeavor. The fund trades on the exchange with good liquidity during market hours; you can buy or sell TFI shares at any time the market is open without waiting days to settle, as you would with many individual municipal bonds.

For a taxable account, TFI's advantage is immediate: the income it distributes is tax-free, which no taxable bond fund can offer. For a tax-deferred account (an IRA or 401(k)), the tax-free status provides no benefit, and you would be better served by a taxable bond fund or a muni fund in a non-retirement account where the advantage materializes.

## How to research this fund

Begin with TFI's prospectus, which spells out the index it tracks, its investment criteria, and the range of credit qualities held. The fund's fact sheet, updated regularly, shows the [current yield](/current-yield/) (the distribution rate relative to the current price), the average maturity, the average credit rating, and the composition by state and bond type.

Look at the historical performance relative to the ICE municipal-bond index itself; TFI should track very closely, with the only meaningful divergence being the expense ratio and minor rebalancing lags. If the fund is significantly lagging, investigate whether there is a structural issue with the index or the fund's implementation.

For context on the municipal-bond market itself — interest-rate trends, credit conditions, supply — read recent reports from bond-market analysts (available through research sites, [broker](/broker/) reports, and municipal-bond data providers like MSRB's Electronic Municipal Market Access system). Finally, before investing a large sum, confirm your own tax situation with a tax professional; the value of muni-bond ownership depends on your marginal tax rate, which determines how much the tax-free income is actually worth in your situation.
