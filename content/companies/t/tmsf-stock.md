*The **[T. Rowe Price](/t-rowe-price/) Multi-Sector [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (TMSF) is an actively managed exchange-traded fund that holds a mixture of dividend-paying stocks, [bonds](/bond/), and other fixed-income securities from multiple sectors and geographies, structured to deliver regular cash distributions to shareholders.*

TMSF represents T. Rowe Price's approach to tackling a problem many income-focused investors face: a single-asset-class strategy leaves money on the table. A bond fund in a low-rate environment yields little; a [dividend](/dividend/) portfolio can fall sharply in a market downturn; [sovereign debt](/sovereign-debt/) in emerging markets offers yield but [currency risk](/currency-risk/). By blending them — equities from dividend aristocrats and stable companies, investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), government securities, and selective emerging-market exposure — TMSF aims to find income wherever it exists and spread the portfolio's risk across multiple sources.

The fund is actively managed, so its exact composition shifts based on the managers' view of which sectors offer the best risk-adjusted income. In periods when equity valuations are stretched, managers might tilt toward fixed income; when [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) are wide (meaning bonds are yielding well relative to their risk), they might increase bond allocation; when dividend stocks look cheap, equity weighting might rise. This flexibility is a core feature — a passive multi-asset fund has to rebalance mechanically, whereas TMSF can respond to changing market conditions.

Monthly distributions are a distinctive feature. Most dividend stocks pay quarterly; most bonds pay semi-annually. TMSF aggregates income from all its holdings and passes cash to shareholders monthly, which appeals to retirees or others who prefer predictable, frequent cash flow. The tradeoff is that monthly distributions may include a small portion of return of principal in some months — something to verify annually on the fund's tax documents — and frequent distributions can incur higher trading costs inside the portfolio if managers are constantly repositioning.

The multi-sector approach hedges single-sector [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/). A pure-dividend-stock portfolio is vulnerable to a correction in equities or a rotation away from low-volatility, high-yield companies. A pure-bond portfolio is vulnerable to rising rates compressing values. By holding both, plus some real-asset or emerging-market exposure, TMSF spreads that vulnerability. The fund still experiences volatility — no [income fund](/income-fund/) is immune to market downturns — but the volatility tends to be smoother because its sources of return are not all moving in the same direction at the same time.

[Expense ratios](/expense-ratio/) for actively managed multi-asset funds are typically higher than passive equivalents, reflecting the cost of a research team that monitors dozens of sectors and countries. The fee structure should be transparent in the prospectus; understanding whether the fund's excess return (compared to a simple benchmark like a 60/40 stock-bond mix) covers the cost of active management is part of evaluating whether the fund is worth buying.

Tax efficiency deserves attention. Dividends from large-cap U.S. stocks are often taxed at favorable capital-gains rates; interest from bonds is taxed as ordinary income. Funds held in taxable accounts should be monitored for their tax profile: a TMSF with heavy yields from corporate bonds might generate more ordinary-income tax liability than a shareholder expects. The fund's documentation should break down the character of its distributions so a holder can calculate after-tax returns.

TMSF trades like any ETF on an exchange, so it can be entered and exited intraday. For someone trying to build a diversified income portfolio, TMSF can function as a single holding that provides broad income exposure in one ticket, rather than buying separate stock funds, bond funds, and emerging-market funds individually.

## How to research TMSF

Read the prospectus and the fund's latest fact sheet, both available from T. Rowe Price, to understand the strategy, the target [asset allocation](/asset-allocation/), and the range of holdings the managers can hold. Review the fund's holdings list and recent allocations to see what mix of stocks and bonds is currently in place. Compare the fund's yield to other multi-asset income funds (such as competitors' offerings) and to a simple 60/40 stock-bond portfolio to get a sense of whether the complexity and active management is delivering a worthwhile return.

Monitor the fund's distributions over time — are they stable or volatile? A spike in distributions followed by a sharp drop may indicate the fund is paying out capital rather than income, which is not necessarily bad but is important to understand. Check the fund's annual fact sheet or prospectus supplement for a breakdown of ordinary income, [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/), and return of capital in each distribution, so you can properly account for taxes.

Watch for major shifts in the fund's holdings mix reported in quarterly or semi-annual reviews. A significant tilt away from bonds and toward equities (or vice versa) suggests the managers' view of risk and opportunity is shifting, which can affect volatility going forward. Finally, compare the fund's performance relative to a simple benchmark like a 60/40 stock-bond index over rolling three- and five-year periods to see whether active management is adding value.
