*The **Truth Social American Next Frontiers ETF** (TSNF) is a concentrated [small-cap growth fund](/small-cap-growth-fund/) that holds 50 US companies selected for innovation in technology, finance, and media, with an explicit tilt toward companies aligned with libertarian principles and critical of traditional institutions—a niche strategy that combines conventional small-cap growth beta with a values-based stock-picking overlay.*

## What does the fund actually hold?

TSNF is a stock-picker's fund, not a market-cap-weighted index. The portfolio contains 50 holdings across technology, financial services, healthcare, and consumer sectors, all headquartered in the United States. The unifying logic is not sector-driven but value-driven: the selection tilts toward companies positioned as challengers to established orders—fintech disrupting banking, direct-to-consumer brands, software-as-a-service providers, and media platforms claiming to be less censorious than incumbents. The prospectus and fact sheet spell out the selection criteria; investors should read both carefully to understand what "innovation" and "alternative" actually mean in this fund's investment process.

A concentrated 50-stock portfolio in the small-cap space is inherently volatile. It is not a passive tracker of the Russell 2000 or other broad small-cap indexes; it is a curated list of names that someone—the fund's adviser—believes will outperform. That requires conviction, and conviction can be right or catastrophically wrong.

## How do small-cap growth funds perform relative to the broader market?

Small-cap stocks, by definition, are companies with smaller [market capitalizations](/market-capitalization/) than large-cap stalwarts. Historically, small caps have offered higher long-term return potential than large caps, partly because they are riskier and therefore require higher expected returns to justify investment, and partly because smaller companies can grow revenues and earnings more rapidly than giants already worth trillions of dollars. However, that higher return potential comes with higher volatility, lower liquidity (it can be harder to buy or sell a small-cap stock without moving the price), and higher failure risk. Many small companies fail, are acquired at unfavorable prices, or simply plateau.

During growth-favoring markets—when investors are willing to bid up future earnings—small-cap [growth funds](/growth-fund/) tend to outperform. In value-favoring markets or [bear markets](/bear-market/), they often lag badly because small companies have less financial cushion and can cut to profitability more slowly. TSNF, as a growth-tilted, values-inflected small-cap fund, will likely behave along those lines: strong in optimistic periods when the companies in the fund are hitting growth targets and weak in downturns when liquidity dries up and growth enthusiasm evaporates.

## What role does the "alternative" framing play?

TSNF's prospectus and marketing materials position it as an investment vehicle for companies challenging the establishment—fintech companies building alternatives to traditional banking, media platforms offering editorial independence from mainstream outlets, health and wellness companies skeptical of conventional medical institutions, and technology vendors claiming to build "without censorship." This framing is part of the selection criteria and part of what differentiates TSNF from conventional small-cap growth funds.

That positioning is a two-edged sword. On one side, it attracts investors who genuinely share those values and want capital allocation aligned with their beliefs. On the other side, "alternative" or "anti-establishment" does not correlate reliably with business quality or long-term returns. A company built to challenge incumbents can fail for reasons unrelated to the challenger narrative—bad management, product-market misfit, or simply being ahead of its time in a way that burns through capital. Conversely, some of the companies in the portfolio may be included partly because they fit the values narrative rather than purely on growth fundamentals. Investors should examine the 50 holdings themselves, read recent earnings reports, and ask whether each business has durable competitive advantages and sensible growth paths, independent of the story layered on top.

## Expense ratio and trading costs

Like all active small-cap equity funds, TSNF's published [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) includes management and operational costs. Because it is actively managed and contains stocks that are less liquid than megacap names, the fund's trading costs (the spreads and slippage from buying and selling those 50 holdings) tend to be higher than a passive large-cap [index fund](/index-fund/). That drag reduces the fund's return relative to its benchmark. Over a year or more, those costs and the performance of the underlying stock picks are the two things that determine whether TSNF's total return beats or lags small-cap growth alternatives.

## What is the liquidity of the fund itself?

TSNF trades on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) as an ETF, so investors can buy and sell shares at market prices during trading hours. However, the fund's own trading volume and [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) depend on demand for the fund itself—not on the liquidity of the underlying 50 stocks. If TSNF is thinly traded, the spread between the buy and sell price may be wide, making frequent trading expensive. Prospective investors should check the fund's trading volume and spreads before committing capital if they plan to move in and out.

## Concentration and diversification trade-offs

A 50-stock portfolio is more diversified than owning 10 stocks but far less diversified than a broad small-cap index (which holds 1,000+). That means TSNF depends more heavily on the individual success of each holding and is less insulated from single-company disasters. If the fund's largest holdings (which are likely to be 5–8% of the portfolio each) stumble, the fund's performance will feel the impact sharply. Conversely, if a few picks are home runs, they can drive outsized returns. This is the classic active-management gamble: concentration for the possibility of outperformance, at the cost of higher tracking error (variance from an index).

## How to research TSNF before investing

Start with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, available from the fund sponsor or the SEC. These documents list all 50 holdings, the expense ratio, and the investment objectives. Then examine the top 10 holdings in detail: read their latest earnings reports, look at revenue and earnings trends, and assess whether the growth narrative is real or narrative [inflation](/inflation/). Check the fund's one-year, three-year, and five-year returns against the Russell 2000 Growth Index or another small-cap growth benchmark—TSNF's fee structure and stock-picking skill determine whether it has beaten or lagged that benchmark over various periods. Finally, ask yourself: would I want to own this fund's current portfolio directly? If the answer is "not all 50 of these companies," that is a sign the fund is being selected partly on the "alternative" story rather than purely on investment merit. That is not necessarily wrong, but it is important to know.
