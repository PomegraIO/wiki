TDSC is designed to appeal to investors who lose sleep over market downturns. The fund holds a mix of stocks and [bonds](/bond/), and a mechanical rule adjusts that mix continuously — selling stocks and buying bonds when the market becomes turbulent, then rotating back to stocks when conditions calm. The goal is to never lose more than 10% from the fund's rolling high point.

## How does the drawdown-control mechanism work?

A drawdown is the decline from a previous high. If TDSC rises from 100 to 120 and then falls to 110, the current drawdown is roughly 8% from the peak. The fund's rule monitors this drawdown constantly and acts if it approaches 10%.

When the drawdown reaches a preset level — say, 7% or 8% — the rule triggers a rebalance. The fund sells a portion of its stock holdings and buys bonds or cash. Bonds are less volatile than stocks, so holding more bonds dampens losses if the market continues to fall. As the market stabilizes and the drawdown shrinks (because the fund's holdings stop declining or begin to recover), the rule gradually rotates back into stocks, capturing gains as the market rebounds.

This is not market-timing in the traditional sense — the rule does not try to predict whether the market will go up or down tomorrow. Instead, it reacts mechanically to the current drawdown level. It is a realized loss-control rule, not a forecast one.

## What does the portfolio actually hold?

TDSC typically holds a mixture of broad US stock index exposure (through positions in [equity ETFs](/equity-etf/) or stocks themselves) and bond index exposure. The exact allocation moves dynamically: when volatility is low and the market is rising, the fund might be 80% stocks and 20% bonds. When drawdown pressure builds, it might shift to 40% stocks and 60% bonds, or even higher bond allocation. The fund does not hold just one target allocation; it is designed to shift continuously in response to market conditions.

The bonds are usually government or investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), chosen for their stability and negative correlation to stocks — when stocks fall, bond prices often rise, providing a cushion. This negative correlation is what makes the stock/bond mix effective as a risk-control mechanism.

## What are the costs and mechanics?

ETC Cabana charges an annual [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) for managing TDSC; this fee typically reflects the cost of the underlying index positions plus the cost of the frequent rebalancing required to manage the drawdown target. Because the fund rebalances often — sometimes monthly, sometimes more frequently depending on market conditions — trading costs inside the fund may be higher than a passive buy-and-hold [index fund](/index-fund/). Check TDSC's prospectus for the exact fee.

The fund trades on an exchange like any ETF, so you buy and sell shares during market hours at whatever price the market is quoting. The underlying holdings (stocks and bonds) trade continuously, but your entry and exit prices are set by the exchange market for TDSC shares themselves.

## What outcomes should you expect?

In calm markets where stocks are rising steadily, TDSC typically trails a pure stock index — it holds bonds that earn less than the booming equities. But that is the price of insurance: you sacrifice some upside in exchange for protection.

In a severe market correction where stocks fall 30%, TDSC's drawdown-control rule should limit the fund's loss to something close to 10%. This is the core value proposition. The 10% loss is still meaningful, but it is vastly smaller than the benchmark decline.

In a choppy, sideways market with frequent small losses and gains, TDSC may underperform due to whipsaw — the rule might sell stocks near a bottom and buy bonds before the rebound begins. Over many cycles this average out, but unlucky timing creates drag.

Over very long periods, the smoothing benefit of the drawdown control has a measurable cost: because TDSC avoids the largest [bull markets](/bull-market/) and stays partly in bonds, long-term returns trail a 100% stock portfolio. For someone with a 30-year horizon and iron discipline, pure equities likely win. For someone who is tempted to sell near market bottoms, TDSC's smoother ride may actually produce better results by keeping them invested.

## Who is this fund for and what are the real risks?

TDSC appeals to investors approaching or in retirement who cannot afford a 30% loss, or to those with low risk tolerance who know they will panic-sell if the market declines steeply. It is also attractive for conservative allocators who want equity exposure but are unwilling to hold 100% stocks.

The main risk is that the 10% drawdown limit is a target, not a guarantee. In a market gap — a sudden, severe decline with no time for the rule to rebalance — TDSC could exceed 10% drawdown temporarily. Additionally, the frequent rebalancing creates tax events for taxable accounts; this is a consideration if TDSC is held outside a retirement account.

A second risk is opportunity cost. Because TDSC dampens losses, it also dampens gains. A twenty-year bull market would see TDSC lag a stock index significantly. Drawdown-control strategies impose a long-term return penalty that you are paying to sleep better at night.

To research TDSC, read the prospectus carefully to understand the exact mechanism by which drawdowns are measured and the rebalancing rule is triggered. Ask whether it is truly mechanical (published, no discretion) or whether ETC Cabana has some discretionary override. Compare TDSC's historical drawdowns and returns to other downside-protection ETFs, and model whether the 10% limit is attractive for your own risk tolerance and time horizon. Check trading volume to ensure the fund is liquid enough for your position size.
