*The **[T. Rowe Price](/t-rowe-price/) Long Municipal [Income ETF](/income-etf/) (TMNL)** holds a portfolio of [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/) issued by U.S. states, cities, and other public entities. It is built for investors who want reliable, tax-exempt income and are willing to hold the [bonds](/bond/) for years.*

## What is a municipal bond, and why would anyone buy one?

A municipal bond is a loan to a state government, a city, a school district, a hospital, or another public body. When you buy one, you are lending money to that entity, and they pay you interest in return. The unique feature is that the interest you receive is exempt from federal income tax — and often from state income tax too if you live in the state that issued the bond.

This tax advantage makes municipal bonds attractive to higher-income investors. If you are in the 35% federal tax bracket, a municipal bond paying 4% yields the equivalent of roughly 6% on a taxable bond (because you do not owe taxes on it). For someone in a lower tax bracket, the advantage is smaller or nonexistent. TMNL is designed for those who do benefit from that tax exemption — typically people with significant income who would otherwise owe substantial federal taxes.

## What does TMNL hold?

TMNL is a fund of municipal bonds with a specific focus: long-term obligations. "Long-term" typically means bonds that mature in 20, 30, or even 40+ years. This is a deliberately narrow slice of the municipal-bond universe. Other municipal funds might mix short- and long-term bonds, or focus on high-yield (lower-credit-quality) municipal bonds. TMNL sticks to longer-dated bonds from creditworthy issuers.

The fund does not hold all municipal bonds equally. T. Rowe Price's portfolio managers select from thousands of available municipals, looking for entities with stable finances, a history of meeting their obligations, and bonds trading at what the managers judge to be fair or attractive prices. The fund might hold bonds from school systems, water districts, airport authorities, state housing agencies, and general-purpose state and local governments. The diversity across issuers and project types reduces the risk that a single default would meaningfully hurt the fund.

## What makes TMNL different from other municipal-bond products?

T. Rowe Price is one of the largest asset managers in the world, with decades of expertise in municipal bonds. TMNL is one of its flagship municipal funds, and it carries the firm's active-management approach: human portfolio managers making selection and timing decisions, not just passively holding a municipal-bond index.

The "long-term" focus is critical to understand. A bond's maturity date matters because longer-dated bonds fluctuate more in price when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) change. If rates rise, a 30-year bond drops in value more dramatically than a 5-year bond does. Conversely, if rates fall, a 30-year bond appreciates more. For someone holding to maturity, this matters less — they get the [coupon payment](/coupon-payment/) and the full principal back regardless of price gyrations. But for someone needing to sell before maturity, or for a fund that marks itself to market daily, duration is a key risk. TMNL holders are taking on that risk in exchange for higher yields.

## How does a municipal fund generate returns?

TMNL's returns come from two sources: the interest payments (coupon income) the bonds pay, and any price appreciation or [depreciation](/depreciation/) if the fund sells bonds before they mature or if interest-rate movements change bond values.

When interest rates are stable and the credit quality of issuers is solid, the fund earns a steady stream of coupon income — the principal source of return. This income passes through to fund shareholders monthly, and it is tax-free at the federal level (and usually at the state level for those in the issuing state). Investors who need current income find this structure attractive.

Price appreciation happens when interest rates fall. If the fund bought bonds yielding 4% and rates subsequently drop, those bonds become more valuable — new bonds would offer only 3%, so the older 4% bonds trade at a premium. The fund can sell them for a capital gain. Conversely, if rates rise after purchase, the fund's bonds lose value. This [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) is the permanent downside of owning long-duration bond funds: if rates move against you, the fund's share price falls.

## What are the risks specific to TMNL?

**Interest-rate risk.** Because TMNL holds long-term bonds, its share price is sensitive to changes in interest rates. A sharp rise in rates — which can happen when the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) tightens [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/), when [inflation expectations](/inflation-expectations/) rise, or when market conditions deteriorate — causes the fund's shares to fall in value. Conversely, falling rates boost the fund's value. Someone buying TMNL needs to be comfortable with this duration risk and prepared to hold through periods of negative returns if rates rise.

**[Credit risk](/credit-risk/).** Although TMNL holds bonds from creditworthy entities, municipal issuers can and do default or face financial stress. Cities have gone bankrupt (Detroit in 2013, multiple smaller cities since). School districts have faced revenue shortfalls. A severe [recession](/recession/) could trigger a wave of municipal defaults if local and state revenues collapse. The portfolio is diversified to limit the impact of any single default, but credit risk is never zero. The tax-exempt status of the bonds does not protect investors from default losses.

**[Inflation risk](/inflation-risk/).** The coupon payments from TMNL's bonds are fixed. If [inflation](/inflation/) rises to 5% or 6%, a bond paying 3% loses purchasing power. Investors in long-term municipal funds face the same inflation risk as any fixed-income holder. They are betting implicitly that inflation will remain moderate.

**[Liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/).** Municipal bonds are less liquid than U.S. [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) or [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/). If TMNL needed to sell a large position quickly, it might face wider [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and difficulty finding buyers. This is a secondary risk for a large fund with many potential sellers, but it is real during market stress.

## Tax treatment and who benefits most

The income from TMNL is exempt from federal income tax and generally from the state income tax of the issuing state (if you are a resident of that state). These tax advantages are most valuable for high-income earners in high-tax states. Someone in the 35% federal bracket who lives in California (with its additional state income tax) benefits much more from municipal bonds than someone in a lower bracket or in a lower-tax state.

It is worth noting that capital gains from selling municipal bonds at a profit are taxable. If TMNL sells bonds for a gain due to falling interest rates, those gains are subject to capital-gains tax. Only the coupon income (the interest payments) is tax-exempt.

## Expenses and how TMNL compares

TMNL carries an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) that covers the costs of the fund's management, trading, and operations. Because it is actively managed, its expense ratio is higher than a passive municipal-bond [index fund](/index-fund/) would charge. Investors pay for the expertise of T. Rowe Price's municipal-bond team; whether they receive value from that active management over time is a question each investor must answer. Some years T. Rowe Price's selection and timing add value; in other periods, a simple index fund would have performed as well or better.

## How to research TMNL

Anyone considering TMNL should read the fund's prospectus (available from T. Rowe Price and the SEC), which details the portfolio composition, the fee structure, and the risk factors. The fund's fact sheets and [annual reports](/10-k/) provide holdings data and performance history. Useful questions to ask: What is the average maturity of the bonds in the fund? What is the average credit rating? How has the fund performed in rising-rate environments? How does its performance compare to a municipal-bond index fund?

For municipal-bond research more broadly, reading about the state or local entities issuing the bonds is important. A bond from a stable, growing city or state is safer than a bond from a municipality facing fiscal stress. The SEC's municipal-bond disclosure platform and rating agencies like Moody's and Standard & Poor's publish information on the creditworthiness of issuers.

TMNL suits conservative investors seeking tax-exempt income for the long term, those in high tax brackets who benefit from the exemption, and those who can tolerate the share-price fluctuations that come with holding long-duration bonds. It is not appropriate for investors who may need liquidity in the short term or who are uncomfortable with interest-rate risk.
