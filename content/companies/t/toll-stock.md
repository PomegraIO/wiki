Investors often chase the idea of investing in **moats** — not water-filled castle walls, but genuine competitive advantages that let a company earn outsized profits without constant fear of cheaper rivals. The **Tema Monopolies and Oligopolies ETF**, ticker TOLL, is built on that thesis: it is a concentrated, [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) that buys stocks believed to possess structural advantages so strong that competition is either absent or severely limited.

The fund's name is its strategy. Management looks for companies that operate in markets where one or two firms dominate (monopolies) or where a small handful control the majority of customers or profit (oligopolies). These are not formal definitions in the securities law sense — they are market observations. The goal is to own the handful of firms that can raise prices without losing customers, face no real threat of disruption, and earn persistent above-average returns on capital.

## How the fund works

Tema, the sponsor, employs active managers who conduct fundamental analysis of candidate holdings. The research leans on qualitative judgment: Does this company have a brand moat (like a luxury house or a trusted financial name)? A network effect (a telecommunications company where the value grows as more people join)? A regulatory license or technical barrier that competitors cannot overcome? A scale advantage that smaller rivals cannot match? The managers attempt to identify these qualities before the market fully prices them in.

Because the fund is concentrated — typically holding 20 to 40 stocks rather than several hundred — it is not a broad market substitute. It is a thematic bet on a specific investment premise: that moat-rich companies will outperform, often over long horizons. The holdings cover multiple sectors and geographies, but they are united by the manager's assessment of competitive strength.

TOLL is actively managed, not index-tracking. That means the fund has higher costs than a passive alternative — expenses run roughly in the 0.60% to 0.70% range per year — and the fund's performance will diverge from any benchmark based on the manager's stock selection and timing decisions. Some years the fund will outperform because the thesis works; other years it may lag if the market favours companies outside the moat-heavy universe (growth companies, for instance, or small-cap disruptors).

## The investment case and the counterargument

The premise behind TOLL is one of the oldest ideas in equity investing: companies with durable competitive advantages compound wealth over time because they earn returns on capital that exceed their cost of capital, year after year. A firm that can command premium prices, hold costs down, and reinvest profits at high returns creates shareholder value without relying on luck or perfect timing.

There is historical precedent. Companies like Coca-Cola, Nestlé, and [American Express](/axp-stock/) sustained outsized profitability for decades. More recently, firms like LVMH and [Berkshire Hathaway](/brk-b-stock/) have demonstrated the power of brands and moats. TOLL is betting that identifying moat-rich companies will outperform the average market.

Yet the counterargument is serious. First, moats erode. The competitive advantages that seem impregnable today — a franchise operating license, a brand loyalty, a proprietary technology — can be overcome, disrupted, or made irrelevant by technology or regulation. Second, moat-heavy companies are often already expensive; the market has already figured out they are good, which limits future upside. Third, the fund's active management adds cost; unless the managers are exceptionally skilled, that cost will drag on returns relative to a low-cost index.

## What TOLL holds

The fund's holdings rotate, but historically they have included household names with visible competitive advantages — pharmaceutical firms with patent portfolios, luxury-goods conglomerates, leading casino operators, financial-services oligopolies, and technology platforms. The concentration is real; the top 10 holdings typically represent half or more of the fund's assets.

That concentration also means the fund is less stable than a broad market fund. A negative surprise at one of the top holdings can move the fund's value substantially. Conversely, if one of the thesis companies executes well, the impact on the fund is outsized.

## Real risks

The most important risk is that the fund's managers misidentify moats or overestimate their durability. A company that seems protected by regulation may face regulatory change; one that seems protected by brand may see younger consumers prefer a new competitor. Technology changes are particularly dangerous; a moat that took 20 years to build can erode in five years once a new platform or business model emerges.

[Concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) is also material. Unlike a fund holding hundreds or thousands of names, TOLL's holdings are significant in size. A poor decision on a top holding, or a market downturn that hits the chosen stocks harder than the broad market, translates directly into fund performance.

Finally, there is selection risk. The fund's returns depend on whether management's thesis holds up and whether they execute it well. Over time, active managers face strong headwinds in beating their benchmarks after fees; TOLL is betting it can overcome those headwinds through superior fundamental analysis.

## How to research this fund

Start with the fund's holdings list and prospectus, which lay out the thesis and the current portfolio. Look at the top 10 holdings and understand why the fund believes each has a durable moat. Research those companies' competitive positions, regulatory environment, and any recent changes in their industries. Track the fund's returns against a broad market index (like the S&P 500) over various time periods to see whether the moat thesis is translating into outperformance, or whether the active management is adding or subtracting value net of costs. For a long-term holding, a 5- and 10-year return track record is more meaningful than recent performance.
