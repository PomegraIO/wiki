**The Institution.** Texas Capital Bancshares (ticker TCBI on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/)) is a regional bank based in Dallas. It operates across Texas and the Southwest, with a customer base skewing heavily toward commercial and middle-market businesses—companies with $10 million to $500 million in revenue—and toward [commercial real estate](/commercial-real-estate/) developers and investors. It is not a consumer bank; you do not open a personal checking account at Texas Capital. It is a commercial lender. The business model is straightforward: take deposits from businesses and institutions, lend money to other businesses (mostly on commercial real estate projects or for working capital and [acquisitions](/acquisition/)), and pocket the spread between what the bank pays on deposits and what it charges for loans.

**Deposits and lending.** The customer base comprises mostly Texas-based, middle-market companies, real estate investors, and commercial borrowers. These customers hold deposit accounts with the bank and also borrow from it. A commercial real estate developer, for example, might keep operational cash on deposit and also borrow to finance a multifamily or office project. That concentration in Texas and in commercial real estate is both an advantage and a risk. An advantage: the bank is well-known in its market, has deep relationships with borrowers and depositors, understands the Texas [real estate cycle](/real-estate-cycle/) intimately, and can move quickly on decisions that larger national banks cannot. A risk: if the Texas economy deteriorates or Texas commercial real estate enters a downturn, the bank's entire loan book suffers [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/).

**The competitive set.** Texas Capital competes against other regional and community banks in Texas (Cullen/Frost, [Prosperity Bancshares](/pb-stock/), [Southside Bancshares](/sbsi-stock/)), against larger national banks with Texas operations ([JPMorgan Chase](/jpmorgan-chase/), [Bank of America](/bank-of-america/), [Wells Fargo](/wells-fargo/)), and against nonbank lenders (private credit firms, alternative finance). The large nationals have more capital, more [diversification](/diversification/), and lower funding costs—they can undercut Texas Capital on pricing. The community banks are smaller and more local. Texas Capital occupies a middle ground: large enough to handle sizeable deals, focused enough to move fast and to know the market. Moat is customer relationship and local expertise. That moat is not absolute—a large bank can buy its way in or a borrower can shop terms—but it is durable if the bank maintains quality service and credit discipline.

**Capital and [credit risk](/credit-risk/).** Banks are fundamentally leveraged businesses. Texas Capital takes customer deposits and deploys them into loans. If loans go bad—if borrowers default—the bank loses money. Prudent credit underwriting and loss management are essential. Commercial real estate lending is complex; the bank must evaluate property values, borrower strength, [market cycles](/market-cycle/), and lease dynamics. During booms, credit looks great and loan losses are low. During downturns (2008–2011, 2020), defaults spike, loan losses mount, and capital is consumed. Banks with high concentrations in one sector (like Texas commercial real estate) are more exposed to sector-specific downturns. The 2008 financial crisis hit banks with heavy real estate exposure hard.

**Interest rate sensitivity.** Banks profit from the spread between what they pay on deposits and what they earn on loans. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises [interest rates](/interest-rate/), deposit costs tend to rise (because depositors demand higher rates on accounts, or they move money to competing banks offering better rates). Loan rates also rise, but the timing and magnitude are not always synchronized. A rapid rate increase can compress net interest margins if deposit costs rise faster than loan rates. Conversely, a rate decrease can help margins. This interest-rate sensitivity is a key driver of bank profitability and stock performance. Investors in TCBI should monitor the Federal Reserve's policy and the bank's margin trends closely.

**[Balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) watch.** Capital levels matter. Regulators require banks to hold sufficient capital relative to [risk-weighted assets](/risk-weighted-assets/). A bank with strong capital can absorb losses; a bank with lean capital is vulnerable. The loan-to-deposit ratio reveals funding stability—a bank that relies too heavily on wholesale funding (short-term borrowing from the market) rather than stable customer deposits is more fragile. Nonperforming loans and loan-loss provisions reveal credit quality. Deposit flows reveal customer confidence and funding stability.

**Recent history and context.** Texas Capital has weathered several cycles. During the 2008–2011 financial crisis, the bank reported significant loan losses and took capital damage, a reminder of how severe [credit cycles](/credit-cycle/) can be. The bank emerged and rebuilt capital. In the low-rate environment of 2010–2021, margins were compressed—deposit costs were near zero, and loan yields were low—limiting profitability growth. When the Federal Reserve began raising rates in 2022–2023, margins began to recover. Like all banks, Texas Capital is exposed to the economic cycle and to the rate environment; both are macro drivers that management cannot control. What management can control is credit discipline, customer service, and capital allocation.

**Risks in the current regime.** A [recession](/recession/) would test credit quality, especially if it affects the Texas commercial real estate market. Persistent high interest rates might pressure loan demand (borrowers become reluctant to take on expensive debt). A major failure in the commercial real estate sector (office towers experiencing sustained vacancies, for instance) could generate surprise losses. Regulatory changes—capital requirements, lending standards, deposit insurance—could affect the bank's business model and profitability. Funding risk: if deposit outflows accelerate (because interest rates at other banks become more attractive or because confidence erodes), the bank might need to source expensive wholesale funding, which squeezes margins.

**The valuation question.** Banks often trade as a multiple of [book value per share](/book-value-per-share/) (sometimes called price-to-book). A bank with strong returns on capital might trade at 1.2× to 1.5× book; a bank with weak returns might trade at 0.8× or less. Valuation depends heavily on the outlook for net interest margins, credit losses, and [return on equity](/return-on-equity/). During rate-hike cycles, consensus often upgrades bank valuations because margins improve. During downturns, valuations compress as loss expectations rise. Texas Capital's valuation should be assessed against peer banks, against its own historical average, and relative to the expected path for interest rates and credit losses. The stock is a bet on the profitability of middle-market commercial lending in Texas under the current and future interest-rate and economic regime.

**For investors tracking the company.** Read the 10-K (SEC CIK 0001077428) for:
- Net interest margin trends. Is the spread widening or compressing?
- Credit metrics: nonperforming loans, loan-loss provisions, charge-offs. Is credit deteriorating?
- Deposits and funding. Are deposits growing or shrinking? At what rate is the cost of deposits rising?
- Capital ratios. How much buffer does the bank have against stress?
- Texas real estate exposure. What percentage of the loan book is in CRE?

On earnings calls, listen for management commentary on loan demand, deposit flow, margin outlook, and any changes in credit quality or underwriting discipline. Watch for any management or strategy changes. A good regional bank with strong credit discipline and customer relationships can deliver solid returns, but the industry is cyclical and sensitive to rates and economic conditions. The returns available depend on where we are in the cycle and whether management executes well on an unstable foundation.
