Teva is the world's largest manufacturer of generic pharmaceuticals — drugs that are chemical copies of brand-name medicines whose patents have expired. The company produces over 500 different generic drugs, ranging from common antibiotics to complex injectables. It operates in North America (where it has the largest market share), Europe, Australia, Israel, and emerging markets, and it earns revenue both from finished generic products sold to pharmacies and hospitals, and from active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) sold to other drugmakers. This is a volume business: Teva competes by making billions of pills and vials every year at the lowest possible cost per unit.

Generic drugs are the backbone of modern pharmacy. When a brand-name drug like Lipitor loses patent protection, any company with manufacturing and regulatory approval can make and sell a chemical copy at a fraction of the original price. Patients and insurance companies demand generics because the savings are enormous — a patient might pay $100 per month for brand-name Lipitor or $10 per month for generic atorvastatin. Teva succeeds because it can make atorvastatin at such scale and efficiency that even at $10 per month it generates acceptable profit margins. A smaller generic maker, burdened with higher per-unit costs, cannot.

The competitive landscape is ruthless. Teva faces competition from other large generics producers like Sandoz (Novartis's generic division) and Mylan, from smaller regional generics companies, and from Indian and Chinese manufacturers competing on the basis of nearly zero cost. A single drug might have twenty competitors, all vying for the lowest price. Teva's answer is relentless cost reduction: it owns or controls its supply of active ingredients, it has optimized its manufacturing processes over decades, and it shifts production to lower-cost jurisdictions when feasible. But there is a price floor below which no one can manufacture safely and remain profitable, and Teva is closer to that floor than it likes to be.

The company's competitive moat is scale and operational efficiency, not innovation or brand. Teva does not invent new drugs; it manufactures copies of old ones. What it does is take a newly generic drug, work out how to make it reliably and cheaply, navigate regulatory approval in every market it wants to serve, and then execute at a scale rivals cannot. For a complex injectable or a drug with tight manufacturing specs, Teva's depth of expertise and its invested capital matter. For simple tablets — aspirin, ibuprofen, common antihistamines — competition is fiercer and margins are thinner.

The business model is built on churn. Patents expire continuously: the loss of patent protection on one blockbuster drug can represent millions in annual revenue. Teva must constantly win new generics conversions and launch drugs faster or cheaper than competitors just to stay in place. Management therefore invests heavily in regulatory compliance, manufacturing efficiency, and new-product launches. A single factory shutdown or a regulatory setback can ripple across the entire business.

Pricing pressure is relentless and structural. Payers — insurance companies, government health systems, hospital groups — demand that generic prices fall every year. In the United States, large pharmacy benefit managers negotiate directly with generic makers, often demanding step discounts as volumes grow. In Europe, price controls are explicit; many countries regulate generic margins or mandate price reductions when multiple competitors enter a market. India and other producers will always undercut by a few percentage points to win volume. The result is that Teva's gross margins are in the 40% to 50% range on finished generics, high-sounding until one realizes the company must also cover massive distribution, regulatory, and manufacturing overhead.

The business is also capital-intensive in ways the market sometimes underestimates. Teva must maintain or upgrade dozens of manufacturing facilities across multiple countries, each of which must meet exacting regulatory standards (FDA, EMA, etc.). It must invest in quality assurance to ensure that its pills and injectables are safe and pure — any contamination or quality failure can destroy reputation and brand. It must maintain inventory of finished goods and APIs across the global supply chain, which ties up working capital. These costs are fixed or semi-fixed and do not scale linearly with volume, which means there is a minimum scale required to be competitive.

Debt is a notable feature of Teva's [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/). The company has incurred substantial debt through [acquisitions](/acquisition/) — notably the 2016 acquisition of Actavis's generic business, which for a time moved Teva's [leverage ratios](/leverage-ratio-forex/) into uncomfortable territory. The company has since reduced debt, but it remains leveraged relative to peers. In a [recession](/recession/), or if generic pricing falls faster than expected, debt service becomes a constraint on flexibility.

The company also operates a small specialty-pharmaceuticals business, selling branded drugs (often acquired from other companies) for niche conditions. This business has higher margins than generics and more pricing power, but it is much smaller than the generic base and does not meaningfully change the competitive picture. It is a hedge: if generic pricing becomes unsustainable, specialty pharma is where Teva might pivot some resources.

Regulatory and supply-chain risk loom large. Teva's largest manufacturing facilities are concentrated in Israel, Puerto Rico, and a few other locations. A facility shutdown due to safety, quality, or geopolitical issues can disrupt supply of multiple drugs. The company has also faced manufacturing challenges in recent years — quality inspections and FDA warnings related to facility conditions — which have damaged investor confidence and created uncertainty about future regulatory approvals.

Anyone assessing Teva should start with the 10-K (SEC CIK 0000818686) and understand the portfolio of drugs: which are growing (newer entries with fewer competitors), which are mature (price-squeezed commodities), and which are at risk of new generic entrants. Watch gross margins by product line and geography; margins are a leading indicator of competitive stress. Pay attention to R&D spending on the specialty-pharma business and any pipeline commentary — management will say whether it believes the specialty business can grow or is simply a cash-harvesting operation.

On calls, listen for updates on manufacturing efficiency, facility investments, and any commentary on pricing trends in major markets like the United States. The United States is where the margin pressure is most acute, as pharmacy benefit managers and large retailers demand ever-lower prices. Monitor debt reduction and [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/); Teva must generate cash to service debt and invest in manufacturing. Finally, watch litigation and regulatory updates carefully. Quality issues, antitrust allegations, or pricing inquiries can move the stock and raise questions about the sustainability of the business model.
