*The **[T. Rowe Price](/t-rowe-price/) Equity [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (ticker: TEQI) is a passively managed exchange-traded fund that holds a diversified portfolio of large US stocks selected for their dividend-paying history and likelihood of raising [dividends](/dividend/) over time. It is aimed at investors who want a steady income stream from stocks without having to hand-pick individual payers.*

## What TEQI holds and how it works

TEQI invests in roughly 100 to 150 large-cap US companies, all of them selected from the universe of firms that pay dividends and show the financial strength to sustain or grow those payouts. The fund constructs its portfolio by screening the market for established payers — firms with strong profitability, durable competitive advantages, and a track record of returning cash to shareholders. Unlike a simple dividend-yield screen, which might chase the highest yields regardless of safety, the TEQI approach aims to capture both current income and the prospect of rising income as selected companies increase their payouts over time.

The result is a portfolio heavy on sectors that naturally generate steady cash and pay out to shareholders: banks and financial services, insurance, utilities, consumer staples, and mature industrials. These are sectors where recession-resistant revenue, predictable demand, and limited growth requirements make large cash distributions economical and sustainable.

TEQI trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) like any other [equity ETF](/equity-etf/), so shareholders can buy and sell shares at market prices throughout the trading day. The fund is not leveraged, not inverse, and not structured to decay — it is a straightforward, buy-and-hold vehicle that pays dividends quarterly. Like all ETFs, TEQI has an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) that covers the cost of running the fund; T. Rowe Price's expense ratios are typically competitive, and a dividend-focused strategy does not require active stock-picking or high trading costs.

## T. Rowe Price and the dividend-income category

T. Rowe Price is a venerable asset manager headquartered in Baltimore, with a long tradition of building funds for long-term investors. The company's approach to [dividend investing](/dividend-investing/) reflects a core belief that stocks selected for financial strength and dividend sustainability can provide inflation-hedged income over decades — a philosophy that has informed the firm's [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) and ETF lineup for years.

TEQI arrived as part of T. Rowe Price's push into the ETF market, a channel that has grown explosive in the decades since the first ETFs launched. The appeal to investors is straightforward: dividend-paying stocks offer current income, and growth in those dividends can outpace general [inflation](/inflation/) over long [holding periods](/holding-period/). That proposition matters especially to retirees or others living on portfolio income, who often cannot afford to wait years for price appreciation alone.

The dividend-focused category itself is large and crowded. Competing funds track similar strategies — screening for dividend history, yield, and safety — and some use entirely passive indexing approaches while others layer in active management. What distinguishes funds like TEQI is the specific screening criteria, the blend of sectors that result, and the expense cost of running the portfolio.

## Who buys TEQI and why

The typical buyer is someone in or approaching retirement who needs current income but does not want to move entirely out of stocks. TEQI offers that combination: monthly cash flows from dividends, while the underlying stocks still have potential to appreciate if the economy and corporate profits remain sound. For taxable accounts, the quarterly distributions are tax-inefficient compared to capital appreciation alone (dividends are taxed when paid, not deferred), so TEQI is often held in tax-advantaged accounts like IRAs where the drag of dividend taxation does not apply.

Another appeal is simplicity. Rather than researching individual dividend stocks or building a portfolio from scratch, investors can buy one fund and own a professionally curated selection. The [diversification](/diversification/) across roughly 100 large, established companies reduces the risk that any single name misbehaves or cuts its dividend unexpectedly.

## Risks and what can go wrong

The main risk is [market risk](/market-risk/) — if stock prices fall sharply, TEQI's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) falls with them, and shareholders can lose principal. Dividend-paying stocks are not immune to [recessions](/recession/) or sector-wide downturns. During severe market stress, even established companies sometimes reduce or suspend dividends, which erodes both the income and the capital-appreciation case.

Another risk is sector concentration. Because dividend payments make most sense in mature, cash-generative industries, TEQI will tend to overweight sectors like utilities, banks, and consumer staples. That tilt away from technology and growth can be a drag during periods when those sectors outperform, meaning the fund may lag a broad market index in strong [bull markets](/bull-market/).

[Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) is subtle but real. Dividend stocks often serve as proxies for [bonds](/bond/) — when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise sharply, the yield on a 3% dividend suddenly looks less attractive compared to a newly issued bond paying 5%, and stock prices adjust downward as investors sell. The reverse is also true: when rates fall, dividend stocks often rally.

Inflation is a longer-term concern. While dividend growth can eventually keep pace with inflation, there is no guarantee, and nominal dividend income does not protect you from inflation by itself. Over decades, real (inflation-adjusted) returns depend more on whether the underlying businesses can raise their prices and maintain margins than on the dividend policy.

## How to research TEQI

Start with the fund's fact sheet and prospectus, available on the T. Rowe Price website. These lay out the expense ratio, the holdings as of the most recent date, and the distribution history. Look at what sectors TEQI overweights relative to the broad market — utilities, financials, and consumer staples will typically be larger slices than in a market-cap-weighted [index fund](/index-fund/).

Track the fund's [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) (the annual [dividend per share](/dividend-per-share/) divided by the current price) and compare it to other dividend-focused competitors and to the broader [stock market](/stock-market/). Over longer periods, measure total return — the combination of price change plus dividends reinvested — to see whether the focus on income has come at the cost of lagging gains elsewhere.

Finally, follow T. Rowe Price's commentary on the fund strategy and the dividend landscape in their investor reports and shareholder letters. As with any stock fund, TEQI's returns depend ultimately on the earnings and dividend policies of the underlying 100-plus companies it holds, so understanding the broader economic backdrop and corporate profitability trends helps frame realistic expectations.
