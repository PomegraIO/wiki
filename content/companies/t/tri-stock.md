Thomson Reuters (New York: TRI) is an information and workflow company that supplies data, content, and software platforms to financial institutions, law firms, regulatory bodies, and corporations. It operates at the intersection of three deep verticals—financial services, legal services, and risk and compliance—and earns revenue primarily through subscription arrangements rather than transaction fees or one-time sales. The company is Canadian-domiciled but serves a global customer base, and its business is anchored in recurring arrangements that prove resilient across economic cycles.

## A centuries-old name built on news and data

Reuters has its roots in the 19th century as a news agency—founded in 1851 by Paul Julius Reuter as a carrier of financial and political information across Europe, first by telegraph and later across every medium available. The brand became synonymous with financial pricing and breaking news, and for more than a century it remained primarily a real-time data and news business. In the late 20th century, as financial markets grew more complex and regulatory demands expanded, the business evolved to include software platforms and specialized intelligence tailored to the financial and legal professions.

Thomson, a separate Canadian company founded in 1893, grew as a newspaper and information conglomerate. In 2008, the Thomson Corporation acquired Reuters in one of the largest media-industry [acquisitions](/acquisition/) of the era, creating Thomson Reuters. The [merger](/merger/) was designed to unite Thomson's existing legal-software strength (inherited from earlier acquisitions like West Publishing and FindLaw) with Reuters's core strength in financial data and news. The combined firm consolidated research across financial services, law, and regulation—the three sectors most dependent on reliable, current information and most willing to pay recurring fees for the tools that deliver it.

## How Thomson Reuters earns money

The company operates through two main business divisions, each with a different revenue structure but both anchored in subscriptions.

**Refinitiv** (until recently branded as the Financial & Risk segment) supplies real-time data, news, pricing, analytics, and workflow software to investment banks, wealth managers, traders, corporations, and [central banks](/central-bank/). Refinitiv's customers pay fixed annual or multi-year subscriptions for access to terminals, data feeds, and intelligence tools. This business resembles the traditional Bloomberg terminal model—customers depend on the data to make decisions, switching is expensive, and price elasticity is low. Refinitiv generates the largest share of the company's revenue and the highest margins.

**Legal & Compliance** (formerly Thomson Reuters Legal & Regulatory) serves law firms, in-house legal departments, courts, and regulators with practice-management software, legal research, compliance automation, and regulatory intelligence. Customers include large law firms that use the firm's flagship research platform (Westlaw) and smaller firms that use cloud-based practice-management and billing tools. Like Refinitiv, much of this revenue is recurring and subscription-based, though the Legal segment has a higher proportion of true per-transaction fees (e.g., per document review or per court filing).

A smaller **Corporate Services** segment provides data and software to corporate back-office, compliance, and risk-management teams.

## The durability of recurring revenue

Thomson Reuters is built on a contract-renewal model. Customers—traders, lawyers, compliance officers, risk managers—are locked into annual or multi-year subscriptions that renew unless actively cancelled. These arrangements produce highly predictable cash flow and allow the company to invest in product development and geographic expansion with confidence. Subscription revenue as a percentage of total revenue has steadily increased, which explains why Thomson Reuters is priced and valued like a software company despite its heritage as a news and data distributor.

The switching costs are material. A law firm that manages its entire practice in Westlaw and runs billing through the same platform faces real friction in moving to a competitor. A trader working inside a Refinitiv terminal every day—monitoring prices, executing research, placing orders—does not switch away lightly. That stickiness translates directly to pricing power and allows the company to implement price increases without losing large customer bases.

## Challenges and pressures

Thomson Reuters competes against Bloomberg in financial data and Lexis-Nexis in legal research—both entrenched, well-capitalized rivals. In recent years the company has also faced competition from free or lower-cost alternatives: open-source legal research, cheaper data terminals for retail investors and smaller traders, and cloud-based practice-management software from startups have all nibbled at margins in specific segments.

The migration of business to cloud platforms and the growing demand for artificial intelligence integration in workflows has required sustained capital investment. The company has moved aggressively to modernize its platforms, cloud-enable its data delivery, and add AI features for document review, contract analysis, and legal research acceleration—work that carries near-term costs and longer-term revenue upside.

Beyond product, regulatory and geopolitical risk matters. Changes to antitrust enforcement, financial-services regulation, or data-privacy rules in large markets like Europe affect Thomson Reuters' operations and customers directly. The company also faces exposure to any deepening of trade tensions or sanctions that affect international markets—financial customers operate globally, and disruption to cross-border data flows or transaction settlement touches revenues.

## Researching Thomson Reuters

The 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001075124) is the essential resource. The filing breaks revenue and [operating margin](/operating-margin/) by segment (Refinitiv and Legal separately), explains contract-renewal rates and pricing dynamics, and lays out the scale of the customer base. Pay attention to management's commentary on cloud migration, AI product adoption, and customer retention rates—particularly for the largest Refinitiv subscribers, who represent a outsized share of revenue.

The quarterly earnings calls reveal the real dynamics: watch the organic revenue growth rate (which strips out acquisitions and currency), the software-specific margin trends, and any color on customer wins and losses in competitive situations. For investors, the durability of Thomson Reuters lies in its stable installed base and recurring contract structure, not in explosive growth.
