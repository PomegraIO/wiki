*The **Truth Social American Icons ETF** — trading under the ticker TSIC on a U.S. exchange — is an exchange-traded fund designed to track companies and holdings reflective of American cultural influence and iconic status in their respective sectors. Rather than following a traditional index of established market segments, it pursues a thematic selection approach, attempting to capture equity exposure to businesses that embody or represent significant American brands and institutions.*

## The thematic investing angle

Thematic exchange-traded funds occupy a middle ground between broad market indexing and highly concentrated sector bets. Where a large-cap [index fund](/index-fund/) holds hundreds of companies regardless of narrative, and a [sector fund](/sector-fund/) narrows to a single industry, a thematic fund draws a circle around a specific idea or cultural thread — in this case, "American icons" — and selects holdings that fit the story. This approach appeals to investors who believe certain cultural or economic trends will outperform, even if they cannot neatly fit into traditional sector classifications.

TSIC's structure reflects this discretionary construction. The fund does not track a published index mechanically; instead, the fund sponsor makes active or semi-active decisions about which companies qualify as emblematic of American identity and leadership. This means the holdings and their weightings can shift based on subjective judgment rather than pure market-cap weighting rules, introducing both opportunity and risk.

## How the fund is built and costs

As an exchange-traded fund, TSIC trades on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) throughout the day at prices set by supply and demand, much like a individual stock. This differs from a [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/), which prices once per day at the close. Investors can buy or sell shares at any moment the market is open, and institutions can create or redeem large blocks of shares directly with the fund sponsor if the fund trades at a discount or premium to its underlying holdings, a mechanism that keeps the fund's market price tethered to the value of the holdings within.

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) — the annual cost expressed as a percentage of assets under management — varies depending on the fund's specific structure and the sponsor's pricing strategy. For a thematic fund of this kind, the expense ratio typically runs higher than a passive broad-market index fund but may be competitive with other actively or semi-actively managed [equity ETFs](/equity-etf/). The fund sponsor charges this fee to cover administration, custody, portfolio construction, and trading costs; it is deducted proportionally from returns over time.

## The geographic and competitive moat

American-focused thematic investing often rests on a specific assumption: that U.S. companies, particularly those rooted in American culture or consumer preferences, have durable advantages in global competition. The theory is that the scale of the American consumer market, the dominance of English-language media and technology, and the historical weight of American brands create structural advantages that will persist.

However, this thesis is contestable. Global companies — including many European and Asian firms — have eroded traditional American competitive moats over decades. What remains genuinely American in origin often operates globally; the distinction between an "American icon" and a multinational conglomerate headquartered in America is increasingly blurred. The fund's construction depends on how precisely the sponsor defines the theme and how much conviction it places in American outperformance.

## Concentration and volatility risks

Thematic funds by design concentrate on a narrower set of ideas than broad indexes. While TSIC attempts to capture American iconic status across sectors, it necessarily excludes huge swathes of the market — foreign companies, domestic firms that do not fit the thematic criterion, and businesses that operate in less-visible sectors. This concentration can boost returns in years when the theme performs well but magnify losses in downturns.

The definition of "American icon" itself carries risk. Cultural and commercial leadership shifts, brands fade, and new companies displace old ones. A fund built around an idea of American identity that no longer resonates with markets or consumers may underperform, not because the companies are poorly run but because the thematic premise has weakened.

## Tracking and research

Investors evaluating TSIC should examine the fund's prospectus and fact sheet for the precise holdings, weighting methodology, and how the sponsor defines the thematic universe. The prospectus outlines the fund's investment objective, strategy, and risk disclosures. The fund's annual and semi-annual reports detail which companies are held and how the portfolio has evolved. Tracking tables — which compare the fund's return to relevant benchmarks — reveal whether the thematic bet has added or subtracted value relative to broader American equity indexes.

Because a thematic fund is only as good as the idea underpinning it and the sponsor's execution, investors should also examine the fund sponsor's track record, the turnover rate (how often holdings are swapped), and the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) (the difference between the buy and sell price on the exchange), which determines how much trading friction one incurs when entering or exiting the position.
