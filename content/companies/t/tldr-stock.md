*The **Laddered T-Bill ETF**, ticker TLDR, is a fund that buys and holds United States [Treasury bills](/treasury-bill/) — short-term loans to the federal government, maturing in 13 weeks or less. Instead of holding all bills at the same maturity date, TLDR spreads its holdings across a ladder of different maturities. This lets you put cash to work earning Treasury rates without locking up your money.*

## Why short-term Treasury bonds matter right now

Treasury bills are among the safest investments on Earth — they are direct obligations of the US government, paid back in a matter of weeks or months. For decades, people looking to keep money liquid and safe accepted very low returns, sometimes almost zero, in exchange for that certainty. The math worked because [interest rates](/interest-rate/) were near zero for years after the 2008 financial crisis and throughout much of the 2010s.

That changed. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) began raising interest rates sharply in 2022 to fight [inflation](/inflation/), the yield on short-term Treasury bills rose from near zero to 5% or higher. Suddenly, parking money in Treasury bills became genuinely interesting — you could earn a real return, not a punishment for caution. TLDR was launched in 2022 to capture that shift: it lets you buy a bundle of these high-yielding bills through a single exchange-traded fund instead of buying them one at a time through a Treasury auction.

## The ladder: spread the maturity dates

Here is how the ladder works. Imagine a fund holds ten bills that mature on ten different dates spread over 13 weeks. One matures this week, one next week, and so on. When the first one matures, the fund uses that cash to buy a new bill maturing 13 weeks from now. Each week, one bill drops off the ladder, and a fresh one is added at the far end. The result is a constant flow of money coming in as bills expire, letting the fund maintain a steady portfolio of short-duration Treasury debt.

This matters practically. Because bills are dropping off the ladder all the time, the fund is constantly reinvesting coming-due money at current Treasury rates. If rates are high, new bills pay high rates. If rates fall, newer bills pay less, but the fund is still collecting the old, higher-paying bills until they expire. The ladder smooths out the timing risk of trying to guess when is the best moment to reinvest a big chunk of cash.

## Interest income and the cost of holding it

When you own TLDR, you earn the interest that the underlying bills pay. That income accumulates in the fund each day and is reflected in the share price — your investment grows without you needing to sell anything. Once a year, TLDR distributes that interest as a [dividend](/dividend/). The amount varies with interest rates and the general level of Treasury yields, so in a high-rate environment you receive more, and in a low-rate environment you receive less.

The catch is the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/): TLDR charges roughly 0.05% to 0.10% per year to manage the fund, much lower than the fee on a stock or [bond](/bond/) [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/), but not zero. For someone earning 4% or 5% on their Treasury bills, paying 0.05% to 0.10% leaves you with 3.9% to 4.95% after costs. That is still a real return, and it is far higher than what a savings account offers. But it is not the full Treasury yield — the fee is a small but real drag.

## Who this is for and why it might not be for everyone

TLDR works best for investors sitting on cash they do not need immediately but want to deploy earning decent returns without taking stock-market risk. A retiree living on distributions could keep several months of spending money in TLDR and earn several percentage points while waiting to spend it. An investor building a large portfolio purchase over time might use TLDR as a holding tank for the cash, earning more than a money-market fund while the account grows. A conservative investor uncomfortable with bonds (which can fall in price if interest rates rise) might use this as the safe part of their portfolio, since bills mature so quickly that rate risk is minimal.

The downside: TLDR has very little upside if interest rates fall. In fact, if rates drop sharply, the fund's yield falls. Holding cash, even in TLDR, is a defensive move. If you believe stocks or longer-term bonds are about to outperform, TLDR keeps you sidelined. Also, TLDR trades at [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) continuously during market hours, so you can buy or sell it anytime the [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) is open — but the underlying Treasury bills themselves do not trade as frequently as stocks do, which means TLDR's trading liquidity is good but not as tight as the biggest stock ETFs.

## Getting the full picture

To evaluate TLDR, start with the fund's fact sheet and prospectus, both available from ProShares or through the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/). These will tell you the current distribution rate (which varies with Treasury yields), the exact maturity dates of the bills the fund holds, and the historical returns for different periods. Compare TLDR's expense ratio to rivals — the iShares Short [Treasury Bond](/treasury-bond/) ETF (SHV) is another popular ladder-based Treasury holding with a different cost structure and maturity profile.

Watch the yield environment. As long as short-term Treasury bills are yielding several percent, TLDR offers real value as a cash alternative. If yields fall to near zero again, the advantage shrinks. Also watch TLDR's share price; because the fund holds bonds, the price will fluctuate slightly as interest-rate expectations shift, even though the underlying bills themselves have almost no price risk. These price movements are small — a 0.5% change is substantial for a Treasury-bill fund — but they are real.

For anyone with cash they need to keep liquid and safe, TLDR has become a practical choice where few good options existed before. The Treasury bills backing it are as safe as it gets, and the yield makes the money work while you wait.
