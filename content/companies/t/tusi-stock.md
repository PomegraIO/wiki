*The **Touchstone Ultra Short [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (ticker TUSI) is an exchange-traded fund that builds a portfolio of ultra-short [bonds](/bond/) and floating-rate debt securities, aiming to provide investors with income from fixed-income instruments while maintaining a short duration profile that limits [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/).*

## What TUSI holds

Think of TUSI as a collection of short-term IOUs. When you hold this fund, you own a piece of many bonds—most of them maturing in a year or three. They come from companies, governments, and banks that need to borrow money for short periods. Some bonds have fixed [interest rates](/interest-rate/). Others have floating rates, meaning the interest they pay changes as overall interest rates move. TUSI mixes both types.

Why [floating-rate bonds](/floating-rate-bond/)? They protect you in rising-rate environments. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises interest rates, floating-rate bonds automatically pay you more interest. A fixed-rate bond loses value when rates rise, but a floating-rate bond can keep paying a steady real return because the coupon resets. TUSI uses this feature to cushion against the negative impact of rising rates.

## How short duration works

Duration is a measure of how much a bond's price falls when interest rates rise. A bond with zero duration would not change price at all if rates moved. A bond with five years of duration would drop about five percent if rates rose one percentage point. TUSI's duration typically sits below two years, and often under one. This means if rates spike, TUSI does not take a big hit to its price.

The tradeoff is simple: you get safety and stability in exchange for modest yield. A long-term bond fund might offer higher income, but it also risks bigger losses when rates climb. TUSI sacrifices some yield to keep the price stable. For investors who cannot tolerate sudden drops in the value of their holdings, or who might need to sell at an unpredictable time, this stability matters.

## Income and distributions

TUSI pays out income regularly—usually monthly or quarterly. The amount changes as the bonds in the fund mature, get replaced, and interest-rate environments shift. When overall interest rates are high, TUSI's income is higher. When rates are low, so is TUSI's income. You cannot count on receiving the same amount every quarter; the distribution rises and falls with the fund's yield.

This income does not come from the fund earning capital gains. It comes from the interest the underlying bonds pay. The fund collects that interest and distributes it to shareholders. If you own TUSI in a regular taxable brokerage account, these distributions are taxable income. If you own it inside an individual retirement account (IRA) or other tax-deferred account, you do not pay taxes on distributions until you withdraw from the account.

## The interest-rate scenario

When the Federal Reserve is raising rates, ultra-short bond funds like TUSI shine. Longer-duration bonds sink in price. TUSI, holding mostly short bonds and [floating-rate notes](/floating-rate-note/), stays relatively flat. Your principal is protected while longer-bond investors watch their holdings decline. The trade-off appears in falling-rate environments: when the Fed cuts rates, long-term bond prices rise sharply while TUSI's price barely moves. You miss the capital-appreciation party, but you also avoid the earlier pain.

For investors living through a period of high and rising interest rates, TUSI can feel like the obvious choice. For investors expecting rates to fall, a longer-duration bond fund might produce better total returns. Picking between them requires a view on where rates are headed, which is speculative and uncertain.

## Credit risk and holdings quality

TUSI holds [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/) in the vast majority of its portfolio. Investment-grade means rated BBB- or higher by the major rating agencies—bonds issued by creditworthy companies and governments. The fund typically avoids junk-rated bonds (below-investment-grade securities from weaker credits) and focuses on names unlikely to default in the next year or two.

This does not mean zero risk. Even investment-grade credits can get into trouble. And in severe economic downturns, spreads—the extra yield investors demand to hold [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) instead of risk-free [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/)—widen sharply, and some bonds rated investment-grade can be downgraded. But the risk is much lower than in high-yield or speculative-grade bond funds.

## Costs and turnover

TUSI has an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/)—the annual fee expressed as a percentage of assets—that is moderate within the ultra-short bond category. This fee covers the fund's operations, management, and trading costs. Because ultra-short bonds mature quickly and are replaced continuously, the fund has meaningful portfolio turnover (the rate at which securities are bought and sold). Higher turnover can create trading costs and tax drag, especially in taxable accounts. Read the fact sheet to understand TUSI's current turnover rate and how it compares to peers.

## Who this fund is for

TUSI works for conservative savers wanting more yield than cash. It suits retirees who need stable income and cannot bear large fluctuations in principal. It makes sense for investors building a [bond ladder](/bond-ladder/)—using TUSI alongside intermediate and long-term bond funds to spread maturity dates. And it attracts anyone who believes interest rates will stay high or rise further and wants to avoid the price damage longer bonds would suffer.

TUSI does not work well for investors expecting rates to fall sharply, since falling rates would produce capital gains in longer-duration bonds while TUSI's price stays flat. It also does not work for investors who need growth: ultra-short bonds are not designed to multiply your money, only to preserve it and pay modest income.

## How to research TUSI

Start with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet from Touchstone. These documents explain the fund's objective, permitted investments, fees, and holdings. Review the [current yield](/current-yield/), the average maturity of the portfolio, and the mix of floating-rate versus fixed-rate bonds.

Compare TUSI's yield to other ultra-short [bond ETFs](/bond-etf/) and to the current yield on short-term Treasury bonds. If TUSI's yield is much lower than alternatives, ask why: it might be higher fees, lower credit quality, or simply that TUSI is a smaller fund with fewer assets to deploy efficiently. Look at the [historical volatility](/historical-volatility/) and [maximum drawdown](/maximum-drawdown/)—how much the fund's price fell in its worst month or year—to confirm that it behaves as you expect.

Read recent fact sheets to understand whether the fund's duration and credit composition are drifting. Ask yourself whether you expect rates to rise, fall, or stay stable, and whether TUSI's ultra-short profile aligns with that expectation.
