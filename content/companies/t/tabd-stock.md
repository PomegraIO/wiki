## What distinguishes an actively managed bond fund from the passive kind?

Most exchange-traded [bond](/bond/) funds track an index passively — they hold a predetermined set of bonds in fixed or slowly rebalancing weights, and the manager's job is simply to track that benchmark efficiently. TABD takes a different approach. Its portfolio manager actively selects bonds and adjusts the portfolio's composition based on forecasts about [interest rates](/interest-rate/), [credit spreads](/credit-spread/), economic conditions, and individual issuer prospects. This is closer to the work of a traditional [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/) manager but packaged in the tax-efficient, intra-day-tradeable wrapper of an ETF.

The manager of TABD may decide, for example, that corporate spreads are too tight — meaning the extra yield a [corporate bond](/corporate-bond/) offers over government debt is insufficient compensation for the [credit risk](/credit-risk/). In response, the manager might reduce corporate exposure and shift into Treasuries until spreads widen. Or the manager might anticipate that the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) is nearing the end of a rate-tightening cycle and position for longer-duration bonds, which perform better when rates fall. These kinds of tactical shifts are the hallmark of active management. A passive [index fund](/index-fund/) cannot make them; it simply holds what the index dictates.

## What goes into the TABD portfolio?

TABD's holdings span the fixed-income landscape. At its core are investment-grade corporate bonds — debt issued by companies with [credit ratings](/credit-rating/) of BBB or higher, including financials, industrials, energy, utilities, and consumer names. The fund also holds U.S. Treasury and agency debt (bonds issued or guaranteed by government agencies like [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/)). Depending on market conditions and the manager's outlook, TABD may hold high-yield (sub-investment-grade) corporate bonds, which offer higher yields but carry greater default risk. Mortgage-backed securities and other securitized debt may round out the portfolio.

The fund's duration — a measure of how sensitive the portfolio is to interest-rate moves — is actively managed. When the manager expects rates to fall, the portfolio tilts toward longer-duration bonds, which rise sharply in price when rates decline. When the manager expects rates to rise, the portfolio reduces duration, limiting the price decline. This tactical flexibility is unavailable in a passive index fund, which must hold whatever duration the index dictates.

## Costs and tax efficiency

TABD charges an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) of approximately 0.50 to 0.70 per cent annually — higher than a passive [bond ETF](/bond-etf/) like the iShares Core Aggregate Bond ETF (which charges roughly 0.04 per cent) but modest compared to an actively managed mutual fund. The higher fee reflects the cost of the manager's research, analysis, and portfolio decisions.

Because TABD is an ETF rather than a traditional mutual fund, it inherits the ETF structure's tax advantage. In-kind creation and redemption mechanisms mean the fund can rebalance and accommodate investor flows without triggering internal capital gains. This tax efficiency has been one of active management's key disadvantages historically — mutual fund managers' frequent trading often generates taxable gains. The ETF wrapper mitigates this, making an actively managed ETF potentially more tax-efficient than an actively managed mutual fund pursuing a similar strategy.

## The bet implicit in owning TABD

Buying TABD is a bet that the fund's manager can add value through credit selection and tactical allocation decisions. This requires a belief in two things: first, that the manager has genuine skill in identifying bonds trading at attractive prices relative to their risk, and second, that this skill will persist and outweigh the fund's expense ratio.

The historical record on active bond management is mixed. Some managers do genuinely outperform their benchmarks consistently, but many do not. The fund's past performance should be reviewed carefully — not just absolute returns, but returns relative to a comparable passive index fund, and especially returns after fees. A fund that beats the index on a gross basis but trails it after fees is a failure. Similarly, performance during a single period can be luck; consistency across full [market cycles](/market-cycle/) (rising and falling rates, spreading and tightening credit conditions) is more meaningful.

## Research essentials

A prospective investor should begin with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet. The prospectus explains the manager's investment process, the types of bonds the fund may hold, and risk disclosures. The fact sheet shows the current portfolio, top holdings, weighted-average credit quality and duration, and expense ratio. Comparing TABD's returns to the Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index (the standard benchmark for broad fixed-income portfolios) over recent years, and especially over a full market cycle, reveals whether the manager has added value. Finally, reviewing the fund's turnover — how frequently the manager buys and sells — gives a sense of the manager's style: very high turnover suggests tactical, frequent repositioning; lower turnover suggests a longer-term holding approach.

For an investor seeking professional bond selection and willing to pay for it, TABD offers liquidity and structural tax advantages that traditional bond mutual funds do not. For an investor content with passive indexing, the expense ratio may not be worth paying.
