T-Mobile operates the third major wireless network in the United States, a facilities-based carrier that owns spectrum licenses and physical infrastructure and sells access to it. The company derives the bulk of its income from monthly subscriptions — postpaid accounts that operate on contracts, prepaid services, and business plans — and supplements that with device sales, tower leasing, and a newer home internet offering. With over 100 million customers, T-Mobile is far smaller than Verizon and AT&T by market cap and revenue, yet large enough to be profitable and to invest meaningfully in network upgrades.

## The products and revenue streams

At heart, T-Mobile sells wireless access — the ability to make calls, send texts, and consume data over its network. Postpaid is the largest and most valuable segment: customers sign up for a plan (entry-level, unlimited, family bundle) and pay a fixed monthly fee. The company tracks postpaid net additions (how many new customers it adds each quarter) and churn (how many it loses) as critical metrics because these customers are more profitable and have higher switching costs than prepaid. Prepaid customers, by contrast, top up their account as they use data and are easier to lose; prepaid revenue is more volatile and carries lower margins.

T-Mobile also serves small and large businesses with tailored plans and services. The home internet business, launched in 2021, uses 5G to bypass traditional cable or fiber providers in select markets; it is higher-margin than wireless (lower churn, higher ARPU potential) but still nascent. Device sales — smartphones, tablets, smartwatches — are lumpy and customer-acquisition-heavy; the company often subsidizes phones to win or upgrade customers, then recoups the cost through higher monthly fees. Tower leasing (T-Mobile owns some towers outright and leases them to other operators and media companies) and roaming agreements with international carriers add smaller revenue streams.

The wireless subscription revenue is highly predictable and recurring, which is why carriers generate stable cash flows. Device and accessory sales are tied to the consumer upgrade cycle and economic sentiment.

## Spectrum and infrastructure: the barrier to entry

Wireless carriers cannot exist without spectrum — radio frequencies licensed by the Federal Communications Commission. T-Mobile's portfolio is diverse:

- **600 MHz (low-band):** Covers vast areas with moderate penetration into buildings; the foundation of nationwide coverage. T-Mobile acquired significant 600 MHz holdings from the Sprint merger.
- **2.5 GHz / n41 (mid-band):** The backbone of modern 5G; faster than low-band but shorter range. T-Mobile has rich mid-band depth.
- **Millimetre-wave (high-band):** Extremely fast but only useful in densely populated urban areas with line of sight.

Operating spectrum is expensive in two ways: first, the company must bid billions in government auctions to acquire licenses; second, it must build and maintain the physical infrastructure — towers, antennas, fiber backhaul, switching equipment — to use that spectrum. A fully deployed national network costs tens of billions to build and requires ongoing capital investment to maintain and upgrade. This capital intensity is the ultimate moat in wireless; once a competitor has built a network, a new entrant cannot.

T-Mobile owns some infrastructure outright but increasingly partners with independent tower companies to reduce capex. The company maintains a network operations center and continuously monitors signal quality, load, and customer experience.

## Positioning and competitive differentiation

T-Mobile has built market share primarily through aggressive pricing and a reputation for simplicity. The company eliminated overage charges before competitors, pushed unlimited data plans, removed long-term contracts (common at AT&T and Verizon), and simplified the pricing structure to reduce plan fragmentation. These moves gained customer loyalty and forced Verizon and AT&T to respond in kind.

The 2020 merger with Sprint was transformative. It eliminated a fourth major competitor, consolidated T-Mobile's spectrum holdings, and created a company with both scale and network depth. Regulatory approval was conditional on network investment and job commitments, both of which the company has largely met.

5G is the current technology battlefield. T-Mobile deployed mid-band 5G faster than AT&T initially, creating a genuine speed advantage that has gradually narrowed as AT&T and Verizon caught up. The real differentiation now is breadth and reliability of coverage across rural and urban markets alike — where T-Mobile's low-band depth is an asset.

## Challenges and structural constraints

**Mature market:** Wireless penetration in the United States is nearly universal; growth is limited to net adds that come from competitors and to device upgrades driven by technology generational shifts.

**Pricing pressure:** Three competitors vying for customers in a commodity market naturally compress margins. Any price increase must be justified by superior network or customer experience.

**Capex treadmill:** Deploying and maintaining a nationwide network is capital-intensive and unending. T-Mobile must invest billions annually to keep pace with competitors.

**Churn risk:** Even with strong brand and network, customers can switch. Postpaid churn is a constant competitive metric; prepaid churn is higher and more volatile.

**Regulatory exposure:** The FCC controls spectrum and can impose conditions on operations. Antitrust regulators scrutinize the industry because it is highly concentrated. Any future consolidation would be blocked. Price regulation is a perpetual risk.

**Technology refresh cycles:** Each generation of wireless (4G, 5G, eventually 6G) requires network upgrades and spectrum investment. Failure to keep pace means network quality lags competitors.

## How investors and analysts track the business

Start with the company's 10-K (SEC CIK 0001283699), which itemizes revenue by customer type, provides capex guidance, discusses spectrum licenses and their terms, and outlines risk factors.

Quarterly earnings are where the narrative unfolds: track postpaid net additions and churn versus competitors, ARPU trends, prepaid momentum, free cash flow, and management commentary on 5G deployment and market share. Compare T-Mobile's gross margins, operating margins, and return on capital to Verizon and AT&T to understand competitive positioning.

Monitor FCC spectrum auctions and licensing decisions. Follow announcements from Verizon and AT&T on pricing, network upgrades, and competitive moves. Watch for any regulatory proposal aimed at price controls or forced infrastructure sharing.

Consider macroeconomic conditions: device upgrade cycles correlate with consumer spending and tech-refresh momentum. Recessions historically hit prepaid harder than postpaid as customers trade down. Interest-rate cycles affect both capex funding costs and consumer ability to buy devices.

T-Mobile is a stable, mature cash-generative business competing in a three-player market where further consolidation is barred. The upside depends on incremental market share gains, home internet scale, and margin expansion. The downside risks are churn, pricing compression, and regulatory constraints.
