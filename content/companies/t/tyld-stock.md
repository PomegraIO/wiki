*TYLD is an exchange-traded fund managed by Cambria Investment Management that rotates capital among stocks, [bonds](/bond/), and alternative income-producing assets based on systematised signals, aiming to deliver higher [current yield](/current-yield/) than a traditional diversified portfolio while managing risk through [diversification](/diversification/) across asset classes.*

Cambria's approach to tactical allocation differs from traditional passive indexing. Rather than holding a static mix of assets — say, 60 percent stocks and 40 percent bonds — TYLD uses quantitative rules to decide how much of the portfolio to allocate to each asset class and category at any point. When the fund's signals suggest heightened equity valuations and lower expected returns, it may reduce equity exposure and increase income-producing alternatives. When bonds offer poor value, it may shift into equities with high [dividend yields](/dividend-yield/) or into real-estate securities and other income generators. The rules are systematic, not based on forecasting or judgment; they are applied consistently and transparently in the fund's prospectus.

## The tactical signals and rebalancing approach

TYLD uses multiple quantitative factors to guide allocation decisions: valuation metrics (how expensive or cheap different asset classes appear relative to history and to each other), momentum (recent price trends), and yield (the income available from different sources). The fund does not predict future returns; instead, it responds to changes in these observable metrics by rebalancing toward assets that currently offer better value.

For instance, if the fund's model determines that stocks are trading at historically elevated valuations while bond yields have risen sharply, the allocation tilts toward bonds and income-yielding alternatives. Conversely, if equities appear relatively cheap and [dividend](/dividend/) yields are elevated, the fund increases stock exposure. Within equities, TYLD favours high-dividend-paying stocks, which provide current income and have historically offered downside protection during market stress. Within fixed income, the fund may hold government bonds, [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), and other yield-bearing instruments depending on their relative attractiveness.

This is not a get-rich-quick approach; it is a disciplined effort to earn returns by avoiding overvalued assets and positioning toward areas of relative value. The rebalancing typically occurs quarterly or semi-annually, preventing the fund from chasing every short-term price movement.

## Income generation and what to expect

TYLD aims to deliver yields substantially above what investors would receive from a simple stock index. A broad stock index carries a dividend yield of roughly 1.5–2.0 percent; a TYLD targeting higher yields might aim for 3–5 percent or more, depending on market conditions. This higher yield comes from three sources: dividends on stock holdings, coupons and yields on bonds, and distributions from alternatives such as real-estate investment trusts, master limited partnerships, and [closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/) that pay out significant income.

For an income-focused investor, this appeal is clear: a portfolio yielding three percent provides more cash to live on or reinvest than one yielding two percent. But yield is not the same as return. A fund that pays a three percent yield but experiences a five percent capital loss in a given year delivers a negative total return. TYLD's tactical approach attempts to manage this by reducing exposure to overvalued assets before sharp declines, but no tactical system catches every drawdown.

## Structure, holdings, and diversification

The fund typically holds dozens of positions across equities (dividend-payers), bonds (Treasuries, corporates, emerging-market sovereigns), and alternatives. Real-estate investment trusts and master limited partnerships are often included because they are required to pay out large portions of their income to shareholders and often provide yields well above the broader market. The fund may also use leverage via swaps or derivatives to amplify yield without buying more underlying assets — a technique for extracting more income from a given capital base.

This diversification across asset classes and sectors is a genuine risk-mitigation tool. When stocks decline sharply, bonds typically provide some downside cushion; when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise and bond prices fall, dividend-paying stocks and alternatives may hold up better. That said, diversification works best when correlations between assets remain low — a condition that sometimes breaks down during severe market stress.

## Costs and tax consequences

TYLD's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically in the 0.50–0.70 percent range, moderate for an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) but higher than a passive stock [index fund](/index-fund/) (which charges 0.03–0.10 percent). Over decades, the compounding cost of an extra 0.50 percent annually is significant; an investor should satisfy themselves that the tactical allocation approach delivers value sufficient to justify the extra fee.

The yield also carries tax consequences. Income from dividends, bond coupons, and alternatives is taxable in the year it is received in non-retirement accounts; distributions of capital gains also trigger tax. For this reason, TYLD is best held in tax-deferred accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s) where the distributions can compound without annual tax drag. In a taxable account, the effective after-tax yield and return may be considerably lower than the stated distributions suggest.

## Real risks and limitations

The first risk is that tactical signals work well in some market environments and poorly in others. If equities enter a sustained [bull market](/bull-market/) despite elevated valuations, a tactical system that reduced equity exposure would lag. The fund's managers believe that their signals have worked historically — that disciplined rebalancing toward value has added value net of costs — but no systematic approach is foolproof.

A second risk is concentration and liquidity within alternatives. If the fund holds a large position in a closed-end fund, a master limited partnership, or an emerging-market bond, and market stress dries up demand for that security, TYLD may face difficulty managing its positions or face wide [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) when rebalancing. Alternatives often trade less actively than large-cap stocks or government bonds.

A third risk is the sustainability of yield sources. If dividend-paying companies cut dividends, or master limited partnerships reduce their distributions (which they sometimes do when commodity prices collapse), the fund's yield can fall sharply even if the fund's tactical signals remain unchanged. For an investor relying on the yield for income, such a cut can be painful.

## How a potential holder would research TYLD

Start with Cambria's website and fund fact sheet, which outline the specific signals and allocation rules, the expense ratio, and the fund's track record. Most important is to read the prospectus, which details the types of investments the fund can hold and any restrictions on leverage or single-position size.

Compare the fund's historical yield and total return against simpler alternatives: a diversified 60-40 portfolio of stocks and bonds, a dividend-focused stock ETF, or a broad [multi-asset ETF](/multi-asset-etf/). Has TYLD's extra complexity and higher expenses actually generated higher after-tax returns for long-term holders? If the fund's track record is less than five years, be cautious; tactical systems often shine in certain market regimes and lag in others, and a short track record may not capture a full cycle.

Finally, consider fit: TYLD is for an investor who values current income and believes that diversification and tactical allocation are worth paying for. It is less appropriate for a young investor with decades until retirement (who should accept more volatility and growth-focused exposure) or for someone seeking maximum returns on a long time horizon (where simpler, lower-cost index approaches have historically worked better). For its intended use — as a core income-generating holding for a retiree or pre-retiree — it deserves serious evaluation.
