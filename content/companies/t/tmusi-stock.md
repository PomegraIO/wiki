T-Mobile is a wireless carrier: it owns spectrum, operates towers, and sells monthly subscriptions for calls, texts, and data to over 100 million customers. Born from the 2013 [merger](/merger/) of T-Mobile and MetroPCS, and significantly enlarged by the 2020 [acquisition](/acquisition/) of Sprint, it sits as the third national player in a three-company market alongside Verizon and AT&T. The business is cyclical yet stable — tied to device upgrade patterns and economic conditions — but the company has navigated the past decade by emphasizing price and 5G, while carving share from competitors.

## The business in one sentence

T-Mobile sells wireless subscriptions and devices by operating a mobile network; its competitiveness hinges on spectrum depth, network quality, and the price point it holds versus Verizon and AT&T.

## Spectrum and infrastructure — what you own

Wireless carriers are utilities of a sort: they own the invisible — licenses from the FCC to transmit radio waves in specific frequency bands. T-Mobile's portfolio includes:

- **Low-band (600 MHz):** Excellent coverage, poor speed; critical for reaching rural and indoor areas. Largely acquired from Sprint.
- **Mid-band (n41):** The workhorse of modern 5G; good coverage and speed. T-Mobile holds significant n41 depth, a legacy of the Sprint merger.
- **High-band (millimetre-wave):** Extremely fast but short-range; used in dense urban areas where capacity is scarce.

The physical network — cell towers, fiber backhaul, switching centers — costs billions to build and maintain. T-Mobile owns some towers outright and leases others from independent tower companies. This capital intensity is non-negotiable; it defines the competitive moat. Once a network is built in a given area, a new competitor cannot easily replicate it.

## How revenue flows

Monthly service fees dominate — postpaid (contract subscribers), prepaid (pay-as-you-go), and business accounts. ARPU (average revenue per user) is a key metric; T-Mobile tracks it meticulously because even small moves matter across 100+ million customers. Device sales create lumpy revenue but high customer acquisition cost; the company subsidizes phones to win customers, then recoups the cost over 24–36 months of higher monthly fees.

Home internet, launched in 2021 using 5G to bypass wired providers, is strategic but still a small slice — competing directly with cable and fiber in select markets. It is also higher-margin than wireless and potential growth lever if 5G quality improves further. Tower leasing to other operators and roaming agreements with international carriers add margin.

## The strategic position: pricing, merger, technology

T-Mobile positioned itself as the disruptor, eliminating overage charges before others, pushing unlimited plans, and simplifying pricing. This gained market share from Verizon and AT&T and forced them to respond. The 2020 Sprint merger was transformative — it eliminated a fourth competitor, deepened T-Mobile's spectrum, and created a much larger company. Regulatory approval hinged on promises about network investment and job retention, which the company has met.

5G deployment is ongoing and capital-intensive. T-Mobile emerged with faster mid-band 5G than competitors initially, a genuine network advantage that has eroded somewhat as AT&T and Verizon caught up. The real competitive metric now is which carrier can sustain superior coverage and speed across rural and urban alike; T-Mobile's low-band depth helps it here.

## Pressures and hard constraints

**Churn:** Wireless is sticky but not locked. T-Mobile's postpaid churn (the rate customers leave monthly) is lower than prepaid or the industry average but is a constant competitive battleground. Any advantage in pricing or network quality can shift share quickly.

**Capex intensity:** Building and upgrading networks is expensive and endless. T-Mobile must outspend competitors or fall behind; there is no way to optimize away the need for investment.

**Pricing pressure:** The three-carrier market structure is stable but price competitive. New technology (5G, eventual 6G) is sold as a reason to upgrade, but subscribers often resist price increases. Regulators periodically propose price controls, especially for broadband.

**Regulatory and spectrum risk:** The FCC controls spectrum allocation through auctions and can impose conditions on mergers or operations. Any future expansion (acquisition) would face intense antitrust scrutiny. Spectrum licenses are time-limited; renewal is typically automatic but not guaranteed.

**Concentration:** With only three national carriers, antitrust regulators scrutinize the industry. Further consolidation is extremely unlikely to be approved.

## What to watch and where to look

Read T-Mobile's 10-K (CIK 0001283699) annually; it itemizes revenue by customer segment, capex plans, spectrum holdings, and all known risks.

Watch quarterly earnings for:
- Postpaid net additions and churn
- Prepaid trends (more volatile, lower-margin)
- ARPU trends — stable or eroding?
- [Free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) — is capex [crowding out](/crowding-out/) shareholder returns?
- 5G deployment progress relative to AT&T and Verizon

Follow FCC spectrum auctions and licensing decisions. Monitor competitive announcements from Verizon and AT&T on pricing, 5G, and home internet. Watch for any regulatory move toward price controls or forced unbundling. Evaluate macro conditions — device upgrade cycles are correlated to consumer spending; [recessions](/recession/) hit prepaid harder than postpaid.

T-Mobile is a mature, stable cash-flow business with genuine network advantages if its 5G lead is durable, but also a company where scale and regulatory approval are already maximized. The upside is incremental — margin expansion, market share gains, or secular growth in home internet. Downside risks are pricing compression, churn, and regulatory pressure.
