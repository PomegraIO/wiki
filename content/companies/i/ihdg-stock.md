*The **WisdomTree International Hedged Quality [Dividend](/dividend/) [Growth Fund](/growth-fund/)** (IHDG) is an exchange-traded fund that buys dividend-paying companies listed in developed countries outside the United States — Canada, Europe, Australia, Japan — with an explicit strategy to filter for higher-quality businesses and to hedge [currency risk](/currency-risk/) so that currency swings do not drive returns.*

## How WisdomTree built an alternative approach to dividend indexing

In the early 2000s, WisdomTree Investments began questioning the way dividend indexes were constructed. The traditional S&P or MSCI dividend-focused index would weight holdings by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) — meaning the largest companies got the largest allocations — and then screen for those that paid dividends. The result was that some of the largest, most mature, but slowest-growing companies dominated the index. WisdomTree argued there was a better way: weight the index by the actual amount of dividends paid, not by company size, and add a quality filter to emphasize companies with strong [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/) and histories of raising their payouts.

This reasoning led to the launch of WisdomTree's family of international dividend strategies, of which IHDG is one. The fund is designed for investors who want exposure to non-US developed markets — Europe, Japan, Canada, Australia — but do not want to be whipped around by currency moves (when the euro weakens against the dollar, unhedged foreign holdings lose value in dollar terms, even if the stock prices themselves are stable). IHDG solves this by hedging currency: the fund uses currency forwards to lock in a fixed dollar value of its foreign holdings, so performance reflects the underlying stocks, not the exchange rate.

## The strategy today

IHDG filters the developed-market universe (roughly 15,000 companies across the EAFE region) down to those paying dividends, then applies a quality screen that emphasizes profitable, lower-leverage companies with stable earnings and a track record of raising dividends over time. The resulting index typically holds 300–400 companies across roughly 20 developed countries.

The fund rebalances once per year, and the weighting scheme is based on the dividend payments themselves. This has two effects: it tilts the portfolio toward companies with high and stable payouts, and it avoids the trap of following momentum into expensive high-growth names that have stopped paying dividends. A company that has raised its dividend 15 years in a row gets heavier weight than a newly added dividend payer.

The currency hedge is mechanical: WisdomTree uses currency-forward contracts to neutralize moves in the euro, the pound, the yen, and the [Australian dollar](/australian-dollar/) relative to the [US dollar](/us-dollar/). This adds a small cost (the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) on the forwards, a handful of basis points per year), but it means an investor in IHDG is not implicitly making a bet on currency movements — pure play on the stocks themselves.

## Why this structure appeals to investors

IHDG serves several different investor needs. For US-based investors who want non-US [diversification](/diversification/) but are uncomfortable with [currency volatility](/currency-volatility/), the hedge is essential — it lets them own international stocks without worrying that a sharp dollar rally will erase gains. For dividend-focused investors, the quality filter and dividend-weighting scheme offer a different entry point than market-cap-weighted dividend indexes — you own the companies actually paying the money, not just the biggest companies that happen to pay dividends.

For long-term holders, dividend strategies can be attractive because they emphasize companies with reliable earnings and a commitment to returning cash to shareholders. The international angle adds geographic diversification without the [execution risk](/execution-risk/) of trying to pick stocks across dozens of countries.

## The underlying tensions

The strategy sits at the intersection of three different investment preferences — international exposure, dividend income, and quality — which sometimes pull in different directions. A company that is raising dividends fast is attractive by IHDG's metrics, but it might be doing so by borrowing heavily or deferring reinvestment, which can hurt long-term growth. The quality filter helps screen this out, but it is not foolproof.

[Currency hedging](/currency-hedging/) is also a two-edged sword. It eliminates the risk that a strong dollar crushes returns, but it also eliminates the upside if a weak dollar helps international stocks outperform. In periods when the dollar is falling, unhedged international funds will outperform hedged ones — so investors choosing IHDG are implicitly neutral on dollar direction, or slightly bullish on the dollar, which may not match their actual view.

The dividend-weighting scheme favors mature, cash-generative businesses over faster-growing companies that reinvest profits. This has helped IHDG outperform in low-growth periods but has cost it in sustained [bull markets](/bull-market/) where growth matters more. An investor in the fund should expect it to lag broad developed-market indexes during strong economic expansions.

## How to research IHDG

The prospectus and fact sheet on WisdomTree's website lay out the fund's exact methodology, the list of holdings, and the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/). The index prospectus (available separately) details the construction rules — which countries are eligible, what the quality criteria are, how the dividend weighting works, and the rebalancing schedule.

An investor considering IHDG should ask: Do I expect the dollar to strengthen or weaken over my [holding period](/holding-period/)? (If I expect weakness, unhedged international is better; if strength, hedged makes sense.) Am I comfortable with the historical return patterns of dividend-focused strategies — strong in low-growth periods, sometimes lagging in bull markets? And do the companies in the fund actually reflect my view of what's attractive in developed international markets, or would I prefer broader market-cap-weighted exposure?

The fund's performance relative to the EAFE index (the broader developed-market ex-US benchmark) and relative to unhedged dividend alternatives offers useful context for understanding what IHDG is adding or subtracting through its strategy choices.
