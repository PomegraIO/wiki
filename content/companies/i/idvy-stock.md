## What is IDVY trying to do?

IDVY holds international companies that have raised their [dividends](/dividend/) for a minimum number of consecutive years, typically five or more. The fund manager selects from this universe based on judgment about which dividend growers will outperform. The thesis is straightforward: companies that have the discipline and the cash generation to raise dividends consistently tend to be well-run, mature, and durable. They are unlikely to be distressed or heavily overleveraged. Their management prefers returning capital to shareholders now rather than betting everything on future growth.

## Why focus on dividend growers rather than just high yielders?

A company paying a very high [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) might be a value opportunity — or it might be a dividend trap, where the payout is unsustainable and likely to be cut. A company that has raised its dividend for five consecutive years, on the other hand, has demonstrated the ability to sustain and grow its cash return to shareholders through [business cycles](/business-cycle/). It signals management confidence and financial stability. Rising dividends also correlate with business improvement and earnings growth. The dividend-growth screen is a filtered approach to finding quality businesses rather than chasing the highest [current yield](/current-yield/).

## Why international instead of US dividend growers?

Several reasons. First, mature international companies often trade at lower valuations than US peers with similar qualities, partly because they face slower growth prospects and less analyst coverage. Second, international [diversification](/diversification/) reduces home-country concentration. Third, international dividend payers — particularly Canadian, UK, Swiss, and Australian companies — have strong track records of consistent, rising payouts and tend to operate in cash-generative sectors like energy, utilities, financials, and consumer staples. The international universe offers exposure to businesses US investors may overlook.

## How does active management make a difference here?

The fund manager does not simply hold every company that meets the dividend-growth criteria. Instead, the manager forms views about which dividend growers will accelerate earnings or expand payouts further, and which might be at risk of cuts or slowdowns. The manager overweights companies they believe have runway for dividend acceleration and underweights those that appear mature or threatened. This discretion can enhance returns if the manager's judgment is sound, but it also introduces manager risk: if the manager's bets go wrong, returns will lag a simple passive dividend-growth index. The manager is being paid (via the higher [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/)) to add value through stock selection, and that is a bet on the manager's skill.

## What are the structural costs?

IDVY charges an expense ratio well above passive funds, typically 0.6% to 0.8% or higher, reflecting active management. This fee drag is meaningful over long [holding periods](/holding-period/). For the fund to justify its fee, the manager's stock picks must outperform a passive dividend-growth alternative by more than the fee cost. This is historically a difficult hurdle to clear; most active managers underperform their passive benchmarks after fees over 10-year periods.

The fund also has higher turnover than a passive index, resulting in more capital-gains distributions and potential tax friction. Investors in high-income tax brackets may want to hold IDVY in tax-deferred accounts.

## What are the key risks?

Dividend cuts are the most obvious risk. Even companies with long histories of raising dividends can be forced to freeze or reduce payouts if earnings collapse, [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/) deteriorate, or macro shocks hit unexpectedly. The 2008 financial crisis saw dividend cuts at banks and other dividend stalwarts; a severe [recession](/recession/) could trigger similar pain. When a dividend is cut, the stock typically declines, creating double damage — both lost income and capital loss.

[Currency risk](/currency-risk/) is substantial. IDVY's returns depend not only on stock price movements but also on exchange rates. A strengthening [US dollar](/us-dollar/) reduces returns on foreign-denominated holdings regardless of underlying business performance. Conversely, a weakening dollar magnifies gains. Investors are implicitly taking a currency bet alongside the equity bet.

International geopolitical and regulatory risks matter too. European companies operate in tighter labor-law and tax environments that can constrain returns. Emerging-market holdings carry political instability, currency controls, and expropriation risk. Tax treatment of dividends varies by country, and there can be withholding taxes that reduce after-tax returns for US-based investors.

## Who should consider IDVY?

IDVY appeals to international-minded income investors seeking diversification beyond US stocks and wanting the discipline of a dividend-growth screen to filter for quality. It works for investors with moderate risk tolerance who can tolerate stock-market volatility but prefer companies that are distributing cash now rather than plowing everything back into growth.

It is less suitable for pure growth investors, for conservative investors uncomfortable with equity volatility, for investors who already have heavy international exposure, or for those skeptical of active management's ability to outperform passive alternatives. The active fee is a headwind, so conviction should extend to the manager's skill.

## How to research and monitor IDVY

Read the fund's fact sheet and recent holdings list to see which companies dominate the portfolio. Look up several of the largest holdings and verify their dividend history independently. Read recent fund letters from the manager to understand their current investment thesis — which regions they favor, which sectors they see as having sustainable dividend growth, and how they are positioning relative to macro conditions.

Compare IDVY's total returns to a passive international dividend-growth index or to a simple broad international equity index to see if the manager is genuinely adding value net of fees. Monitor the fund's distribution yield and the growth in distributions year-over-year: if distributions are accelerating, the underlying holdings are raising payouts. If distributions are flat or declining, it signals either that companies are cutting dividends or that the fund's positions have rotated toward lower payers.

Track currency movements, especially the US dollar versus the euro and [British pound](/british-pound/), since these will have material effects on returns. Finally, understand that dividend-growth screens perform best in periods of economic stability and expansion; in severe recessions or depressions, even the most carefully screened dividend payers often disappoint their investors.
