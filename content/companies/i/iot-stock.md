Samsara is an Internet of Things software company that uses sensors, cameras, and cloud computing to give large fleets and warehouses real-time visibility into their operations and to automate routine decisions that reduce accidents, fuel waste, and downtime. The company was founded in 2015 and went public in 2022. Its customers are logistics networks, waste collection companies, food and beverage distributors, construction firms, and other enterprises with thousands of vehicles or pieces of equipment that need centralised oversight.

## The physical-world data problem

Most large enterprises have a simple but intractable problem: they own thousands of trucks, trailers, or warehouse assets spread across a wide geography, and the data about what those assets are doing — where they are, whether they are moving, what condition they are in — lives in silos. One division has GPS tracking; another has a safety and maintenance log; a third has driver and payload records. No single source of truth exists, and so operations people make decisions on incomplete information: a maintenance team does not learn that a bearing has begun to wear until the asset has already broken down on a job; a fleet manager cannot tell whether a driver is taking dangerous shortcuts; a warehouse manager has no visibility into why goods are not flowing through the facility on schedule.

Samsara's fundamental insight is that connecting all these data sources in real time and feeding that information through algorithmic analytics creates value at every level — the driver level (instant feedback on harsh braking or speeding), the vehicle level (predictive maintenance alerts), and the fleet level (optimised routing, proof of compliance for regulators, reduced insurance claims). The company supplies the cameras and sensors (installed in vehicles, on assets, or around warehouses), maintains the cloud platform that ingests and processes the data, and presents the analytics to customers through web and mobile apps.

## How Samsara makes money

The business operates on a subscription model. Customers pay per vehicle, per trailer, or per asset tracked, typically at a rate in the low triple digits of dollars per unit per year. Implementation and onboarding carry upfront fees, but the bulk of revenue is recurring: once a customer has equipped its fleet or warehouse, the sensors and software stay in place for years, and the customer pays month after month to retain access and the alerting.

This structure has two strategic advantages. First, it creates strong retention: a company that has outfitted 1,000 vehicles with Samsara cameras and integrated the alerts into its dispatch system faces substantial switching costs, because ripping out the hardware and reconfiguring operations for a competitor is expensive and disruptive. Second, it incentivises Samsara to keep deepening its analytics and integrations so that customers derive more value and are less inclined to look elsewhere.

The largest customer verticals are long-haul trucking and logistics, waste collection, and retail and restaurant supply chains. These are industries where fleet size is large (hundreds or thousands of vehicles), drivers are distributed across a wide geography, safety and compliance are heavily regulated, and accidents or downtime carry real business costs. In these environments, a data platform that prevents accidents, reduces fuel waste, or flags maintenance issues before they become catastrophic has a clear, measurable return on investment.

## Competitive pressures and moats

The space is not empty. Established telematics companies — including Geotab, Verizon Connect (acquired by Verizon from the now-defunct Fleetmatics), and a host of regional players — have long-term customer relationships and brand awareness in specific verticals. Samsara competes partly on superior product (its camera and sensor suite is often considered more comprehensive), partly on ease of use and modern cloud architecture (as opposed to older on-premise or hybrid systems), and partly on the breadth of use cases — it has worked to expand beyond simple tracking into driver coaching, maintenance scheduling, and warehouse analytics.

A real advantage Samsara holds is the closed loop between hardware and software: because the company controls both the sensors and the platform, it can optimise them together in ways a pure-software competitor cannot easily match. The installed base — growing steadily — also creates network effects of a sort: as more competitors use the same platform, Samsara can benchmark them against each other, refine its analytics, and potentially offer marketplace services (insurance partnerships, parts and fuel vendors) that create additional stickiness.

## The challenges ahead

IoT hardware-plus-software businesses face two chronic headaches. The first is the cost and complexity of maintaining a hardware fleet: sensors fail, breakage happens, and customer support scales with the installed base. The second is that the business is capital-intensive relative to pure software: developing sensors, certifying them for use in vehicles, managing supply chains, and shipping physical goods requires significant engineering and operational infrastructure.

Samsara also faces cyclical pressures. In recessions, companies cut capital spending on telematics; in boom times they invest. A slowdown in logistics and construction would reduce customer acquisition. And though switching costs are real, they are not infinite — a customer sufficiently frustrated with price, features, or service can eventually justify the effort to migrate.

## How to research Samsara

Investors interested in Samsara should begin with the company's 10-K (SEC CIK 0001642896), which details revenue by customer segment and geography, explains the subscription economics and dollar-based net retention rate, and lists the competitive and operational risks. The quarterly earnings calls reveal customer acquisition costs, churn, and the product roadmap.

Key metrics to follow: the growth rate of dollar-based net retention (a measure of how much larger Samsara can make existing customers' spending over time), the rate at which new customers are acquired, and the gross margin trend (indicating whether the hardware and platform are becoming more profitable at scale). The company also discloses its subscription backlog, which signals customer confidence in future value. Watch for expansion into adjacent verticals or new geographies, which would be the most obvious next phase of growth.
