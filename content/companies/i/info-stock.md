*The **Harbor PanAgora Dynamic Large Cap Core ETF** ([NASDAQ](/nasdaq/): INFO) is an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) that holds large-cap U.S. equities — typically 50 to 100 holdings drawn from the roughly 500 largest companies — and uses quantitative models and fundamental research to identify stocks trading below their [intrinsic value](/intrinsic-value/). It competes in the crowded large-cap space not by passive indexing but by trying to find genuine mispricings that active managers can exploit, a harder sell in an era when most stock investors have switched to trackers of broad indexes.*

## The active-management case in large-cap

INFO sits at a strategic crossroads in modern finance. Passive large-cap [index funds](/index-fund/) — which simply buy the 500 largest U.S. companies and hold them in market-weight proportion — have become the lowest-cost, lowest-friction way for most investors to own U.S. equities. Their dominance is real and justified for buy-and-hold portfolios. Yet the existence of that passive default does not mean active managers cannot add value by being more selective. Information exists; not every stock in the S&P 500 is correctly priced at any given moment. A fund that can identify the cheaper, better-positioned companies and overweight them while shedding the expensive ones can, in principle, outperform the index by enough to pay back its higher fee and deliver net gains.

Harbor PanAgora makes that bet with a two-layer approach. The quantitative component uses data and statistical models to screen the large-cap universe for value characteristics — companies showing attractive [price-to-earnings ratios](/price-to-earnings-ratio/), high [dividend yields](/dividend-yield/), and other backward-looking signals of undervaluation. The fundamental layer brings human analysts into the fold, who dive deeper into the companies the models flag and make final buy-or-sell calls based on deeper business knowledge. The result is a hybrid strategy: systematic enough to be repeatable and scale efficiently, yet retaining the discretion that human judgment can provide.

## How the fund is structured and what it holds

INFO typically maintains a portfolio of 50 to 100 stocks, each chosen from the universe of large-cap companies. The fund does not attempt to hold a representative slice of the entire S&P 500; instead, it is concentrated in what the managers believe are the highest-conviction opportunities. A stock might be overweighted because the quantitative models signal it is cheap and the fundamentals team agrees the market has overlooked genuine business strength. Another might be excluded entirely because the same process concluded it was overvalued.

This selectivity is the core driver of the fund's hope to outperform. It also introduces the central risk: if the managers are wrong about which stocks are mispriced, concentration will hurt rather than help. A portfolio of 100 hand-picked stocks is far more fragile than a portfolio of 500, and if the selection process is flawed, the fund will lag. Fee drag matters too — active management does not come free, and the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) must be paid out of any outperformance to deliver value to shareholders. This is the eternal tension: active management can work only if the skill of the active managers exceeds the cost of the active management.

## The supply and demand angle

From a supply-chain perspective, INFO sits at an interesting point. On the upstream side, it depends on the liquidity and diversity of large-cap U.S. equities — the willingness of investors and traders to continuously trade these stocks, providing the fund with the ability to rebalance, buy, and sell at reasonable cost. The better the liquidity in the 500 largest U.S. companies, the easier it is for Harbor's traders to execute the managers' ideas.

Downstream, the fund serves a specific class of investor: typically financial advisers or individuals who believe that active management can add value, who are willing to pay higher fees in exchange for the manager's skill in stock picking. This is a shrinking market in aggregate — the share of assets in passive funds has been rising steadily — but it persists among investors who retain skepticism about the efficiency of markets or who value the risk-management overlay and tactical flexibility that active managers offer.

## How to research and evaluate INFO

An investor considering INFO should start with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, available through Harbor or the fund's official documentation. The prospectus explains the strategy in full and discloses the expense ratio, which is the annual cost of ownership. The fact sheet lists the current top holdings, giving a sense of what the portfolio looks like at any moment.

The harder question — one the prospectus cannot answer — is whether the active management will actually outperform. Checking historical returns against a passively managed large-cap benchmark like the S&P 500 offers one lens, but past performance is not guaranteed to repeat. The skill of the analysts, the turnover rate (how often positions change), and the fee level all affect the probability that outperformance will persist. The quantitative strategy is documented in marketing materials and research papers; a deep dive into that methodology helps assess whether the approach is sensible or built on assumptions that may not hold under different market conditions. As with any fund, the governing documents, the fund sponsor's track record, and the fund's risk factors matter far more than any single year's returns.
