*The **iShares Global Infrastructure ETF (IGF)** is an exchange-traded fund that gives investors exposure to infrastructure operators and utilities around the world—the businesses that build and run the physical systems societies depend on. Unlike equities in pure-play technology or consumer companies, infrastructure assets tend to throw off reliable cash flows, operate with regulated or contracted revenue streams, and appeal to investors seeking income and stability alongside capital appreciation.*

## What the fund holds and tracks

IGF tracks the [S&P Global](/spgi-stock/) Infrastructure Index, a broad, float-adjusted index of around 100–150 companies engaged in infrastructure ownership and operation across developed and emerging economies. The fund weights holdings by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/), so larger and more liquid businesses dominate the portfolio. The index spans three broad categories: utilities (electric, water, gas), energy infrastructure (pipelines, storage, transmission), and transport infrastructure (toll roads, airports, railroads, ports). Sector weighting shifts with market values, but utilities typically anchor the largest chunk, followed by energy infrastructure and then transport and other segments.

Geographically, the fund maintains exposure to North America, Europe, Australia, and a smaller allocation to Asian and emerging markets. This regional diversity reflects where infrastructure investments are most tradable and regulated. The index rebalances and reconstitutes periodically to stay aligned with the universe of eligible companies.

## Why infrastructure matters as an investment

Infrastructure sits at an unusual nexus in the equity market. Unlike technology stocks, which compete on innovation and growth rates, or consumer discretionary businesses, which thrive on brand and expansion, infrastructure companies earn stable, often contracted or regulated returns on assets in place. An airport operator, a water utility, a transmission-line owner—these do not typically chase market share through price wars. Their revenue comes from fees, tariffs, or regulatory allowances set in relation to the capital they have invested. That stability attracts institutional investors hunting for yield and makes infrastructure a defensive equity holding during economic slowdowns.

The flip side is that infrastructure growth depends on population growth, urbanization, and capital spending decisions made by governments and large corporations. Climate policy, energy transition, and grid modernization create tailwinds for some infrastructure segments (renewable-energy transmission, water treatment) and headwinds for others (fossil-fuel pipelines in some jurisdictions). These dynamics shift slowly but persistently, making infrastructure sectors neither pure-play growth nor pure-play value, but something between—a barbell of steady cashflow plus long-term structural themes.

## The fund structure and costs

IGF is a traditional open-end ETF domiciled in the United States, trading on NYSE Arca under the ticker IGF. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is modest, in the range typical of broad iShares funds—low double-digit basis points—making it a low-friction way to gain infrastructure exposure. The fund is highly liquid, with substantial trading volume and tight [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/), so large investors can move in and out without moving prices.

[Dividends](/dividend/) arrive regularly, reflecting the income orientation of infrastructure holdings. Most infrastructure companies maintain or grow their cash dividends year after year, so IGF shareholders receive distributions several times a year. These are reinvestable through brokerage accounts and often flow into the fund's automatic [dividend reinvestment plans](/dividend-reinvestment-plan/) (DRIPs).

## Risks and concentration

Infrastructure funds inherit risks from their underlying holdings. Regulatory risk is the most fundamental: toll roads, utilities, and transmission operators live inside regulatory frameworks that can shift. A change in allowed returns, new environmental rules, or political pressure can reduce revenue or raise costs. Geopolitical risk affects energy infrastructure especially, as pipelines and cross-border assets become tools in state power plays. [Currency risk](/currency-risk/) is material for US investors holding non-US infrastructure; a strong dollar erodes returns from foreign holdings.

[Concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) merits attention. While the index is broad, the largest holdings—multinational utilities and energy-infrastructure giants—can comprise 10–15% each of the portfolio. A major scandal or operational failure at a top holding ripples through returns. Interest-rate sensitivity is another wrinkle: infrastructure stocks often trade more like [bonds](/bond/) when rates move sharply, because investors reprice the value of stable, long-term cash flows relative to risk-free alternatives.

Economic slowdowns also matter, though in a different way than for cyclical stocks. A [recession](/recession/) may dampen demand for electricity or transport, squeezing some revenue lines; but infrastructure also provides defensive characteristics because essential services—water, electricity—remain in demand even in downturns.

## Who this fund serves

IGF appeals to investors seeking equity-like returns from assets with bond-like stability. Retirees or near-retirees often favor infrastructure funds for income and capital preservation. Investors in developed-world portfolios use infrastructure as a non-correlated diversifier to stocks and bonds. Long-term holders appreciate the secular tailwinds from urbanization and energy transition. Conversely, the fund is less suitable for growth-hungry investors or those with high marginal tax rates (dividend income is taxed as ordinary income), and short-term traders find the slow-moving nature of infrastructure less rewarding.

## How to research and evaluate IGF

Start with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet on BlackRock's iShares website, which lay out holdings, sector breakdowns, and expense details plainly. Compare the top 10 holdings and sector weights against peer infrastructure funds, such as those tracking the Brookfield Global Infrastructure Index, to understand the differences in philosophy and coverage.

Examine the underlying S&P Global Infrastructure Index's methodology and constituents through S&P's documentation. Track how the fund's returns align with the benchmark and whether tracking error is negligible (as it should be for a passive fund). Watch the [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) relative to the dividend yield of the overall equity market to gauge whether infrastructure is attracting capital or losing appeal. And for geopolitical and regulatory color, read industry publications tracking utility regulation, energy policy, and toll-road concessions to understand structural pressures building in the fund's core holdings.
