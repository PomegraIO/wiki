*International Seaways owns and operates oceangoing tanker vessels that move [crude oil](/crude-oil/) and refined petroleum products from refineries, storage terminals, and production facilities across the world's oceans. The company competes in the global shipping market as a smaller but disciplined player in a capital-intensive, cyclical industry shaped by oil demand, refining margins, trade routes, and the supply and demand for vessel capacity itself.*

## Tanker Shipping: A Window into Global Energy and Trade

The tanker industry moves petroleum around the world—crude oil from producing regions (the Middle East, West Africa, Russia, the North Sea) to refineries, and refined products (gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, [heating oil](/heating-oil/)) from refineries to distribution points, consuming nations, and eventually end users. It is an essential infrastructure, largely invisible to consumers, that underpins global energy security and economic activity.

The industry is structured by vessel class: ultra-large crude carriers (ULCCs), very-large crude carriers (VLCCs), supertankers, Aframaxes, and smaller product tankers. Each class serves specific routes, cargo types, and economic niches. A VLCC might haul crude from the Persian Gulf to refineries in Asia or Europe on multi-week transits; a smaller product tanker might move refined fuels on shorter regional routes. International Seaways operates a diversified fleet across multiple classes and cargo types, balancing exposure to different route economics and demand patterns.

## Fleet Composition and Market Positioning

International Seaways' competitive position rests on its fleet composition, operating costs, management expertise, and access to charters and long-term contract counterparties. Larger tanker owners benefit from economies of scale in maintenance, crew management, and insurance, while smaller, more nimble operators can compete on flexibility and willingness to serve less-profitable but reliable regional routes. International Seaways positions itself as a mid-sized owner with sufficient scale to manage operational complexity but with the agility to pivot vessel deployments as market conditions shift.

The company owns its vessels, rather than operating on pure charter-in, charter-out models. This ownership creates capital intensity and exposes the business to the full cycle of shipping economics. When spot rates for tanker freight are high—driven by scarcity of capacity, geopolitical disruptions, or surges in global petroleum demand—the company earns exceptional returns on its capital. When rates collapse—during oversupply, demand weakness, or economic slowdown—profitability can evaporate, and the company must manage through a downturn with high fixed costs.

## The Tanker Rate Cycle and Earnings Volatility

Tanker shipping is notoriously cyclical. Freight rates are set in spot markets and term contracts, moving daily based on the balance of available cargo and available vessels. A geopolitical disruption that closes key shipping routes suddenly (such as Suez Canal congestion or sanctions on Russian oil exports) can double tanker rates overnight. Conversely, new vessel deliveries that add capacity to the global fleet can depress rates for extended periods.

International Seaways' earnings are thus highly variable quarter to quarter and year to year. Investors must accept wide swings in profitability as a fact of the business. During peaks, the company can generate exceptional cash returns; during troughs, it may report minimal profits or losses. This volatility makes the company difficult for risk-averse investors but attractive to those who can tolerate or speculate on the cycle.

## Operating Leverage and Capital Deployment

Tanker ownership is capital-intensive. A modern VLCC costs over $100 million to build; supertankers and newer vessels cost more. The company must continuously invest in vessel [acquisition](/acquisition/) (whether through newbuilds or secondhand purchases), as older vessels become obsolete due to environmental regulations, efficiency requirements, and age. Financing these purchases typically involves a mix of [equity](/common-stock/), [debt](/corporate-bond/), and [retained earnings](/retained-earnings/).

The company also faces material operating costs: crew wages, fuel, insurance, port fees, maintenance, and dry-docking (periodic hull inspection and maintenance). Fuel costs in particular are substantial and volatile, exposing the company to global oil-price moves. Some contracts pass fuel costs through to charterers; others do not, creating profit variability.

## International Regulatory Environment and Environmental Compliance

Tanker shipping is heavily regulated. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) sets standards for vessel safety, pollution prevention, and crew welfare. Major recent regulations include the IMO 2020 sulfur cap, which required vessels to use low-sulfur fuel or install scrubber systems to reduce emissions. These regulations increase operating costs, favor newer, more efficient vessels, and disadvantage older fleets.

International Seaways must navigate these regulations through fleet renewal, technology investment, and compliance spending. Environmental regulations are expected to tighten further (including potential carbon caps and zero-emission mandates), creating medium-term uncertainty about the viability of older vessels and the marginal economics of the shipping business.

## Geopolitics, Trade Routes, and Structural Shifts

Tanker shipping is sensitive to geopolitical disruptions and trade patterns. Sanctions on Russian oil, for example, have rerouted crude flows and altered the supply-demand balance for tanker services. Discussions about energy transition and reduced global petroleum demand create long-term structural headwinds: if electric vehicles and renewables reduce oil consumption, tanker demand may decline over decades.

International Seaways must balance near-term cyclical dynamics against potential long-term demand erosion. The company has made strategic bets on which cargo types and routes will remain viable as the energy transition unfolds. [Diversification](/diversification/) into renewable products transport (such as biofuels) or other liquid cargoes (chemicals, liquefied [natural gas](/natural-gas/)) offers some hedging, but the core business remains hydrocarbon transportation.

## Charter Relationships and Commercial Acumen

International Seaways competes for contracts with major oil majors, refiners, and trading houses. Long-term contracts provide revenue stability but often at fixed or formula-based rates that don't capture spot-market peaks. Spot charters offer upside during booms but leave the company exposed to downturns. The company's commercial team must balance these tradeoffs continuously.

Success requires not just owning vessels but actively managing their employment—where each ship will go, what cargo it will carry, at what rate, and on what terms. Companies with superior commercial intelligence and relationships capture better contracts and time deployments more effectively. Conversely, poor execution can leave vessels idle or poorly utilized during soft markets.
