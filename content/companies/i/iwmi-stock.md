*The **NEOS Russell 2000 High [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (ticker IWMI) screens the Russell 2000 universe for companies with above-average [dividend yields](/dividend-yield/), creating a portfolio weighted toward the highest-yielding small-cap stocks. Unlike a broad-market index that holds all 2,000 stocks equally weighted by size, IWMI applies a dividend-yield filter that concentrates the fund in a subset of those 2,000 stocks — typically two to three hundred — that pay the most attractive yields.*

The core insight behind IWMI is simple: not all small-cap stocks pay [dividends](/dividend/), and those that do vary wildly in yield. Some small-cap REITs, limited partnerships, and mature industrial companies throw off 5%, 7%, or even higher yields. Others in the small-cap universe pay nothing at all — growth companies reinvest all earnings rather than distribute them. By filtering explicitly for yield, IWMI concentrates in the subset of the Russell 2000 that actually distributes cash to shareholders.

This focus creates a specific sector tilt. [Real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/) — which are required by law to distribute most of their taxable income — will be overweight in IWMI. Master limited partnerships, energy companies with stable cash flows, some utilities (though most utilities are larger), and mature industrial or financial firms with established dividend histories will also be overrepresented. The technology, healthcare, and consumer-discretionary sectors — which rarely pay meaningful dividends in the small-cap space — will be underweight or absent entirely. For an investor who wanted broad small-cap exposure, this tilt would be a drawback; for an investor who explicitly wants income, it is the entire point.

The yield on IWMI is consequently meaningfully higher than the yield on a broad small-cap index like IWM. A broad small-cap index might yield around 1% or 2%, depending on the [market cycle](/market-cycle/). IWMI, concentrated in the small-caps that actually pay dividends, typically yields 3% to 5% or more. That differential is not a free lunch — it comes at the cost of sector concentration and the exclusion of the fast-growing, non-dividend-paying small-caps that have historically driven much of small-cap returns. An investor who owns IWMI is betting that the higher current income is worth the risk of underperformance versus a broad small-cap index during periods when non-dividend-paying growth stocks run.

The tax implications of IWMI matter for taxable accounts. Because REITs and MLPs distribute a large portion of their income as dividends (and MLP distributions often have different tax character — some qualified, some ordinary income), IWMI will throw off more taxable distributions annually than a broad [index fund](/index-fund/) would. In a tax-advantaged account like an IRA or 401(k), this is irrelevant. In a taxable brokerage account, the higher yield comes with higher annual tax bills. An investor considering IWMI in a taxable account should model the after-tax return, not just the pre-tax yield.

The dividend sustainability question is also worth examining. Yields are always at risk — a company paying a 6% yield might slash its dividend if business deteriorates. Small-cap companies are generally more economically sensitive than large-caps, so in a [recession](/recession/) or sharp downturn, dividend cuts can ripple through the fund quickly. Some of IWMI's larger contributors — REITs and energy partnerships — are particularly vulnerable to economic shocks. Investors who are counting on the high yield to supplement their income should stress-test their assumptions about dividend stability and be prepared for the possibility of cuts.

How to research IWMI: Start with the fund's fact sheet, which lists the current holdings and sector allocation. Note the concentration in REITs, MLPs, and energy stocks — that sector tilt is the engine of IWMI's yield. Compare IWMI's [current yield](/current-yield/) to the yield of a broad small-cap index like IWM to understand the yield premium you are buying. Examine IWMI's distribution history — when it pays, how much, and whether those payments have been stable or volatile. Look at trailing-period returns relative to the Russell 2000 to see whether the yield advantage has translated to competitive total returns or whether the sector concentration has detracted from performance. Finally, consider your tax situation — if you hold it in a taxable account, factor the high annual distributions into your after-tax return expectations.
