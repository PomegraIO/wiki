*The **iShares 5-10 Year Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (IGIB)** holds bonds issued by established, credit-worthy corporations with maturities between five and ten years—a slice of the bond market that sits between the stability of shorter-dated debt and the higher yields (and price volatility) of longer bonds. For investors seeking regular income from corporate debt without the duration risk of longer-dated portfolios, IGIB offers a simple, diversified basket of these mid-maturity securities.*

The fund tracks the Bloomberg U.S. 5-10 Year Corporate Bond Index, a broad float-adjusted index that captures the universe of investment-grade corporate debt in that maturity band. Investment-grade means the bonds are rated BBB- or higher by at least one of the major rating agencies (Standard & Poor's, Moody's, Fitch), representing companies that have demonstrated the capacity and willingness to meet their debt obligations. The index includes issues from financial services, technology, industrials, utilities, consumer staples, and other sectors, with weighting reflecting the outstanding value of each bond issue.

The five-to-ten year bucket is strategically positioned within the credit landscape. Shorter bonds (one to five years) carry less interest-rate risk but offer lower yields, often tracking close to what an investor could earn in short-term corporate loans or floating-rate notes. Longer bonds (10+ years) offer higher yields but swing more in price when interest rates move, a characteristic called duration risk. The 5-10 year sweet spot delivers moderate income with moderate interest-rate sensitivity—enough yield to matter, enough stability to sleep on most nights.

IGIB's composition shifts with market conditions. When credit spreads (the additional yield demanded for corporate bonds above risk-free government bonds) widen, corporate bonds become cheaper relative to Treasuries, and the fund's valuations adjust downward along with the market. When spreads tighten, the opposite happens. The fund adjusts holdings to track the index, meaning as bonds age into shorter maturities, they are sold and replaced with newer issues in the target range.

The fund's dividend is monthly, paid from the coupon income the underlying bonds generate. The yield on the fund fluctuates with prevailing interest rates and credit conditions but generally hovers in a range that reflects the bonds' average maturity and the credit quality of the issuers. Higher-rated issuers (AAA and AA) dominate the portfolio by count, though concentration in the largest issuers is typically modest—no single company's debt usually exceeds 3-4% of the fund's assets.

For an investor, the key distinction IGIB offers versus a Treasury-only or money-market fund is corporate-credit exposure without concentration in a single borrower. The corporate spread—the extra yield over a similar-maturity Treasury—compensates investors for credit risk: the possibility that a company hits financial trouble and cannot pay in full. Historically, investment-grade defaults have been rare, but they do happen, especially during recessions. A single default or downgrade in the portfolio touches returns, but the diversification of IGIB means no single issuer can crater the fund.

Interest-rate risk is the more tangible daily force. When the Federal Reserve raises rates or inflation concerns spike, investors demand higher yields to own existing bonds, pushing prices down. A 1% rise in yield typically translates to a 4-5% loss in the value of a 5-10 year bond portfolio, a straightforward duration math. Conversely, rate declines drive capital appreciation. Over longer holding periods, the monthly coupon income gradually offsets price volatility.

IGIB suits conservative equity investors looking to diversify into income-producing assets, retirees seeking monthly cash flow from their portfolio, and bond-focused investors who want to sidestep the complexity of stock-picking without accepting zero yield. The fund is also a tool for tactical tilt into corporate credit when spreads look attractive (overly wide) or away from credit when spreads have compressed and seem rich (too tight for the risk). For trading-oriented investors, the fund's high liquidity and tight spreads on most corporate bonds mean entry and exit costs are low. Passive investors benefit from the low expense ratio and the fact that passive index tracking removes the bet-taking and manager risk of actively managed corporate bond funds.

To evaluate IGIB, begin with the prospectus and fact sheets available through BlackRock's iShares website, which detail the index's methodology, the fund's current holdings, and sector and issuer breakdowns. Compare the fund's yield and duration to peer 5-10 year corporate bond ETFs and to actively managed bond funds in the same space to judge whether passive indexing delivers comparable returns after fees. Watch credit spreads through financial media and data providers: when spreads spike, it is a sign that the market is repricing corporate credit risk upward, which may foreshadow difficulty in the fund's holdings. Track major rating actions (downgrades and upgrades) to see if the overall credit quality of the fund's benchmark is deteriorating. And for broader context, read the Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index commentary and Federal Reserve statements on monetary policy, as rate expectations move the entire bond market far more than credit news does.
