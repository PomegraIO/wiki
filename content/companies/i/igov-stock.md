*The **iShares International [Treasury Bond](/treasury-bond/) ETF (IGOV)** is an exchange-traded fund that holds [bonds](/bond/) issued by the governments of developed nations outside the United States—Treasuries from Canada, the United Kingdom, Germany, Japan, Australia, and other countries with strong [credit ratings](/credit-rating/). It offers investors a way to diversify fixed-income holdings across borders and currencies without picking individual foreign bonds.*

## What does IGOV actually hold?

IGOV tracks the Bloomberg International Treasury Bond Index, which includes investment-grade government bonds from approximately 20 major developed-market countries. The fund holds bonds of all maturities offered by the index, so holdings range from very short-term (a year or less to maturity) to long-dated (20+ years). The weighting is market-cap based, so the largest bond issuers by outstanding debt—typically countries with large economies and large debt stocks like the United Kingdom, Germany, Canada, and Japan—dominate the portfolio. The index is float-adjusted, meaning some bonds are excluded if they are held by [central banks](/central-bank/) or otherwise illiquid.

The fund's holdings are denominated in foreign currencies: [British pounds](/british-pound/), euros, [Canadian dollars](/canadian-dollar/), [Japanese yen](/japanese-yen/), [Australian dollars](/australian-dollar/), and others. For a US investor, this means you are taking on [currency risk](/currency-risk/) alongside [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/). When the [US dollar](/us-dollar/) strengthens, the value of foreign-currency bonds held by IGOV declines in dollar terms; when the dollar weakens, foreign holdings gain. This currency effect can enhance or detract from total returns substantially, depending on exchange-rate moves.

## Why hold foreign government bonds instead of US Treasuries?

Foreign government bonds offer different yields, durations, and risk characteristics than US Treasuries. A Canadian government bond might offer a slightly higher yield than a similar-maturity US Treasury if the Bank of Canada's [interest rates](/interest-rate/) differ from the [Federal Reserve's](/federal-reserve/), creating a relative-value opportunity. Currencies diversify a portfolio: if the US dollar is strong and likely to weaken, foreign bonds offer a currency hedge. Some investors also use international bonds to diversify across different central-bank policies and economic cycles—a [recession](/recession/) in the United States may not coincide with one in Europe or Australia, so their government bonds might perform differently.

Politically, holding bonds from multiple sovereigns reduces concentrated exposure to US government policy, debt dynamics, or [political risk](/political-risk/). A foreign investor might use IGOV to avoid concentration in any single country's [credit risk](/credit-risk/), though investment-grade sovereigns in the developed world have very low default risk.

## How do interest rates and currencies interact in the fund?

IGOV's returns depend on two main forces: interest-rate moves in foreign markets and exchange-rate moves. If interest rates in Germany fall while the euro strengthens against the dollar, a US investor in German bunds profits both from rising bond prices and from currency appreciation. But if German rates rise while the euro weakens, both effects work against you, magnifying losses. This dual exposure makes IGOV more volatile than a pure-duration trade—the fund moves not only with global interest rates but also with forex sentiment.

Central banks and economic data drive these dynamics. When the [European Central Bank](/european-central-bank/) signals rate hikes, euro rates rise and the euro often appreciates, affecting IGOV's return. When the [Bank of Japan](/bank-of-japan/) maintains ultra-low rates while the yen weakens, Japanese government bonds fall in dollar terms. Sophisticated investors track the forward yields on each country's bonds and currency forwards to judge whether foreign bonds offer attractive value adjusted for risk.

## What are the actual risks here?

The primary risks are interest-rate risk, currency risk, and [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/). Interest-rate risk is straightforward: when yields on foreign government bonds rise, the market value of IGOV falls. Longer-dated bonds in the fund experience larger moves. Currency risk is less obvious to many retail investors: IGOV's holdings are in foreign currencies, so you are betting that these currencies will hold their value or appreciate against the dollar. A sharp dollar rally, as happened in 2022-2023, hurts the fund's dollar-denominated returns.

Concentration risk arises because a few countries' large debt markets dominate the index. Japan, the United Kingdom, and Germany together often represent half or more of the portfolio. A credit downgrade, fiscal crisis, or severe recession in any of these countries would ripple through the fund. While investment-grade sovereigns are very safe in absolute terms, they are not free from risk—the euro-zone debt crisis (2010-2015) proved that developed-market government defaults are theoretically possible, even if uncommon.

Geopolitical risk affects some holdings more than others. Some investors worry about Japan's [debt-to-GDP ratio](/debt-to-gdp-ratio/), which is historically high; others worry about political instability in select regions. These concerns shift the [relative valuations](/relative-valuation/) and yields across different countries' bonds over time.

## Who should consider IGOV?

IGOV appeals to investors who want to diversify fixed-income holdings across multiple currency zones and countries, who believe the US dollar is overvalued and want currency exposure, who are seeking different yield or duration profiles than available in US Treasuries, or who are building a globally diversified portfolio. It is popular among institutional investors managing global [asset allocation](/asset-allocation/) who need to match liabilities in multiple currencies.

IGOV is less suitable for investors who are uncomfortable with [currency volatility](/currency-volatility/), who are bullish on the US dollar, who have short time horizons (currency swings can be large over weeks or months), or who want to keep their fixed income in US dollars only. Short-term traders should note that while the fund is liquid, large positions can move markets for specific foreign government bonds, and trading costs may be higher than for US Treasuries.

## How to research IGOV and evaluate it for your portfolio

Start with the [BlackRock](/blk-stock/) iShares fact sheet and prospectus, which detail the fund's country and maturity composition. Compare the fund's weighted-average maturity and yield to the yield of a similar-duration US Treasury or US bond fund to understand the relative value proposition. Watch the Bloomberg International Treasury Bond Index to see how it moves versus the US Aggregate Bond Index or US Treasury Index.

Track exchange rates daily if you hold IGOV, as currency moves often dominate short-term returns. Use a financial website to view the USD Index or individual [currency pairs](/currency-pair/) (EUR/USD, GBP/USD, JPY/USD) to contextualize the fund's performance. Read foreign central bank announcements and economic data releases from major economies, as these drive interest-rate expectations and currency moves. Finally, compare IGOV's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) and tracking error to alternative foreign bond funds to ensure you are getting efficient exposure.
