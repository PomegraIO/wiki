*A **[sector fund](/sector-fund/)** is a portfolio of companies in a single industry — in this case, technology. IGM holds a diverse set of publicly traded tech firms, from the largest ([Apple](/aapl-stock/), [Microsoft](/msft-stock/), [Nvidia](/nvda-stock/)) to hundreds of smaller technology-focused companies, offering exposure to an entire economic sector in a single tradable share.*

## Why "expanded" matters

The tech sector is notoriously difficult to define at the edges. Is a telecommunications company with a software business inside technology or telecom? Is a semiconductor maker a tech company or an industrial one? Should cloud infrastructure providers be counted? The iShares Expanded Tech fund takes a broad view. Rather than holding only computer-hardware makers and pure-software companies, it includes semiconductors, telecommunications with significant digital segments, data-center operators, and other businesses whose core economics have become tech-dependent.

This "expanded" approach matters because it captures more of the technology ecosystem than a narrower definition would. The classic "tech sector" might miss meaningful exposures to infrastructure providers and telecom-adjacent names that are increasingly core to the digital economy. By broadening the tent, IGM aims to avoid missing large technology-driven companies that might technically fall under a different industrial classification.

## Concentration risk in a diversified fund

IGM holds over 200 companies, which sounds diversified. In practice, the fund is heavily concentrated in its top holdings. The ten largest positions — which include names like Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Meta, and [Tesla](/tsla-stock/) — typically account for 40–50% of the fund's value. This is both inevitable and problematic. Inevitable because the largest technology companies are, by far, the most valuable in the world; problematic because it means the fund's returns are driven disproportionately by the quarterly earnings and stock moves of a handful of mega-cap names.

When Nvidia has a strong quarter on AI demand, IGM rallies. When Meta disappoints on advertising, IGM feels it. When the entire large-cap tech sector hits a [bear market](/bear-market/), even a "diversified" 200-name fund declines steeply. This concentration is important to understand: you are not buying "technology" in the abstract; you are buying a market-cap-weighted bet on whichever tech companies the market values most highly at any moment. For now, that means artificial intelligence hardware and software leaders; five years from now, it could be something entirely different.

## The sector's sensitivity to interest rates and growth expectations

Technology stocks are particularly sensitive to expectations about economic growth and [interest rates](/interest-rate/). When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) is cutting rates and the economy is expected to accelerate, tech tends to outperform the broader market. When rates are rising and growth is slowing, tech often declines sharply because investors become less willing to pay high multiples for uncertain future earnings. Over the past 15 years, the technology sector has had long stretches of wild outperformance (2010–2021 in particular) and shorter stretches of severe underperformance (parts of 2022, for instance).

This rhythm is fundamental to how tech investing works. Technology companies tend to have capital-light business models, high operating leverage, and earnings that scale quickly if demand grows but collapse if it does not. This makes them valuable in a boom and frightening in a contraction. An investor in IGM is implicitly betting that tech's structural advantages will continue to shine and that near-term [recessions](/recession/) will be brief enough to avoid catastrophic multiples compression.

## What "expanded" does and does not include

The fund includes semiconductor companies like [Broadcom](/avgo-stock/) and Applied Materials, which design and manufacture the chips that run everything from phones to data centers. It includes cloud-infrastructure platforms like AWS-owning Amazon (though partially classified here as tech rather than retail). It includes telecommunications companies with massive software and digital-services segments, like T-Mobile. It includes business-software giants like [Salesforce](/crm-stock/) and CRM platforms. It includes smaller, faster-growing software makers that might not make it into a narrower tech index. What it does not include — or includes only minimally — are traditional industrial companies that happen to use technology heavily, or non-tech companies whose supply chains depend on semiconductors.

## How IGM differs from QQQ and other tech benchmarks

The most famous tech-heavy fund, QQQ, tracks the [Nasdaq](/nasdaq/) 100 — the hundred largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq exchange. Because the Nasdaq is home to most tech companies, QQQ is de facto a tech fund, albeit one that also captures a few non-tech Nasdaq names. IGM is explicitly a technology-sector fund, which means its methodology is designed to capture all meaningful technology companies, regardless of exchange. This makes IGM slightly broader and more inclusive than QQQ in theory, though in practice the two overlap heavily because the same mega-cap tech leaders dominate both.

## Evaluating IGM as a holding

Owning IGM is a bet that technology will remain a central driver of profit growth and economic value creation. That is probably true, but it is not certain. Artificial intelligence, semiconductors, cloud computing, and digital platforms are real structural forces. But they can also be priced too high, disrupted unexpectedly, or face regulatory pressure that slows growth. The past 15 years have been extraordinarily kind to tech investors; the next 15 might not be.

From a practical standpoint, IGM is useful as a [sector rotation](/sector-rotation/) tool — if you believe tech will outperform, IGM is a clean way to add exposure. It is less useful as a core, forever holding if you are not comfortable with the sector's ups and downs and its heavy dependence on a few mega-cap names. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is low, so costs are not a barrier. Liquidity is excellent. The real question is whether you want that much concentration in tech at current valuations.

## What to watch

Track the earnings growth of the top 10 holdings — they drive returns. Watch whether the concentration (the percentage of the fund in the top 10) is rising or falling, as rising concentration signals increasing dominance by a smaller set of companies. Monitor valuation metrics like the [price-to-earnings ratio](/price-to-earnings-ratio/) and compare it to historical norms; if tech multiples expand dramatically, future returns are likely to be constrained. Pay attention to any regulatory threats to large tech companies, as a single antitrust action or data-privacy law can ripple through the entire sector.
