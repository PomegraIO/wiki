*The **iShares [Interest Rate](/interest-rate/) Hedged Long-Term [Corporate Bond](/corporate-bond/) ETF** (IGBH) invests in long-term U.S. investment-grade corporate [bonds](/bond/) while simultaneously entering into interest-rate [derivative contracts](/derivative-contract/) designed to offset, or hedge, the price loss that would normally occur if interest rates rise.*

Most bond investors face a familiar dilemma: longer-maturity bonds offer higher yields and more sensitivity to interest-rate movements, which can amplify losses if rates rise unexpectedly. Shorter-maturity bonds shield against rate risk but deliver lower yield. IGBH attempts to break that trade-off by combining the yield of a long-duration corporate bond portfolio with a hedging programme that dampens the interest-rate sensitivity. The result is a fund that seeks the yield advantage of long bonds while containing the volatility that rising rates would normally inflict.

Blackrock's iShares, one of the largest ETF issuers in the world, manages IGBH as part of its suite of fixed-income products. The fund holds a diversified portfolio of long-term (generally 10+ year maturity) corporate bonds rated investment grade, spanning sectors from financials and industrials to healthcare and energy. The selection reflects the breadth of the U.S. corporate debt market at the longer end of the curve, where companies issue debt to fund long-term capital projects and refinance aging debt at predictable, multi-decade intervals.

The hedging mechanism — the core innovation — typically involves interest-rate futures, interest-rate swaps, or other derivatives. These instruments allow the fund to take a short position in longer-term interest rates, which gains in value when rates rise. That gain offsets the loss in the bond holdings themselves. Conceptually, if the bonds fall 2% in value because rates rose, and the hedges gain 2%, the fund's net performance is neutral with respect to rate movements — the investor captures the yield from the bonds (the income component) without the price volatility from rate swings.

In practice, the hedge is imperfect. The derivatives have costs, administrative burden, and timing mismatches with the underlying bonds. The relationship between long-term rates and the instruments used to hedge is not always one-to-one; if the [yield curve](/yield-curve/) shifts in unexpected ways (for instance, if long-term rates fall faster than short-term rates), the hedge may not protect perfectly. Additionally, the fund's holdings and hedge ratios are rebalanced periodically, creating small slippages. But the intent and broad effect are clear: reduce the interest-rate sensitivity (duration) of a long-bond portfolio while preserving access to the yield.

For investors who want corporate-bond income but fear a rising-rate environment, IGBH offers a middle path. Traditional long-bond funds deliver high yield but maximum rate sensitivity; traditional short-bond or floating-rate funds have low sensitivity but minimal yield. IGBH seeks to deliver a high portion of the yield with substantially lower rate sensitivity — trading some yield potential (the cost of the hedge) for peace of mind about volatility.

The trade-offs are substantial, however. First, the hedging costs money. The fund must pay for the derivatives, the rebalancing, and the financing costs of maintaining a short interest-rate position. These costs show up in the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), which is typically higher than a straightforward corporate bond [index fund](/index-fund/) but competitive with other actively managed or complex-structure bond funds. Second, the hedge constrains upside in a falling-rate scenario. When rates fall and long bonds rally sharply, the hedges lose money in direct proportion. An unhedged long-bond fund can deliver spectacular returns in a benign rate environment; IGBH will lag. Third, the complexity of the hedging creates timing risk and imperfection: the hedges may slip out of sync with the bonds, leaving the investor exposed to residual volatility.

Default risk and [credit risk](/credit-risk/) are not hedged away. IGBH's focus on investment-grade corporates offers some protection against systemic credit catastrophe, but individual issuers can still deteriorate or fail. If a major corporate bond default occurs, IGBH will suffer real loss regardless of interest rates. In the 2008 financial crisis, [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) widened dramatically (the yield premium corporate bonds pay over Treasuries), and many funds that were hedged against rate risk experienced losses from credit spread widening. The hedging protects against one dimension of risk (interest-rate moves) but leaves credit risk fully exposed.

The fund's applicability is narrow but genuine. For an investor with a specific, intermediate time horizon who cannot tolerate interest-rate volatility but wants corporate-bond income, IGBH fits the bill. For a retiree who needs steady cash flow but worries that rising rates will crater a long-bond portfolio, IGBH offers a compromise. For a core bond investor seeking broad, reliable returns, a standard corporate bond index fund is simpler and cheaper.

Researching IGBH requires understanding the hedge design. [Blackrock](/blk-stock/) publishes fact sheets that describe the hedging methodology and show the fund's [effective duration](/effective-duration/) (how much it would decline in percentage terms if rates rose by one percentage point). Compare IGBH's duration to an unhedged long-term corporate bond index ETF: IGBH should show substantially lower duration, often in the 1 to 3 year range compared to 8 to 12 for an unhedged equivalent. Monitor the fund's performance in both rising-rate and falling-rate environments. Does the hedge behave as intended in rising-rate scenarios? Does the fund deliver the corporate-bond yield it promises? Over longer periods, has the hedge's cost been justified by the volatility reduction it provided? The prospectus details the hedging instruments and their rebalancing frequency, revealing the operational complexity underneath the simple name.
