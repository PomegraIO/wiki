*IQMM ([NASDAQ](/nasdaq/): IQMM) is an exchange-traded fund that tracks the Morningstar Short-Term [Treasury Bill](/treasury-bill/) Index, providing direct exposure to a rotating basket of U.S. Treasury bills with maturities of one year or less. It exists at the intersection of money-market funds and equity-market convenience: the holdings are government debt, the expenses are minimal, and the fund trades on an exchange like a stock rather than settling through the money-market plumbing.*

## A money market fund traded like a stock

IQMM's essential appeal is structural. Traditional money-market funds are settlement bodies — you buy shares through a brokerage, and the transaction settles through the Fed's money-market infrastructure, subject to regulatory caps on share price and yield. IQMM sidesteps that by wrapping Treasury-bill exposure in an ETF wrapper: it trades on NASDAQ during market hours like any equity, settles in T+2 (two trading days) like a stock, and holds no daily-sweep mechanism.

The fund tracks the Morningstar Short-Term Treasury Bill Index, which is rebuilt monthly to hold Treasury bills with an average maturity of one to two months. The index is constructed to be liquid and representative of the short-end of the bill market. Because IQMM buys that index, its holdings turn over rapidly — the bills mature, are replaced, and the process repeats. For the investor, that means the fund's yield reflects the current money-market rate (the yield on short-term government debt) without the friction of separate money-market-account agreements.

## Why this fund exists

ProShares created IQMM to address a real frustration: money-market funds sit at an awkward place in the financial ecosystem. They are extremely safe (government-backed) and offer yields that move with short-term [interest rates](/interest-rate/), but they are administratively separate from a brokerage account. Some brokerage platforms sweep cash into their own money-market funds; others offer no competitive rate at all; still others charge fees that whittle down the yield.

An ETF trades on the open market, so the yield is transparent and set by supply and demand, not by a fund sponsor's choice. It settles like an equity, so it integrates cleanly with a brokerage account and trading systems. And because it tracks only U.S. Treasury bills — the safest, most liquid short-term debt in the world — the fund carries essentially zero [credit risk](/credit-risk/) and extraordinary liquidity.

## Holdings and interest-rate sensitivity

IQMM holds a diversified set of Treasury bills across the maturity spectrum (up to one year) in order to track its index. The total holdings might number in the dozens or hundreds, so no single bill dominates. Because bills are so short-duration, the fund has very low sensitivity to interest-rate moves — a 1% rise in Treasury yields will shift the fund's price almost imperceptibly. The fund's yield, by contrast, moves in nearly real time with short-term money-market rates, so investors holding IQMM capture the benefit of any rate increase immediately through the next distribution.

That low duration makes IQMM appropriate as a cash substitute (a place to park money for very short periods while earning the money-market rate) but inappropriate as a total return play (if you believe short-term rates will fall, and you want to profit from that fall, you would need longer-duration [bonds](/bond/), not bills).

## Expenses and tax treatment

IQMM's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is minimal — typically in the basis-point range (less than 0.10% per year). The index itself is mechanical (no active selection, just bills of certain maturities), and the fund manager's job is largely administrative: buy the bills, hold them to maturity, reinvest the proceeds.

As a Treasury fund, all income is taxable as ordinary income at the federal level (and in most states and localities), not as capital gains. This is standard for bond funds, but it is worth knowing if you hold IQMM in a taxable account — the distributions, while safe in dollars, will be taxed as interest, not as [qualified dividend](/qualified-dividend/) income.

## Who should consider it

IQMM is built for investors who want money-market returns without the administrative overhead of a separate money-market account. It is particularly useful for:

- Brokerage accounts where the default sweep rate is poor or where no sweep rate exists
- Investors who want to see Treasury-bill yields in real time and move in and out of short-term government debt without transaction barriers
- Those who believe short-term rates will remain elevated and want to capture that yield while holding a highly liquid, safe asset
- Traders or tactical allocators who need to park capital temporarily and prefer an exchange-traded structure

It is not appropriate for total-return seeking or as a general portfolio builder — it is structurally a cash equivalent, not an investment position.

## Understanding the fund — where to look

The fund's prospectus (available through NASDAQ or ProShares' website) details the exact methodology of the Morningstar Short-Term Treasury Bill Index and the fund's fee structure. For current holdings and composition, check the fund's fact sheet, which shows the exact maturity distribution and average life of the bill portfolio. The Treasury Department's website publishes auction data and current bill yields, which set the context for what IQMM's yield represents in the broader money-market landscape. As with any fund, the share price changes with prevailing short-term interest rates and money-market demand, but the underlying credit risk is minimal: the U.S. government is the borrower, and the Fed stands as the liquidity backstop.
