*IYRI is an actively managed exchange-traded fund that seeks high current income from U.S. real-estate holdings—primarily REITs—by using leverage, derivative strategies, and tactical sector bets to amplify yields and deliver distributions above what a standard, unleveraged REIT fund would produce.*

## The proposition

Standard REIT funds deliver 3–4% yields. IYRI aims higher: 7–9% or more. That gap comes from leverage and active management. The fund borrows money and uses it to amplify its positions in REIT equities and options strategies. It is not simply a buy-and-hold basket of properties; it is a dynamic income machine that trades in and out of positions and uses financial derivatives to harvest yield. The trade-off is complexity: more return potential in normal times, but amplified volatility and [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) in bad times.

## Structure and mechanics

IYRI is actively managed, meaning a team at NEOS makes decisions about what to buy, sell, and how much leverage to deploy. The fund is allowed to use leverage (borrow) to increase its assets under management and return. It also uses options strategies—selling [covered calls](/covered-call/) against REIT positions, for instance—to generate additional income beyond [dividends](/dividend/). Those tactical moves are where the high yield comes from.

The fund holds a concentrated portfolio of perhaps 20–40 REIT holdings, not the 70 that an unleveraged broad REIT fund might own. Concentration means bigger bets on specific properties and REIT types. If the chosen handful performs well, that concentration amplifies returns. If they stumble, losses are magnified.

Distributions are paid monthly, not quarterly like most REIT funds. That frequency is marketing sugar—it feels good to get paid often—but the total annual yield is what matters. The monthly dole also makes it tempting to spend the income instead of reinvesting it, which many investors who choose high-income funds intend to do.

## Income in normal times, pain in stress

When the REIT market is calm and cash flows are reliable, IYRI works. The leverage does not burden the fund, and the option strategies generate steady premium income. Distributions flow reliably. An investor collecting income gets what they paid for.

But REITs are cyclical. When [interest rates](/interest-rate/) spike, property valuations fall, and REIT prices decline. A fund using leverage to own those depreciating assets bleeds faster than an unlevered competitor. The leverage works in reverse: instead of amplifying gains, it amplifies losses. If rates stay elevated for months, the damage compounds. In the 2022 rising-rate environment, high-income leveraged REIT ETFs suffered steep declines.

There is also a phenomenon called "distribution capture" that can confuse investors. A fund paying high distributions is sometimes returning capital (your own money), not just income. In tax-inefficient situations or during market downturns, a large portion of the distribution can come from the fund's declining [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) (NAV), not from the income-generating properties. The distribution amount stays stable on paper while the fund's true value shrinks. That is a form of return of capital, which carries tax consequences: you may owe taxes on a "distribution" that was really a refund of your own investment.

## Who this fits and who it does not

IYRI works for retirees who need to draw monthly cash and can tolerate significant price swings. It works for investors in high-tax-bracket years (though REIT income is taxed as ordinary income, and any return of capital has its own tax treatment) who want to accept the leverage. It does not work for investors with a long time horizon who can reinvest—the leverage and active trading create tax drag and volatility that a simple REIT [index fund](/index-fund/) avoids. It does not work for those who cannot stomach REIT prices falling 30–40% in rising-rate environments.

It also does not work for anyone who thinks high distributions equal high returns. A fund can pay 9% annually while losing 15% in price, netting a negative return. The distribution and the price are separate—you need both to evaluate total returns.

## The watchlist

Check the fund's holdings and strategy documents to understand which REIT types are favored and how much leverage is currently deployed. [Leverage ratios](/leverage-ratio-forex/) (how much borrowed money as a percent of assets) tell you how much amplification is baked in. Higher leverage means higher distributions in calm times and worse losses in downturns.

Monitor interest-rate expectations. Rising rates are the dominant risk. A forecast for stable or falling rates is favorable for IYRI; a forecast for sustained high rates is not.

Track the NAV versus the share price. If the fund's net asset value per share is falling while distributions stay flat, that is red flag territory—distributions are cannibalizing capital. Compare the fund's performance to a plain REIT index fund (like IYR) over rolling one- and three-year periods. Leverage sometimes wins and sometimes loses; longer periods show whether the active management and leverage add genuine value or just create noise.

Watch monthly distributions, but do not confuse a high payout with a high return. Over full years, calculate the total return: dividends reinvested plus or minus the fund's price movement. That is what actually landed in your pocket.

Read the prospectus and strategy documents to understand the leverage limits, the options strategies, and the rebalancing rules. NEOS publishes educational content on its real-estate strategies; that is worth reviewing to understand the philosophy.

IYRI is a specialized tool, not a starter fund. It suits specific needs and specific time horizons. As with all leveraged and [actively managed funds](/actively-managed-fund/), the complexity and costs deserve scrutiny. This is not an investment recommendation, but a guide to how this particular fund works and where its risks lie.
