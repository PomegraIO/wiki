*A **[thematic ETF](/thematic-etf/)** is an exchange-traded fund designed around a specific investment theme or trend rather than a traditional sector or market-cap index. Themes can range from "clean energy" to "artificial intelligence" to "robotics." The iShares Neuroscience and Healthcare ETF (IBRN) is thematic — it holds companies developing treatments, devices, and diagnostics for neurological and psychiatric diseases, including Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, Schizophrenia, Autism, PTSD, and Depression.*

## What is the fund targeting?

The neuroscience theme is narrower than "healthcare" at large. Healthcare ETFs typically hold a broad mix of pharmaceuticals, medical devices, diagnostics, hospitals, insurers, and services companies. IBRN is a slice of that: companies explicitly engaged in neuroscience research, drug development, or treatment delivery. The motivation is that neuroscience is a fast-growing field — aging populations create rising demand for Alzheimer's and Parkinson's treatments, awareness of mental health is increasing, and scientific progress in understanding the brain is accelerating.

The fund's holdings span the biotech-to-commercialization spectrum. Some companies are pure research-stage biotechs with one or two experimental drugs in clinical trials. Others are established pharmaceutical firms with neuroscience divisions, medical-device makers producing implants or diagnostic tools, and diagnostics companies developing tests for neurological disease. This range means the fund captures both small, high-growth (and high-risk) names and larger, more established players.

## Why concentrate on neuroscience?

The case for a neuroscience theme rests on several factors. The global burden of neurological and psychiatric disease is enormous and growing — Alzheimer's affects tens of millions worldwide and is projected to grow sharply as populations age. Depression and other mental-health conditions are increasingly recognized as major public-health concerns. At the same time, neuroscience research has been advancing: we understand more about the brain's biology, and drug pipelines are progressing from earlier stages into later-stage trials and commercialization.

Investment in neuroscience by both public markets and venture capital has risen. Investors betting on the theme believe that the next decade will bring meaningful progress in treating conditions that have historically been difficult, and that companies positioned in that space stand to capture significant revenue and profit growth. This is speculative — drug development is notoriously uncertain — but the case for structural tailwinds in the sector has logic.

## The composition and concentration risk

IBRN holds roughly 70 to 100 companies, depending on the market and index composition at any given time. The largest positions are typically large-cap pharmaceutical firms with meaningful neuroscience divisions — [Eli Lilly](/lly-stock/), [Novartis](/nvs-stock/), [Merck](/mrk-stock/), [Johnson & Johnson](/jnj-stock/) — as well as established biotech companies like [Biogen](/biib-stock/), which have made their name in neuroscience. Mid-cap and small-cap positions include more specialized neuroscience players and earlier-stage biotechs.

The sector concentration can be high. Many holdings are biotech companies, which are sensitive to clinical-trial results, regulatory approval odds, and financing conditions. A disappointing trial result for one company can ripple through sentiment for the entire sector. The fund lacks [diversification](/diversification/) into defensive healthcare areas like medical supplies, medical services, or established generics, meaning it is purely a growth-and-development play on neuroscience specifically.

## The risk structure

Investing in IBRN is not like owning a broad healthcare index. The risks are skewed toward execution and innovation. For biotech and pharmaceutical companies, the critical uncertainties are: Will their experimental drugs work? Will they be safe enough? Will regulators approve them? Can they attract capital and partnerships? A pipeline problem at one company is not material; a systematic slowdown in approvals across neuroscience would be material.

A second risk is valuation. Thematic ETFs often attract momentum and trendy capital, which can drive valuations high. If the theme falls out of favour or fails to deliver on expectations, the fund can underperform. Neuroscience has had several periods of hype followed by disappointment — the failure of certain Alzheimer's drugs after years in development, for instance, has repeatedly reset sentiment.

A third risk is regulatory. Drug approvals are uncertain and geopolitically fragmented. A company relying on FDA approval faces one set of hurdles; if it needs EMA approval in Europe or approval from other regulators, the approval timeline and probability can differ. Mental-health treatment is also subject to shifting societal attitudes, reimbursement pressures, and clinical debate about the right approach.

## Who holds this fund and why?

IBRN appeals to investors with a conviction about neuroscience being a growth frontier. It is not a core holding but rather a satellite position for someone who believes in the theme and is willing to accept the concentration and [execution risk](/execution-risk/) that comes with it. Some investors use it for an allocation to biotech without having to pick individual stocks. Others use it as a thematic bet as part of a broader healthcare allocation.

The fund's performance will lag or lead the broader market depending on whether neuroscience narratives are in or out of favour and whether actual progress in drug development is perceived as accelerating or slowing. There is no steady-state return here — IBRN is a cyclical, trend-sensitive holding.

## How to research IBRN and neuroscience investing

Start with the fund's holdings list, which shows exactly which companies are included and their weights. Understanding the largest positions — typically the big pharma names with neuroscience divisions and the established biotech leaders — is essential. Look at the index methodology: does it screen for revenue from neuroscience specifically, or include any company in the space?

Follow the clinical-trial calendar. When major neuroscience drug trials report results, they move individual stocks and can move the entire fund. Stay aware of FDA approvals, rejections, and regulatory guidance letters — these are the events that reset sentiment in biotech.

Read earnings calls and investor presentations from the largest holdings to understand their neuroscience pipelines — how many drugs are in Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3, and near approval. A company with a robust pipeline advancing is a different story from one where a key program has stumbled.

Finally, track sector-wide metrics: how much capital is flowing into neuroscience startups, what are approval trends looking like, and is the scientific consensus on neurological disease treatment advancing or stalling. IBRN's returns depend not on macroeconomics or [interest rates](/interest-rate/) alone, but on the health and momentum of the neuroscience innovation ecosystem.
