*The **iShares Select U.S. REIT ETF** (ticker ICF) holds a curated portfolio of the largest, most-liquid [real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/) traded on American exchanges. It is designed for investors seeking exposure to the broad real estate market — office buildings, shopping centers, apartments, industrial warehouses, and specialized properties — without owning individual properties or managing tenants.*

## The evolution of REIT investing and iShares' place in it

Real estate investment trusts emerged as a modern asset class in the 1960s when Congress created the REIT structure to allow small investors to own commercial property the way they own stocks. For decades, REITs remained a niche category: professional investors and real estate specialists understood them, but they were absent from most retail portfolios and mainstream financial discussion.

That began to change in the 1990s and 2000s as REITs became more standardized, more transparent in their accounting, and more actively traded on major exchanges. The 2008 financial crisis created a dark period for REITs as leverage in the real estate market unwound dramatically, but the subsequent recovery and secular shift toward passive investing through [index funds](/index-fund/) accelerated REIT adoption. By the 2010s, REITs had become core holdings in many diversified portfolios.

iShares, owned by [BlackRock](/blk-stock/), launched ETFs focused on REITs early in the industry's explosive growth. The iShares approach emphasizes liquid, large-cap REITs traded on major U.S. exchanges rather than illiquid private properties or small, thinly traded property trusts. ICF, in particular, uses a selective approach: rather than tracking every REIT in existence, it holds the largest and most widely held trusts, with [concentration limits](/concentration-limits/) that prevent any one property type or company from dominating the fund. This makes it both more stable and more representative of the REIT market as experienced by most investors.

## REITs and the property market

A REIT is a corporate wrapper around real estate assets. The REIT owns the property, collects rent and lease payments from tenants, pays operating costs and debt service, and is required by law to distribute at least 90% of taxable income to shareholders as [dividends](/dividend/). That high [payout ratio](/payout-ratio/) — far higher than ordinary corporate stocks — is what makes REITs attractive to income-seeking investors.

ICF's underlying holdings span multiple property types: office towers, apartment complexes, shopping malls and retail centers, industrial warehouses and logistics parks, specialized facilities like data centers and cell-tower networks, and even timberland and agricultural land in some cases. Each category behaves differently in different economic environments. Office properties are sensitive to office employment and work-from-home trends. Retail is sensitive to brick-and-mortar commerce and consumer spending. Industrial properties are sensitive to supply-chain activity and e-commerce volumes. Residential is sensitive to [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and household formation.

By holding a diversified mix of property types and managers, ICF reduces the [idiosyncratic risk](/idiosyncratic-risk/) of any single property or REIT company and gives an investor broad exposure to real estate as an asset class.

## The dividend yield and tax treatment

ICF's most visible characteristic to income investors is its [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/). Because REITs must distribute nearly all earnings, they typically yield substantially more than broad stock ETFs — often in the 3–5% range depending on interest rates and property valuations. For investors in low tax brackets or using tax-advantaged retirement accounts, this yield is attractive. For investors in high tax brackets in taxable brokerage accounts, it is less attractive because REIT distributions are typically taxed as ordinary income rather than as [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/) (the lower-taxed kind).

The tax treatment is an important detail anyone holding ICF in a taxable account should understand. Unlike dividend income from ordinary corporate stocks, most REIT distributions are ordinary income and are taxed at full ordinary income rates. Some distributions may be treated as capital returns or as [depreciation](/depreciation/) recapture, but the bulk is ordinary income. This is another reason REITs have traditionally fit better in retirement accounts where tax efficiency is less of a concern.

## Interest rates and property values

REITs are sensitive to the interest-rate environment in multiple ways. When interest rates fall, property values tend to rise because the [discount rate](/discount-rate/) used to value future lease payments falls. Moreover, lower rates make REIT debt cheaper to refinance. Conversely, rising rates depress property valuations and make debt more expensive. This means ICF's price rises and falls with the broader interest-rate cycle, often more dramatically than dividend-paying stocks.

Additionally, the dividend yield makes ICF implicitly sensitive to the [risk-free rate](/risk-free-rate/). If Treasury yields are very low, a 4% REIT yield looks attractive and attracts capital. If Treasuries are offering 5% with no risk, REIT valuations can compress until the REIT yield is competitive with [bond](/bond/) yields. Understanding this dynamic is essential to understanding ICF's price behavior over longer periods.

## The shift in commercial real estate

Over the past five years, several headwinds have affected the [commercial real estate](/commercial-real-estate/) market: the pandemic accelerated work-from-home arrangements, stranding some office properties; e-commerce continued to shift demand away from traditional retail toward industrial logistics; and rising interest rates impaired property values and made new development less economical. These macroeconomic forces directly affect the properties ICF holds and the companies that own them.

Forward-looking investors in ICF should monitor the office-property environment (higher vacancy, potential for write-downs), the retail landscape (which tenants are struggling, which are thriving), and industrial supply (whether logistics and warehouse demand remains strong). Many REITs in ICF are actively repositioning their portfolios away from struggling property types and toward sectors with secular tailwinds, but this transition takes time.

## How ICF works in a portfolio

ICF is most useful as a portfolio diversifier and income source. Real estate tends to move somewhat independently of stocks and bonds — the Correlation is not zero, but neither are REITs simply stocks repackaged. Adding ICF to a stock-and-bond portfolio can reduce overall volatility and boost income. However, because REITs are still equities and are sensitive to interest rates, they are not a substitute for bonds in a defensive portfolio.

The fund trades on exchanges like any stock ETF, with liquidity decent enough for retail investors (though not as deep as the S&P 500 ETF). Its [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically in the 0.4–0.5% range, which is reasonable for a specialized [sector fund](/sector-fund/). The prospectus and fact sheet will detail the current holdings, property-type breakdown, and historical dividend frequency — essential research before committing capital.
