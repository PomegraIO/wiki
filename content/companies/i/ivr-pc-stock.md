Invesco Mortgage Capital is a mortgage [real estate investment trust](/real-estate-investment-trust/), or mREIT — a company that earns money by holding mortgages and mortgage-backed securities and collecting the interest and principal payments that flow from them. The structure is straightforward: the trust buys portfolios of mortgages or securities backed by mortgages, finances them with debt, and passes the resulting net interest income to shareholders as [dividends](/dividend/). The business hinges on the interest-rate spread between what the mortgages yield and what the company pays to finance them, and mREIT investors are explicitly trading that spread and the risks that come with it.

## Birth in the post-crisis mortgage environment

Invesco Mortgage Capital emerged in 2009 as IVCAP (Invesco Capital Markets) in the aftermath of the financial crisis. The housing bubble had burst, mortgage securities had imploded, and the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) was in emergency mode, holding [interest rates](/interest-rate/) near zero and buying mortgage-backed securities to stabilize the market. In that environment, mortgage securities that had been untradeable became liquid again, and the interest yields on them were historically high relative to [risk-free rates](/risk-free-rate/). [Mortgage REITs](/mortgage-reit/), an older structure that had fallen out of favor during the boom, became attractive again because they could borrow at the near-zero rates the Fed was fostering and invest in mortgages yielding several percentage points higher. The spread — borrow at 0.5%, invest at 4% — was the alchemy that made the model work.

The company positioned itself to exploit that spread. It hired mortgage specialists, built risk-management infrastructure, and began assembling portfolios of mortgages and mortgage-backed securities, primarily those guaranteed by [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/) and [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/) (agency mortgages). Agency mortgages carry far less [credit risk](/credit-risk/) than non-agency (private) mortgages because the government-sponsored entities stand behind the principal and interest. For a new mREIT, focusing on agency mortgages was prudent; it let the company avoid the complexities of underwriting non-agency risk and leverage the stability of the government guarantee.

## The rate-spread model and the dividend promise

The mREIT model is simple in concept but sensitive in practice. The company borrows money at short-term rates (often overnight or a few days) and invests in longer-term mortgages or mortgage-backed securities that yield more. The difference is the profit. If a mortgage pays 3% and the company's cost of funds is 2%, the spread is 1%, which flows down to equity after the company covers its operating costs.

The dividend promise hinges on maintaining this spread. Mortgage REITs typically distribute most or all of their taxable income to shareholders as dividends, creating high yields — often 8% to 12% or higher in certain rate environments. This attracts income investors, particularly in periods of low rates elsewhere. The risk is that the spread can compress or reverse. If the Fed raises interest rates sharply, the short-term funding costs the company pays can spike while the yield on mortgages already held adjusts more slowly. Or the market value of the mortgage securities themselves can fall if rates rise (because [bonds](/bond/) decline in value when yields rise). The company may be forced to realize losses or reduce the dividend to conserve capital.

## The 2013 taper tantrum and volatility

In 2013, the Fed signaled that it would begin tapering its bond purchases, and rates rose sharply. Mortgage REITs were hammered: the value of their mortgage portfolios fell, funding costs rose, and spreads compressed. Invesco Mortgage Capital, like its peers, saw dividends cut and share prices plunge. The episode exposed the hidden volatility in the model — what looked like safe income in a low-rate environment could evaporate quickly if rates moved.

From that lesson, Invesco Mortgage Capital and other mREITs learned to manage [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) more carefully. The company began using interest-rate hedges — derivatives like swaps and swaptions that protect against rate movements — to lock in spreads and reduce the damage from volatile rate changes. Hedging costs money but buys predictability. A well-hedged mREIT can offer more stable dividends; an unhedged one is a rate-bet, not a mortgage business.

## The pandemic and Fed accommodation

The COVID-19 pandemic saw the Fed return to near-zero rates and massive asset purchases. For mREITs, this was a revival: spreads widened again, funding costs fell, and mortgage yields remained attractive. Invesco Mortgage Capital could borrow cheaply and buy mortgages at good yields. Dividends recovered and share prices stabilized. The [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) again looked compelling to retirees and income-focused investors.

The period also saw housing demand surge — historically low rates drove a refinancing wave and strong demand for new mortgages. Mortgage volumes were abundant, so Invesco Mortgage Capital had plenty to invest in. The company's [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) expanded and dividends were restored.

## The 2022 tightening cycle and the present challenge

In 2022, [inflation](/inflation/) surged and the Fed began raising rates aggressively. Long-term mortgage yields rose, but Invesco Mortgage Capital's short-term funding costs rose even faster. The spread collapsed. The market value of the company's existing mortgage portfolio fell sharply as rates rose. The company faced pressure to cut dividends and take losses.

This is the perpetual tension in mREIT investing: the business model is fundamentally sensitive to the level of and changes in interest rates. When the Fed is accommodative and rates are falling, mREITs flourish. When the Fed is tightening and rates are rising, spreads compress and valuations fall. Unlike a bank, which has deposit funding and longer-dated liabilities that can be stable, an mREIT using short-term repo financing is acutely exposed to rate moves.

## From IVCAP to Invesco

Over its life, the company has undergone reshaping. Originally independent, it eventually came under the Invesco corporate umbrella, which provided capital, risk management, and platform benefits. Invesco's global reach and brand have helped the company access financing and investors. The shift to Invesco ownership did not materially change the core business — buy mortgages, earn the spread, pay dividends — but added operational and financial stability.

## Preferred shares and the risk waterfall

IVR-PC represents preferred shares. Like the common shares, they derive value from the mREIT's ability to generate net interest income and maintain capital. However, preferred shareholders typically have a stated dividend rate and priority in the capital waterfall: if dividends must be cut, common shares are cut first; preferred shares retain their claim longer. In an mREIT, this means preferred shareholders are somewhat insulated from the worst of an interest-rate shock, but not immune. The preference is real but not absolute.

## Following Invesco Mortgage Capital

For investors tracking the company, start with the quarterly reports (SEC CIK 0001437071) and earnings calls, which disclose the mortgage portfolio composition, the hedge ratio, the funding costs, the net interest margin, and the [book value per share](/book-value-per-share/). The book value (assets minus liabilities, divided by shares outstanding) is the reported [net asset value](/net-asset-value/); in periods of rising rates, it often falls significantly.

Key metrics: the net interest margin, the [dividend payout ratio](/dividend-payout-ratio/) relative to net interest income (if above 100%, the company is drawing on capital), the average life of the mortgage portfolio and how it changes as rates move, and the notional amount of interest-rate hedges in place. The yield on the common shares and preferred shares tells you what the market prices in for dividend sustainability.

The fundamental question for any mREIT investor is whether the interest-rate environment is likely to support spreads and dividends. The Fed's policy stance, the shape of the [yield curve](/yield-curve/), and expectations for rate changes drive the answer. IVR-PC offers higher yield than many alternatives, but that yield exists precisely because of the rate sensitivity and the risk that it will be cut if conditions turn unfavorable.
