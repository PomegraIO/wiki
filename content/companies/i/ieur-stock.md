## What does IEUR hold?

IEUR is a plain-vanilla index tracker that holds the large and mid-cap [stock markets](/stock-market/) of developed Europe. The portfolio mirrors the MSCI Europe index, which includes companies from the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Switzerland, Netherlands, Scandinavia, and other Western European nations. It excludes Eastern Europe and emerging markets. The fund owns roughly 500–700 stocks, from banks (HSBC, [Deutsche Bank](/deutsche-bank/)) to consumer brands ([Unilever](/ul-stock/), Nestlé) to industrials (Siemens, Airbus) to luxury (LVMH, Hermès).

The goal is vanilla regional [diversification](/diversification/): a European investor wanting broad U.S. exposure buys a U.S. [index fund](/index-fund/); a U.S. investor wanting broad European exposure buys IEUR or its peers. IEUR achieves this with minimal fuss. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is razor-thin — typically 0.12–0.16 percent annually — which is the iShares calling card. The fund has tens of billions in assets, so it trades with tight spreads and negligible tracking error against its benchmark.

## The MSCI Europe composition

The index is market-cap-weighted, so the largest holdings dominate. HSBC, the London-headquartered multinational bank, typically anchors the portfolio, along with Nestlé (Switzerland) and [Novartis](/nvs-stock/) (Switzerland). The UK and Switzerland together often represent a third of the index weight, despite being much smaller economies, because their listed companies are multinational giants. Germany, France, and the Nordic countries fill most of the rest.

The sector breakdown tends toward financials and industrials — Europe's traditional strengths — with a modest tilt toward consumer staples and healthcare. Technology is underweight relative to the U.S. market, which is one reason European indices historically lag in [bull markets](/bull-market/) dominated by software and semiconductors.

## When does IEUR do well?

IEUR thrives when European growth accelerates, when the euro is strong against the dollar, or when investors shift from U.S. growth stocks toward more defensive, value-tilted diversification. It also performs when energy prices rise sharply — Europe's economies depend on imported oil and gas, and high prices hurt consumers and the euro, but they also lift the share prices of energy exporters (Norway, Russia, but IEUR excludes Russia post-2022) and reduce [relative valuations](/relative-valuation/) that attract value hunters.

The fund struggled in the 2010s when the U.S. outpaced Europe in growth and tech leadership, and when the euro was weak from sovereign-debt crises and structural issues. It staged a recovery after the 2020 COVID crash, particularly in 2021 when Europe's economy rebounded and the euro climbed.

## Currency exposure and hedging

IEUR returns are denominated in euros — if you are a U.S. investor, your returns are the European stock returns plus or minus the euro's movement against the dollar. The euro strengthening against the dollar is a tailwind for IEUR; a weakening euro is a headwind.

For U.S. investors uncomfortable with [currency risk](/currency-risk/), iShares offers IEUR's currency-hedged cousin, usually labeled with an H (IEURH), which neutralizes the euro-dollar effect. Hedged versions trade with a slightly higher expense ratio (the hedge costs a little) and dampen both upside and downside from currency moves.

## Risks and the European context

IEUR is exposed to the cyclical health of the European economy. [Recessions](/recession/), sovereign-debt crises, energy shocks, and geopolitical disruption (Brexit, Russia-Ukraine) have all wounded Europe's growth. The continent is aging, its labor-force participation is lower than the U.S., and its regulatory environment is more restrictive on business. These structural headwinds are baked into European valuations: European stocks trade at a consistent discount to U.S. stocks on [price-to-earnings ratios](/price-to-earnings-ratio/), reflecting lower expected growth.

The fund also carries home-country bias for European residents: if you hold IEUR and you are European, you are overweighting your own region, missing diversification to North America and Asia. But for U.S. investors, IEUR provides that geographic hedge.

Finally, the euro itself is a perpetual risk: political fragmentation within the EU, divergent fiscal policies across member states, or [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) divergence between the [European Central Bank](/european-central-bank/) and other [central banks](/central-bank/) can trigger euro volatility or even existential questions about the currency. During crises, the euro often weakens, which hurts IEUR's dollar-denominated returns.

## How to research IEUR

Start with iShares' fact sheet, which lists the current top holdings, sector breakdown, and country allocation. Watch the euro-dollar exchange rate to understand currency impact on returns. Scan economic calendars for European gross-domestic-product releases, manufacturing PMI, and employment data — these indicators drive European equities' near-term direction.

Monitor the European Central Bank's monetary policy stance, because [interest rates](/interest-rate/) shape valuations and investor risk appetite. Read earnings reports from a few of IEUR's largest holdings each quarter; their guidance on European demand and supply-chain issues is a window into the regional economy.

IEUR is a sensible choice for investors wanting diversified developed-market exposure beyond North America, particularly those comfortable with modest currency exposure and willing to wait for mean reversion in valuations or for European growth to accelerate.
