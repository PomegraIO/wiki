> "The Nasdaq-100, evened out: all 100 stocks get one percent each, so the hundredth-largest company moves the needle just as hard as the first."

*The **Invesco QQQ Equal Weight ETF** (ticker QEW) is an equal-weight variant of the Nasdaq-100 index. Unlike the standard cap-weighted Nasdaq-100 (tracked by QQQ), where the ten largest companies dominate the portfolio, QEW assigns each of the 100 constituents an identical one-percent weight. This weighting scheme forces quarterly rebalancing and tilts the fund toward smaller Nasdaq firms that would otherwise be underweights.*

## Rebalancing and the forced discipline

The equal-weight structure requires one critical operation: quarterly rebalancing. As stock prices drift, some holdings grow beyond one percent of the fund's total value, while others shrink below it. QEW must periodically trim the winners and reinvest in the laggards to restore equal weight across all 100 names.

This rebalancing incurs two costs. The first is mechanical: trading expenses and potential taxable gains in taxable accounts. The second, paradoxically, is an opportunity. The fund is forced to sell stocks that have outperformed and buy stocks that have underperformed — a systematic sell-high-buy-low discipline. In choppy or sideways markets, where mean reversion occurs frequently, this rebalancing can enhance returns relative to buy-and-hold.

## The mega-cap discount and the mid-tier exposure

The cap-weighted Nasdaq-100 (QQQ) is heavily dominated by the largest ten firms. These ten often represent 40 to 50 percent of the cap-weighted index. QEW, by contrast, holds these same ten at a combined ten percent of its portfolio. The capital freed up goes to the 90th through 100th constituents — smaller, less-liquid, less-followed companies that carry higher volatility and higher idiosyncratic risk.

For investors who believe mega-cap technology stocks are richly priced and smaller Nasdaq constituents offer better value, QEW is a mechanical lever. For investors betting on large-cap tech dominance, QEW is a drag. Over any given period, QEW outperforms cap-weighted only when the smaller Nasdaq constituents outperform the giants, a condition that does not hold consistently across market cycles.

## Risks and the concentrated sector bet

The primary risk is concentrated sector exposure. QEW is still a Nasdaq product, so it remains heavily tilted toward technology, semiconductors, software, and biotechnology. Equal weighting does not diversify away the sector concentration; it merely redistributes exposure within the tech universe.

The second risk is smaller-company volatility. Equal weighting amplifies the weight of illiquid, thinly-traded smaller Nasdaq firms. They can gap sharply on earnings or strategic announcements, introducing idiosyncratic shocks that do not affect the cap-weighted version as severely.

## How to research and decide

Invesco's prospectus and factsheet detail the current equal-weighted constituents and the rebalancing schedule. Investors should compare QEW's trailing returns (1-year, 3-year, 5-year) to QQQ to see whether the equal-weight structure has added or subtracted value over recent market cycles. In periods when mega-cap tech dominates, QEW lags significantly. In more balanced markets or periods when mid-tier stocks outperform, QEW can lead.
