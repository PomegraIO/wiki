## What companies does this fund own?

QQQS looks for companies traded on the Nasdaq Stock Market that hold valuable patent portfolios relative to their market size. The fund excludes any firm already in the Nasdaq-100 (the megacaps and largest mid-caps) and the Nasdaq Next Generation 100, a second-tier large-cap index. This leaves a universe of smaller public companies — many with market capitalizations in the hundreds of millions — that are innovative and active in patenting but not household names. The equal weighting means that a smaller company has the same weight as a larger one in the portfolio, a structural choice that creates a tilt toward smaller firms and away from market-cap dominance.

The index methodology is transparent: companies are ranked by the value of their patent portfolios and selected to populate the fund. The equal-weight approach introduces higher turnover and higher trading costs than a market-cap weighted alternative, since the fund must rebalance periodically to keep weights equal as stock prices move.

## Why focus on patents?

The patenting activity of a company is one signal of ongoing innovation and R&D productivity. Firms with large patent portfolios are often active in biotechnology, medical devices, semiconductors, software, semiconductor manufacturing, and other IP-intensive sectors. By selecting companies with valuable patents, the fund philosophy assumes that patent holdings signal future earnings potential or competitive advantage. Unlike a traditional growth-stock screen based on analyst estimates or earnings forecasts — which are forward-looking but inherently speculative — patent holdings are historical and public data, making them objective and auditable. That does not mean patent concentration guarantees success, but it is one way to isolate a cohort of companies making heavy innovation investments.

## Differences from mainstream indices

A fund like this operates outside the traditional Nasdaq-100 or S&P 500 framework. It is smaller, less liquid than the megacap trackers, and carries higher costs due to equal weighting and the proprietary methodology involved. The equal-weight structure is particularly important: it means performance can diverge sharply from market-cap weighted alternatives. When the very largest tech stocks carry valuations that make them the index heavyweights, equal-weight alternatives force a contrarian tilt toward the smaller players, which can outperform or underperform depending on the business cycle and investor appetite for small-cap risk.

The Nasdaq Future Gen 200 has no banks, no financial companies, and a secular tilt toward technology, healthcare, and industrials with engineering-heavy processes. That concentration comes partly from the Nasdaq venue itself and partly from the patent-selection methodology.

## Costs and trading considerations

QQQS is an ETF and trades on the Nasdaq under that ticker. The expense ratio reflects the active selection process, the patent data, and the equal-weight rebalancing required to keep all holdings at equal weight. Equal weighting typically costs more than market-cap weighting because the rebalancing creates higher turnover; smaller holdings are sold when they rise and bought when they fall, incurring more transaction costs. The fund's liquidity depends on the breadth of investor interest; it is more liquid than a micro-cap fund but less widely held than QQQ. Wide bid-ask spreads during periods of thin trading are a risk, particularly in stressed markets.

## Who should consider this fund and how to research it

QQQS appeals to investors who believe smaller, patent-heavy companies represent undervalued innovation and who are comfortable with the higher costs and higher tracking error of equal-weight indices. It also suits investors who want exposure to Nasdaq-listed companies but believe the Nasdaq-100's concentration in megacaps is excessive and prefer a more distributed small-cap alternative. It is unsuitable for investors seeking broad diversification, low costs, or high liquidity; those should use mainstream indices.

Start with Invesco's fund prospectus and fact sheet, which detail the methodology for patent valuation and the selection process. Review the current holdings to understand the size, sectors, and specific companies involved. Compare performance against equal-weight and market-cap weighted Nasdaq alternatives like the Nasdaq Composite or the Invesco QQQ itself to see how the patent-value tilt has performed. Because the fund includes smaller, less liquid companies, execution risk and bid-ask spreads matter more than for larger ETFs.
