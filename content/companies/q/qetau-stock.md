Quetta Acquisition Corp units (QETAU) bundle together the ordinary shares, rights, and warrant components of a special-purpose acquisition company preparing to merge with Jiujiang Lida Technology Co. Ltd., a Chinese manufacturer of automotive protective films and advanced coatings. The unit structure is a common SPAC mechanism: investors in the IPO purchased bundled securities expecting to receive separate tradeable components—ordinary shares (QETA), rights (QETAR), and warrants (QETAW)—upon separation. Understanding the merged entity requires understanding both the SPAC mechanics and the operating business being acquired.

## The SPAC Structure and Unit Economics

A SPAC unit bundled together ordinary shares, one right, and one warrant. The ordinary shares entitle the holder to voting power in the shell company and, following the merger, ownership in the combined entity. The right is a security that gives the holder priority to subscribe for additional shares at a fixed price—effectively a call option exercised if the combined company's shares trade above that subscription price. The warrant is a longer-dated option to purchase common stock at a preset strike price, usually exercisable years after merger closing.

In unit form, all three components trade as a single security (QETAU). Once separated, they trade individually. The warrant (QETAW initially; NAKAW after any name change) trades independently of the equity, allowing investors to take leveraged positions on the equity upside or hedge against it. The right (QETAR) is typically exercised or expires after a defined period. The ordinary share (QETA) becomes the direct ownership stake in the merged company.

From an investor perspective, the unit structure is attractive at the IPO stage because it lowers the effective capital requirement—you get exposure to multiple securities at a bundled price. After separation, the tradeoff between unit convenience and individual security specialization becomes a personal preference.

## The Manufacturing Business: Lida Technology

Lida Technology operates the actual revenue-generating business within the merged entity. The company manufactures automotive protective films—transparent coatings that shield vehicle paint from stone chips, weather damage, and contamination. The product is applied to new and custom vehicles, particularly in premium segments where paint protection is valued. The company commands approximately 59 percent of the Chinese automotive film market and exports to over 57 countries.

The manufacturing footprint is concentrated in Jiangxi province, a central China location that provides labor cost advantages and established industrial infrastructure. Jiangxi's industrial park development and access to chemical suppliers make it a reasonable location for film-extrusion manufacturing. The company operates what management describes as the world's largest solar-film production facility, indicating significant manufacturing scale and vertical integration into related coatings.

## Geographic Segments and Customer Access

Lida maintains a representative office in Guangzhou, the commercial capital of Guangdong province in southern China. Guangzhou serves as a logistics and customer-access hub for Asian markets, allowing the company to coordinate with regional automotive distributors, aftermarket installers, and original-equipment manufacturers. The office handles sales, distribution partnerships, and regional marketing—functions that require local presence and understanding of Chinese business culture and supply chains.

A third office operates in Los Angeles, representing the company's entry into direct North American operations. Los Angeles is the epicenter of automotive design, testing, and high-end customization in the Americas. Lida's presence there is strategic: it allows direct collaboration with automotive designers, access to customer testing facilities, and understanding of North American regulatory and quality standards. The Los Angeles office is smaller and newer than Jiangxi or Guangzhou but represents the company's growth ambition in the world's largest premium automotive market.

## Product Categories and Expansion Trajectory

The company's primary revenue historically comes from automotive protective films (PPF)—the paint-protection product. The portfolio expands into window tints (for heat and privacy), car wraps (decorative vinyl coverings), and smart films (electrochromic coatings that adjust transparency). This diversification across multiple film categories reduces dependence on any single product and allows the company to cross-sell to customers.

The stated growth ambition is toward advanced materials. The company has announced plans to increase nanomaterial production to 5,000 tons annually and high-end TPU automotive paint-protection film production to 10 million meters per year. Nanomaterials represent a shift from commodity extrusion toward specialty chemistry—higher-margin products requiring closer customer collaboration and more specialized manufacturing. This transition is geographically meaningful: specialty materials development and customer co-creation tend to gravitate toward design hubs like Los Angeles and away from commodity manufacturing centers like Jiangxi.

## Competitive Position and Market Dynamics

In China, Lida's 59 percent market share indicates a dominant incumbent position. The Chinese automotive market is large and growing, making the addressable market for protective films substantial. However, market share of that magnitude is usually vulnerable to competition from lower-cost players or to loss of a single major customer. The company's global export to 57 countries indicates it has moved beyond domestic dominance into genuine international business, which creates more diversified revenue streams but also exposes it to exchange-rate and regulatory risks in multiple jurisdictions.

The product category itself faces long-term questions. Paint protection films are increasingly specified by original-equipment manufacturers during production rather than applied as aftermarket upgrades. This shift changes the business model from aftermarket installer sales toward original-equipment-manufacturer supplier relationships—a transition that favors companies with engineering scale and certification capabilities. Lida will need to secure OEM design wins and supply agreements to sustain growth, which requires the kind of local-market presence that the Los Angeles office is meant to provide.

## Merger Timeline and Integration

The business combination is pending closing, subject to regulatory approvals and customary conditions. The SPAC structure means the transaction is already agreed in principle; closing is the administrative step of satisfying conditions and obtaining shareholder/regulatory approval. Once closed, Lida's existing management will likely retain operational control while Quetta's shareholders hold equity ownership in the merged entity.

The geographic spread—Jiangxi manufacturing, Guangzhou regional hub, Los Angeles growth office—will remain in place post-merger. Integration risks are primarily operational (maintaining manufacturing quality and cost discipline) and strategic (executing the transition toward advanced materials and OEM supplier relationships). The SPAC structure itself does not create operational integration challenges; those lie entirely in the business and its markets.

## What Investors Track

Investors in the merged company should monitor Lida's revenue trends by geography (China domestic versus international export), by product category (protective films versus specialty materials and smart films), and by customer type (aftermarket installers versus OEM suppliers). Watch for progress on the nanomaterial expansion and any OEM design wins or supply agreements announced post-closing. Track competitive pressures from other Chinese film manufacturers and larger global specialty-coating companies entering the automotive protective film space. The company's ability to grow revenue, maintain margins in the face of rising labor costs in China, and successfully expand the Los Angeles operations will determine whether the merged entity trades at a premium or discount to its base manufacturing earnings.
