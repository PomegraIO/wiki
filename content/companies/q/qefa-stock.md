QEFA is an exchange-traded fund that gives investors exposure to the developed equity markets of Europe, Japan, Australia, and the Asia-Pacific region — the same broad universe that the MSCI EAFE Index represents — but with a deliberate tilt toward companies that exhibit specific financial traits: strong profitability, reasonable valuations, and momentum in recent returns. Rather than holding every company in those markets in equal weight, the fund uses factor screening to concentrate its holdings in the ones it believes offer better risk-adjusted returns.

## What QEFA tracks and why the factor tilt matters

The MSCI EAFE Index is the standard universe for developed-market equities outside North America. It spans roughly 900 companies across Europe, Japan, and Australia, capturing roughly 85 per cent of the float-adjusted [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) in those regions. Most investors encounter it through passive index ETFs that hold all those companies in market-weight proportion — the largest by market cap gets the largest allocation.

QEFA takes a different approach. Rather than buying all 900 companies equally, it uses three factors — quality (profitability and balance-sheet strength), value (cheaper valuations relative to earnings and book value), and momentum (outperformers over recent months) — to select and weight a smaller, more concentrated basket. In practice, this means it holds several hundred companies, not nine hundred, and a company that ticks all three boxes (high quality, reasonably valued, positive momentum) will be significantly overweighted compared to the broad index.

The intent behind factor-based tilting is to capture outperformance without abandoning [diversification](/diversification/) entirely. Academic research has long documented that, over sufficiently long periods, stocks with higher quality metrics, more attractive valuations, or stronger momentum tend to outpace the average. Factor funds try to industrialize that insight — screening systematically rather than relying on stock-picker judgment.

## How the fund works and what it costs

QEFA is a structured as an ETF, meaning it trades throughout the day on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) exchange like a stock, rather than pricing once daily as a [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/) would. The fund holds a portfolio that typically includes 300 to 400 individual securities drawn from the EAFE universe, rebalanced periodically to maintain its factor exposures.

Because QEFA uses a rules-based screening process to select holdings rather than tracking a simple, predetermined index, it incurs higher expenses than a straight buy-all-the-companies passive EAFE fund. Its [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typical for a factor-tilted or lightly actively-managed product — not as low as a plain index ETF, but not as high as a traditional actively-managed mutual fund. Investors pay the ratio as an ongoing drag on returns but gain the systematic discipline of factor weighting.

The fund is highly liquid: as a NASDAQ-traded vehicle with meaningful asset base, it trades tight [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and can be bought or sold in seconds during market hours. For a large holder or a trader, that liquidity is essential; for someone dollar-cost-averaging monthly contributions, it is incidental.

## Who this fund is for and what the risks are

QEFA suits an investor who believes that developed-market equities outside the US are a worthwhile diversifier within a larger portfolio, and who additionally thinks that [factor tilting](/factor-tilting/) — selecting for quality, value, and momentum — offers a path to better long-term returns than broad-market weighting. It is most useful as a core holding in an international-equity sleeve rather than as a stand-alone investment.

The risks are real. First, [factor rotations](/factor-rotation/): the factors that outperform in one decade may underperform in the next. The 2010s saw a long period in which large-cap growth stocks vastly outpaced value, meaning that value-tilted funds like QEFA lagged the broader market despite strong long-term value returns historically. There is no guarantee that quality, value, and momentum will all offer returns above the index over the next ten years.

Second, currency exposure: because QEFA holds European, Japanese, Australian, and other non-dollar currencies, movements in foreign-exchange rates affect returns for a US-based investor. A weakening dollar lifts returns; a strengthening dollar acts as a headwind. The fund does not hedge this exposure — it simply passes it through to the investor.

Third, concentration: by design, QEFA holds fewer companies than a full-market EAFE fund and overweights its factor-consistent holdings. This concentrates [idiosyncratic risk](/idiosyncratic-risk/). A severe downturn in one large holding can hurt the fund more than it would hurt a broader index.

## How to research QEFA

Begin with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, which [State Street](/stt-stock/) Global Advisors publishes and updates regularly. These documents lay out the fund's construction rules, factor definitions, and expense ratios clearly.

Compare QEFA's performance and holdings to other developed-market international funds — both passive (like VEA, which tracks a market-weight EAFE index) and other factor-tilted products. Ask: does the factor tilt add meaningful outperformance, or does higher expenses consume any benefit? Look at the fund's constituent holdings and geographic breakdown to confirm they align with your own conviction about international diversification and [factor investing](/factor-investing/).

Over any specific period, check the fund's factor exposures and returns versus the unweighted EAFE benchmark. Factor funds will look smart after strong value and momentum years, and foolish after strong growth years. What matters is whether the long-term benefit justifies the fee.
