*The U.S. Global Jets ETF ([NASDAQ](/nasdaq/): JETS) is a [passively managed fund](/passively-managed-fund/) that tracks companies across the full aviation value chain — airlines, manufacturers, lessors, and suppliers. It is a direct bet on the prosperity of commercial air travel and the companies that profit from it.*

## What JETS holds and how it works

JETS combines two broad buckets of companies: the airlines themselves (carriers like Delta, United, Southwest, Alaska) and their supply chain — aircraft manufacturers such as [Boeing](/ba-stock/), suppliers like Raytheon Technologies and [General Electric](/ge-stock/), and aircraft leasing companies. The weighting skews heavily toward the major U.S. carriers because they have the largest [market capitalizations](/market-capitalization/), but the fund's charter is the full ecosystem.

Because JETS is passively indexed, the fund rebalances mechanically to match its chosen methodology rather than employing stock-pickers. This keeps costs lower than an actively managed aviation fund would charge, and it removes the risk that a manager's bets go wrong. The trade-off is that the fund rises and falls with the sector's cyclical fortunes without active hedging or discretionary moves.

## Why investors buy and what drives the bet

Aviation is structurally cyclical. When the economy is strong, business and leisure travel expand, and airlines fill seats and raise prices. Plane manufacturers book forward orders. Parts suppliers run at full capacity. When [recession](/recession/) hits or travel dries up (as it did during the 2020 pandemic), the entire chain collapses, airlines park aircraft and burn cash, and manufacturers slash output. JETS captures that full cycle.

Investors hold JETS for one of two reasons. First, they believe that commercial air travel will continue to grow over time — emerging markets expanding, domestic trip frequency rising, new routes opening — and want exposure to that secular tailwind. Second, they view the sector as oversold after a downturn and are betting on a cyclical recovery. Both are coherent theses. Both require tolerance for volatility.

## The real drivers of performance

Three factors dominate JETS' returns. The first is capacity and load factors: how many seats airlines are flying and how full they are. A 5 percent increase in seats flown with stable prices is a 5 percent boost to revenue. The second is yield — the average price per seat-mile. When fuel is cheap and competition is fierce, yields compress. When fuel is expensive or demand outstrips capacity, airlines raise fares and yields expand. Third is fuel costs, which move independently of demand. A sudden spike in oil prices can cut airline profits in half regardless of how busy they are. Airlines hedge fuel exposure, but the hedges are imperfect and temporary.

For aircraft manufacturers, order flow dominates. A new order from a major airline signals confidence in demand years ahead and justifies capital expenditure. Cancellations or deferrals signal trouble and drag on stock price. Parts suppliers live or die by how many planes are flying and being maintained.

## Concentrated risk and the sector's volatility

JETS' holdings are heavily weighted toward three or four mega-cap carriers, so the fund is more concentrated than a diversified equity index. If Southwest or United runs into trouble, it moves the whole fund. The sector is also, by nature, boom-and-bust. JETS can lose 40 to 50 percent in a recession or demand shock, and it can gain 50 to 100 percent in a recovery. This is not a fund for investors uncomfortable with three-month periods of 15 percent drawdowns.

Regulation also matters. Airline slots, international routes, [mergers](/merger/), and climate policy are all in the hands of the FAA and Congress. Sudden regulatory changes — stricter environmental rules, new labor protections, or antitrust intervention into consolidation — can repricing the entire sector overnight.

Fuel hedging is a further complication. Airlines lock in fuel at forward prices, which creates mismatches. In periods of rising oil prices, an airline that failed to hedge is suddenly exposed to margin compression. These misses can swing earnings dramatically from quarter to quarter.

## How to evaluate and research JETS

Check the fund's current holdings and their weights on the U.S. Global Investors website or a major ETF database. Look at the composition: how much is pure airlines, how much is manufacturing, how much is suppliers and lessors. Understand that a fund heavily weighted to one or two carriers is not as diversified as it might appear.

Watch the airlines' reported metrics: capacity, load factors, unit revenue, fuel costs, and backlog of new aircraft. A period of strong demand, rising yields, and growing orders is generally favorable. Conversely, declining load factors and defensive commentary from management signal trouble. Check the manufacturers' order books and delivery schedules — Boeing and Airbus's backlogs indicate how much demand the supply chain has locked in.

The prospectus lays out the methodology and the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/). Because JETS is passively indexed, reading the prospectus is the main research task; there is no manager skill to evaluate. The rest is reading the aviation industry — quarterly reports, analyst calls, fuel prices, and economic [forward guidance](/forward-guidance/) — and deciding whether the sector is cheap, fairly valued, or overpriced relative to the long-term opportunity and near-term risk.
