*[Jerash Holdings (US), Inc.](/jrsh-stock/) (JRSH) earns revenue by manufacturing apparel and clothing products under contract for U.S. and international brands, retailers, and private-label customers. The company operates manufacturing facilities in Jordan, leveraging trade agreements and labor costs to position itself as a competitive contract manufacturer for brands seeking alternatives to higher-cost production locations.*

## Contract manufacturing economics

Jerash operates as a contract manufacturer—a business model where the company owns no retail brands and sells no consumer products directly. Instead, major apparel brands and retailers send purchase orders specifying designs, materials, quantities, and delivery timelines. Jerash manufactures to specification and invoices the buyer for units produced plus a manufacturing fee.

Revenue is therefore pure volume multiplied by per-unit manufacturing fees. A typical arrangement might be: a brand orders 100,000 units of a specific shirt style; Jerash sources fabrics and materials (sometimes provided by the buyer, sometimes sourced by Jerash), manufactures the shirts, and ships them to the buyer's warehouse. Jerash's profit is the manufacturing fee per unit minus the cost of labor, overhead, utilities, and materials (if Jerash sources them).

This business model is capital-efficient compared to owning retail brands. Jerash does not finance inventory for long periods; customers typically pay after goods are shipped or per terms agreed upfront. Capacity utilization is the critical lever: a facility running at 80% capacity will have idle fixed costs that depress margins; the same facility at full capacity spreads overhead across more units, improving per-unit margins significantly.

## Geographic arbitrage and labor cost positioning

Jerash manufactures in Jordan, a location chosen for several competitive reasons. Jordan has lower labor costs than the United States or Western Europe, reducing the total cost per unit. Jordan has preferential trade agreements (including U.S. trade preferences through NAFTA/USMCA and bilateral agreements) that make Jordanian-sourced apparel attractive to U.S. brands seeking tariff-advantaged production.

Additionally, Jordan's geographic position—between Europe, the Middle East, and Asia—allows flexible sourcing of materials and relatively efficient logistics to multiple markets. The country has other apparel manufacturers, creating an ecosystem of supplier relationships and expertise.

However, this geographic arbitrage is contingent on two factors: (1) labor costs in Jordan remaining competitive relative to other low-cost locations (Vietnam, Bangladesh, Indonesia, India), and (2) trade policy remaining stable. Tariff changes, preferential trade agreement revisions, or shifts in which countries are eligible for trade preferences can rapidly alter Jerash's cost position relative to competitors.

## Customer concentration risk

Jerash's revenue is heavily concentrated among a small number of major brand customers. A single large retailer or brand might represent 20–40% of annual revenue. This concentration creates significant business risk: loss of a major customer or a substantial order reduction can directly shrink revenue and leave manufacturing capacity underutilized.

Customers are also in a strong bargaining position. A major brand can threaten to shift orders to competing manufacturers in cheaper locations, putting downward pressure on Jerash's per-unit fees and operating margins. Price negotiations are continuous, and Jerash must continually improve efficiency or accept lower margins to retain customers.

## Gross margin dynamics and cost structure

Gross margins in contract apparel manufacturing are typically moderate—often 15–25% on a per-unit basis, depending on product complexity and production volume. Smaller orders with custom requirements carry higher costs relative to commoditized high-volume styles. A brand ordering 50,000 units of a basic t-shirt will receive a much lower per-unit manufacturing fee than a brand ordering 5,000 units of a complex jacket with many components and steps.

Variable costs (fabric, labor, packaging) move with production volume, but fixed costs (facility rent, utilities, management salaries) remain largely constant. Running below capacity means fixed costs are spread across fewer units, compressing margins. Running at full capacity improves fixed-cost absorption but requires sustained customer demand to maintain order flow.

## Supply chain dependencies

Jerash depends on timely sourcing of fabrics, buttons, thread, and other inputs. Disruptions in the supply chain—shipping delays, supplier shortages, raw material price increases—directly affect the cost per unit and the company's ability to meet customer delivery deadlines.

Fabric costs are particularly volatile, moving with global commodity prices for cotton and synthetic fibers. If Jerash commits to a manufacturing fee based on expected fabric costs and costs subsequently rise, margins compress. If the customer specifies particular fabric sources or suppliers, Jerash's sourcing flexibility is limited.

## Capacity planning and utilization

The company manages physical manufacturing capacity—the number of sewing lines, cutting tables, and skilled workers. Capacity planning requires forecasting customer demand, which is highly uncertain. Under-ordering capacity risks losing customers to competitors who can accommodate larger orders; over-ordering capacity creates idle assets and unabsorbed overhead.

Customer orders are typically seasonal. High-street retailers need inventory for spring/summer and fall/winter seasons, creating predictable ordering surges. Large sportswear brands have their own seasonal patterns. Jerash must navigate these seasonalities, sometimes carrying excess capacity in slow quarters to avoid capacity constraints during peak demand.

## Quality and compliance burden

Customers expect consistent quality and on-time delivery. Quality failures (defects, color inconsistencies, stitching problems) require rework and damage relationships with major accounts. Compliance with labor standards, environmental regulations, and product safety standards is non-negotiable for brands selling to U.S. or European consumers, increasing operational complexity and cost.

These quality and compliance requirements raise the bar for competing, favoring manufacturers with sophisticated process control and management disciplines.

## Working capital and cash flow characteristics

Contract manufacturing generates favorable working capital dynamics compared to branded retail. Customers typically pay net-30 or net-45 terms after goods ship. Jerash pays suppliers on similar or longer terms, creating a net working capital benefit. However, this requires sufficient operational scale and customer quality to merit favorable payment terms. A customer in financial distress or relationship strain might demand cash on delivery, reversing the working capital advantage.

## Research pointers

Review the [10-K](/10-K/) for detailed customer concentration data and the percentage of revenue from each major customer. Study gross margins by product category and how they trend quarter-to-quarter and year-to-year—compression indicates either customer mix shift toward lower-margin products or cost inflation outpacing pricing. Monitor capacity utilization rates and the company's capital expenditure for new production equipment—these signal management's confidence in future demand. Pay attention to foreign exchange exposure (costs in one currency, potentially some revenues in others) and trade policy disclosures related to Jordan's preferential agreements. Cash flow from operations relative to net income reveals working capital management and the sustainability of profitability.
