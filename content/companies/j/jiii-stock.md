*The **[Janus Henderson](/jhg-stock/) [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (JIII) is an actively managed equity portfolio concentrated on stocks with above-average [dividend yields](/dividend-yield/) and reliable cash-payment histories, constructed to deliver both current income and long-term capital growth through sectors spanning utilities, energy, real estate, and mature industrials.*

## Income investing: yield plus capital appreciation

Investors seek income from stocks for two primary reasons. First, a company that pays a [dividend](/dividend/) offers a stream of cash that does not depend on selling the stock — a useful feature for retirees or those who prefer not to time market sales. Second, high-yielding stocks are typically mature, profitable companies with less glamorous growth prospects but strong current cash generation. These firms have often saturated their [primary markets](/primary-market/), so they return excess cash to shareholders rather than reinvest it. JIII pursues this opportunity, building a portfolio of stocks it judges to have attractive yields, durable competitive positions, and the financial strength to sustain or grow their dividends over time.

The fund is not a [bond](/bond/) fund; its holdings are equities, so capital can rise or fall. But its goal is to marry yield with capital appreciation, seeking total return — dividend income plus price gains — that exceeds [inflation](/inflation/) and [interest rates](/interest-rate/) over a full [market cycle](/market-cycle/).

## Sectors naturally suited to high yield

Certain industries naturally generate high dividends because their competitive dynamics and capital needs align with returning cash to shareholders. Utilities — companies that operate electric, gas, and water systems — earn regulated returns on capital and face slow growth, so they distribute most earnings as dividends. [Real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/), or REITs, are required by law to distribute ninety percent of taxable income to shareholders, making them inherently high-yielding. Energy companies, especially integrated oil-and-gas firms, earn lumpy cash flows that they distribute as dividends. Mature pharmaceutical and healthcare companies, telcos, and industrial conglomerates with dominant market positions also tend toward higher yields. JIII builds core positions in these sectors, though its selection process can favor high-yielding stocks in any sector.

The concentration in these sectors creates a distinct risk: JIII is vulnerable to their specific cycles. A sustained fall in energy prices crushes energy-sector dividends and stock prices. Rising interest rates threaten REITs because their property values fall and borrowing costs rise. Utility stocks decline when rates rise sharply because the [discount rate](/discount-rate/) applied to their predictable future cash flows falls. Regulatory changes — stricter environmental rules for energy, price caps on utilities — can disrupt entire sectors. By contrast, consumer discretionary and technology companies rarely appear in JIII's portfolio in large weight, limiting JIII's exposure to the frothier bull-market trades that can deliver the highest returns but also the sharpest drawdowns.

## Selection discipline: yield and durability

The fund does not simply hold every high-yielding stock. JIII applies discipline to distinguish between yields that are safe and sustainable versus yields that are honey traps — high dividends that are unsustainable and will soon be slashed, destroying capital. A bank yielding eight percent might be offering that yield because it is struggling; a year later, the dividend is cut and the stock falls to re-price the business at a lower valuation. A REIT yielding six percent might be borrowing to fund its distributions, building leverage that will eventually force a cut. JIII's selection process examines dividend coverage (how many times operating cash flow covers the dividend), balance-sheet strength, and payout history to distinguish the durable payers from the trap.

The holdings typically number 40–70 stocks, creating a concentrated but diversified portfolio. The largest position rarely exceeds three percent of the fund; smaller positions may be half a percent. The fund rebalances periodically, trimming winners and reloading in positions where valuations have reset lower, a disciplined approach that mechanically sells high and buys low.

## Dividend growth and the power of compounding

A powerful feature of dividend-focused investing is compounding. A stock that pays a three-percent yield can reinvest that dividend into the same stock, earning returns on the returns. Over decades, that power is immense. A stock that doubles its dividend over twenty years while also appreciating substantially can produce total returns well in excess of its current [price-to-earnings ratio](/price-to-earnings-ratio/) would suggest. JIII benefits from this dynamic, especially when dividend-paying stocks are out of favor — after a [bull market](/bull-market/) ends and investors rotate from growth into income — and new dividend investors buy shares, pushing prices higher and yields lower.

The downside is that this compounding thesis works only if the company sustains or grows its dividend. A company that cuts its dividend crushes the capital story and often the stock price as well.

## Cyclicality: booms, busts, and income flows

During economic expansions, when growth stocks surge and money rotates into technology and consumer discretionary, high-yield stocks often underperform. Investors abandon boring, stable dividend payers for high-growth names. JIII trails broader equity indexes in such periods. The fund delivers its performance advantage during downturns or periods of lackluster growth: when the economy stumbles, growth stocks crater, but dividend payers hold value because they continue paying cash. [Recessions](/recession/) are punishing for the ultra-high-growth names but more manageable for utilities, REITs, and mature industrials.

This cyclical pattern has played out repeatedly. The 2010–2018 period saw low interest rates and strong growth, creating a headwind for dividend stocks; JIII underperformed the broad market. The 2022 drawdown, following the Fed's rate hikes, battered growth stocks but allowed dividend stocks to recover better. The ideal timing to own JIII is ahead of an earnings disappointment that whipsaws growth but doesn't crimp established dividend flows, but making that call is notoriously hard.

## Interest rates and valuation pressure

Rising interest rates create twin pressures on JIII. First, they reduce the appeal of stocks that pay three to four percent yields when [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) suddenly offer comparable returns without equity volatility. Second, they increase the discount rate used to value future cash flows, mechanically lowering stock prices. A utility stock worth one hundred dollars when five-year Treasury yields are one percent may be worth only eighty dollars when those yields rise to three percent, all else equal. JIII's portfolio is susceptible to this dynamic. Falling interest rates, conversely, boost JIII: stocks yield more than safe alternatives, attracting capital inflows, and the lower discount rate pushes valuations higher.

## Tax efficiency and dividend taxation

Dividends are taxable. A stock that yields four percent and appreciates five percent — delivering nine percent total return — generates four percentage points of taxable income immediately if held in a taxable account. For high-tax-bracket investors, the federal tax on dividends can be twenty percent, meaning the after-tax drag is substantial. This is one reason JIII is often held in tax-deferred accounts like IRAs or retirement plans, where dividend taxation is deferred or eliminated. For taxable investors, the tax drag on high-yield income can significantly reduce after-tax returns, an important consideration when evaluating JIII versus lower-yielding, growth-oriented alternatives.

## Building a position in JIII

Investors considering JIII should examine the current sector breakdown and largest holdings to assess the portfolio's concentration. The fund's factsheet shows the weighted-average yield, which tells you roughly what income to expect going forward (though yields change as stock prices move). The [annual report](/10-k/) details dividend-payment history, documenting any cuts or suspensions. Comparing JIII's yield to benchmark alternatives — a diversified dividend-stock index, a bond index, an inflation-adjusted return target — helps assess whether the [current yield](/current-yield/) is adequate compensation for the equity risk being taken.

JIII is most suitable for investors who prioritize steady cash income and expect it to be stable across economic cycles, rather than those chasing capital appreciation. Holdings are securities trading on exchanges, meaning the fund's price moves with market sentiment; JIII is not a guaranteed-income product despite its focus on dividends. The fund's long-term appeal rests on the historical tendency of dividend-growth companies to deliver respectable total returns over decades, offsetting the near-term cyclicality that can create periods of underperformance.
