*The **John Hancock Dynamic [Municipal Bond](/municipal-bond/) ETF** (JHMU) is a [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/) wrapper that holds a basket of tax-exempt municipal [bonds](/bond/) tied to an index, seeking to capture returns both from interest payments and from shifts in bond prices as [interest rates](/interest-rate/) move.*

## Municipal debt wrapped in an active framework

JHMU provides exposure to the municipal-bond market — the roughly two-trillion-dollar universe of state, local, and public-authority debt that carries a federal income-tax exemption. Most municipal bonds appeal to investors in high tax brackets because the [coupon payments](/coupon-payment/) avoid federal tax. That tax advantage makes municipal bonds fundamentally different from corporate or government bonds: the after-tax return often beats even higher-yielding taxable alternatives for households with substantial income. JHMU tracks this opportunity through an index-based vehicle, applying a methodology that weights bonds according to their estimated sensitivity to changes in interest rates rather than by market value. This approach shapes how the fund behaves across the credit and rate cycle.

The fund holds a diversified portfolio spanning [general obligation bonds](/general-obligation-bond/) from cities and states, [revenue bonds](/revenue-bond/) from transit authorities and water systems, and bonds issued by public universities and health-care entities. The holdings typically number in the hundreds; the largest individual positions are quite small, so single-bond [credit risk](/credit-risk/) is muted. What dominates JHMU's behavior is not which issuer defaults but whether interest rates rise or fall, because rate movements swing the market value of bonds by far more than [credit events](/credit-event-sovereign/) typically do.

## The dynamics of interest-rate sensitivity

The driving mechanism behind the fund's strategy is duration-based reweighting. Duration measures a bond's effective maturity, accounting for coupon payments and yield; a bond with ten-year duration loses roughly ten percent of its value for every one-percentage-point rise in interest rates. JHMU overweights bonds with high duration during periods when rates are expected to fall (capturing amplified gains on the bounce) and tilts toward shorter-duration securities when rates are likely to rise (limiting downside). In theory, this dynamic positioning improves returns through a full rate cycle relative to a static index that ignores this pattern.

During economic booms, when the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) typically raises rates to cool [inflation](/inflation/), shorter-duration bonds in the fund protect investors from the price declines that plague longer-duration securities. Conversely, in downturns or [disinflation](/disinflation/), when the Fed cuts rates, longer-duration bonds soar, and a fund overweighted toward them captures outsized gains. The practical challenge is that predicting rate direction is difficult; the fund's weighting logic is backward-looking, reacting to recent rate changes and yield-curve shape rather than forecasting inflection points. In consequence, it may hold maximum duration just before rates spike, or hold minimum duration just before rates crash.

## Taxability and the municipal advantage

The defining feature of municipal bonds is their tax exemption. For investors in the highest federal tax bracket, a four-percent tax-exempt municipal yield is equivalent to roughly six-percent pre-tax income from a taxable bond. This equivalence shifts with tax rates and across regions: some bonds are exempt from state and local taxes as well if held by residents of the issuing state, creating additional yield compression for those investors. JHMU delivers this tax advantage at a fund level; the tax-exempt status of each underlying bond flows through. For investors outside top-rate brackets, or in tax-deferred accounts like IRAs, municipal bonds offer no advantage and may underperform taxable alternatives.

## Credit risk, insolvency, and the default cycle

Municipal defaults are rare but not unknown. Cities and states cannot simply print money like the federal government, so prolonged revenue downturns — a [recession](/recession/) that slashes income-tax collections, falling property values that erode real-estate tax bases — can force issuers to cut services or ultimately default. The Great Financial Crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic both highlighted this risk, especially for bonds backed by revenues from tourism or hospitality. JHMU's [diversification](/diversification/) across hundreds of issuers and regions limits any single default's impact, but a widespread recession that crimp revenues across multiple states simultaneously poses a [tail risk](/tail-risk/) to all municipal bonds at once.

The fund's index constituents reflect investment-grade credit quality, so the starting point is bonds with low historical [default rates](/default-rate/). But "investment grade" is a label applied at issuance and does not update in real time; a municipality can drift from stable to troubled while its bonds still carry that rating. JHMU rebalances periodically, so severely impaired bonds are eventually removed, but that process lags market repricing.

## Trading costs and expense structure

JHMU trades on the [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) at a [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), with [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) determined by dealer inventory and demand. For large positions or frequent trading, spreads are typically narrow. The fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) — the annual charge for management, custody, and trading — is stated as a percentage of assets. For municipal-bond index trackers, this figure is typically modest, usually in the range of 0.40 to 0.50 percent annually. That cost computes as two dollars to five dollars per thousand dollars held each year, paid from fund assets invisibly.

The index itself rebalances quarterly or semiannually, triggering small amounts of portfolio turnover. Each trade incurs transaction costs — commissions and spreads — that reduce returns slightly. In periods of market stress or when municipal-bond volumes fall, spreads widen, raising the cost to execute the rebalancing. The fund assumes these costs as they occur, so they are reflected in the fund's trailing return rather than itemized to shareholders.

## How to study JHMU

Investors interested in municipal bonds for their tax-exempt income should start with the fund's factsheet and prospectus, which outline the index methodology and the fund's investment approach. The prospectus specifies which bonds are eligible for inclusion (typically those with ratings of BBB- or higher, though the index may include some lower-rated debt), the geographic diversification, and the maturity distribution. A look at the current holdings reveals the issuer mix and the duration positioning.

For context, municipal yields should be compared against taxable alternatives using the formula: tax-exempt yield divided by (one minus your marginal tax rate). If that calculation suggests municipal bonds offer a better after-tax return than [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) or high-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), the asset class merits a position. JHMU delivers a broad, liquid, low-cost way to implement that view. The Bloomberg Municipal Bond Dynamics Index, which JHMU tracks, is published regularly, allowing investors to see how the fund's current duration stance compares to the static market weight and to assess whether the timing looks prudent or unlucky relative to the subsequent rate path.
