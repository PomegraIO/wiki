*The **John Hancock Disciplined Value Select ETF** (JDVL) is a passively managed exchange-traded fund that tracks a rules-based strategy focused on U.S. mid- and large-capitalization equities with strong valuations and quality characteristics. It sits in the [value investing](/value-investing/) camp but applies quantitative discipline to filter stocks, avoiding the human emotion and stock-picking inconsistency that often derail active value managers.*

## The John Hancock toolbox and the rise of systematic investing

John Hancock Investment Management built its reputation over decades in the active-management world, running [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) where portfolio managers made individual stock bets based on research, conviction, and judgment. That model worked well when the firm was smaller and information flowed slowly; in a world of real-time data and [algorithmic trading](/algorithmic-trading/), the human touch became harder to justify once costs were factored in. The shift toward passive and rules-based investing across the industry prompted John Hancock, like many established managers, to develop systematic strategies — rules-based approaches that could be transparent, replicable, and cost-efficient while still delivering a different risk-return profile than simple market-cap-weighted indexing.

JDVL launched in 2020 as part of that evolution, designed to capture the [value premium](/value-premium/) — the historical tendency of cheap, profitable stocks to outperform expensive ones — without relying on a manager's judgment call. The fund applies quantitative filters to a large universe of U.S. stocks, selecting those that meet criteria for valuation discipline and quality, then holds them in equal or weighted form that avoids concentration in any single stock.

## What JDVL holds and how it screens

The fund's underlying index — the Disciplined Value Select Index — begins with a broad universe of U.S. stocks and applies a multi-stage screening process. Companies are ranked on value metrics (typically price-to-book, price-to-earnings, and [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/)), quality metrics (earnings stability, [return on equity](/return-on-equity/), debt levels), and sometimes momentum or growth characteristics to avoid catching truly broken firms. The index then selects a defined-size portfolio of stocks that pass the thresholds, typically numbering in the low hundreds, and weights them equally or by some variation of weighting that avoids mega-cap overconcentration.

The result is a portfolio that tends toward mid-cap and large-cap U.S. stocks trading at lower multiples than the broader market, but with stronger fundamental business characteristics — higher profitability, lower leverage, more stable earnings — than the cheapest quintile of stocks. This combination aims to avoid the "value trap" problem, where a stock is cheap because it genuinely is a deteriorating business, not because the market has misprioritized a solid company.

JDVL's holdings will shift periodically as the underlying index reconstitutes, typically rebalancing quarterly or semi-annually. The fund does not attempt to predict market direction or time [sector rotations](/sector-rotation/); it mechanically holds whatever stocks the rules select.

## The value premium: what the research says

The case for value investing rests on decades of academic and practitioner research suggesting that portfolios of cheap, profitable stocks have outperformed expensive, unprofitable ones over long periods. The value factor shows up as a persistent return spread across different markets and time periods, though it is not constant — value has dramatically underperformed growth during the technology booms of the late 1990s and 2020s, leading many to pronounce value "dead." Yet every time that declaration has been made, mean reversion has eventually vindicated the strategy, at least for a time.

The appeal of a disciplined, rules-based value approach like JDVL's is that it removes the emotional and behavioral mistakes that active value managers often make — doubling down on conviction positions, holding losers too long hoping for vindication, or abandoning the discipline when cheap stocks have underperformed for several years. By locking in quantitative criteria, the fund adheres to the strategy even during periods when it is unpopular.

## Costs, liquidity, and holding this fund

JDVL is a low-cost vehicle by industry standards, though marginally more expensive than a plain vanilla market-cap [index fund](/index-fund/), reflecting the additional index construction and rebalancing required. The fund typically trades with modest [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/), ensuring that investors can enter and exit positions without unusual friction.

Holding JDVL means accepting that value investing has periods — sometimes years — when it significantly lags growth, technology, and momentum-driven markets. The fund also carries the risk that whatever value premium exists may erode further or disappear if markets become permanently less efficient and more dominated by passive flows and price-insensitive investors. Concentration in any one sector or size band is controlled by the index design, but the fund is not immune to market-wide downturns, sector rotations, or macro shocks that hit all U.S. equities.

JDVL appeals to investors who believe in the value premium and prefer a transparent, low-cost, rules-based approach to capturing it rather than paying high fees to an active manager or accepting the unpredictability of active stock picking.

## How to research and monitor JDVL

Investors evaluating JDVL should start with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet on the John Hancock website, which lay out the exact index definition, holdings, sector exposure, and historical performance relative to the broader market and to value benchmarks. The index methodology is the core of the fund — understanding what stocks it selects and how often it rebalances is far more informative than any marketing narrative.

Watch the fund's performance relative to broad market benchmarks (such as the Russell 3000 or S&P 500) and to peer value-focused ETFs, especially during periods of tech and growth outperformance, to see how disciplined the strategy remains under pressure. Also monitor the fund's turnover and the underlying index's sector allocations, which shift as valuations change across the economy. The fund's suitability for a given investor depends entirely on whether that investor believes the value premium will persist and whether they can tolerate periods of significant underperformance when growth and expensive stocks dominate.
