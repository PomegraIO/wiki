*The **[Janus Henderson](/jhg-stock/) Equity Linked High [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (JELH) combines a portfolio of U.S. stocks with overlay strategies — primarily [covered calls](/covered-call/) written on the holdings and sometimes equity-linked notes — to generate current income and distributions higher than the [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) of the underlying stocks alone. It is designed for investors who prioritize cash flow over capital appreciation.*

## How equity-linked notes and covered calls boost yield

Most ETFs hold stocks and pass through whatever [dividends](/dividend/) the companies pay. If those dividends yield 2 percent a year, that is all the fund distributes. JELH adds two extra income layers on top of that base yield.

The primary tool is a **covered call strategy**. The fund holds a portfolio of U.S. stocks and sells [call options](/call-option/) against them — promising to deliver the shares at a set price if the stock rises above that level. Buyers of those calls pay upfront, and the fund keeps that premium, boosting the current income. The trade-off is that if the stock surges past the [strike price](/strike-price/), the shares get called away and the fund caps its gain at that level. Covered calls are a classic income trade: you sacrifice some upside to get paid premium today.

The secondary tool is **equity-linked notes** (sometimes called structured notes), which are debt securities issued by Janus Henderson and backed by or linked to the performance of the underlying stocks. These notes are engineered to generate higher [coupon payments](/coupon-payment/) than the dividend yield on the stocks themselves, in exchange for capped upside or other terms that benefit the issuer. The fund receives those higher coupons, which it distributes to shareholders.

Together, these strategies push JELH's yield well above what the core stock holdings would generate on their own — often 6 to 8 percent or higher in a normal interest-rate environment, compared to 1.5 to 2.5 percent for a broad U.S. stock index.

## The income-seeking trade-off: what it costs

Higher [current yield](/current-yield/) always comes at a cost. In JELH's case, the costs are built in.

**Capped upside**: When the fund sells covered calls, it agrees not to benefit from gains above the call strike. If the market rallies hard, JELH will lag, because its capital gains are capped while investors in plain stock ETFs participate in the full move. Over periods of strong market performance, this is a meaningful drag.

**Equity-linked note risks**: These structures are credit exposures to Janus Henderson (or whatever bank issues them). If the issuer's credit quality deteriorates, the value of the notes can suffer. Additionally, equity-linked notes often include terms that limit upside or include barriers; the fund is paying for a high coupon by accepting terms that would limit gains or shift volatility in ways that hurt shareholders.

**Volatility and income drag in downturns**: When stocks fall, the premium income from covered calls and note coupons does not fully offset the loss. An investor holding JELH during a 20 percent market decline gains only a few percentage points of that premium income — not enough to compensate for the drop. In [bull markets](/bull-market/), the fund lags; in [bear markets](/bear-market/), it still falls but with a smaller cushion of income.

**Tax inefficiency**: The high turnover from call management and the constant distribution of premium income (taxed as ordinary income, not capital gain) makes this fund very tax-inefficient in taxable accounts.

## When this fund makes sense

JELH appeals to retirees or income-focused investors in taxable accounts who value predictable cash flow and are willing to trade market participation for higher current distributions. It also appeals to those in low tax brackets or in tax-advantaged accounts where the tax drag is irrelevant.

The fund makes less sense for long-term growth portfolios, tax-advantaged accounts, or investors who expect meaningful market appreciation over the [holding period](/holding-period/). It also requires understanding that the yield is partly a return of capital, not a free gift — the covered calls and equity-linked notes come at a cost that shows up as dampened capital appreciation.

JELH's suitability depends on the investor's income needs, tax situation, time horizon, and comfort with the idea that higher current income means lower expected capital growth.

## The broader context: structured yields in a changing rate environment

JELH and similar income-enhanced ETFs thrive when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) are high and investors are desperate for yield. In low-rate environments, the appeal of covered calls and structured notes dims because the opportunity cost of selling away upside declines. Conversely, in high-rate environments, investors are less inclined to buy stocks for current income anyway — they can get safe income from [bonds](/bond/) or money-market funds.

The fund was launched in 2015, when interest rates were still depressed and yield-hungry investors had fewer alternatives. As rates rose starting in 2022, traditional bonds and fixed-income products again became competitive with complex equity-linked structures, and the appeal of JELH and similar funds ebbed.

## Researching and monitoring JELH

Investors evaluating JELH should obtain the prospectus from Janus Henderson and understand the exact composition of the current portfolio — what percentages are covered calls versus equity-linked notes, what the strike prices of the calls are, and what the current yield is. This information changes regularly as the fund rebalances.

Compare JELH's distribution rate to the underlying portfolio's dividend yield to see the income enhancement. Watch how the fund performs during sharp market rallies to confirm that the capped upside is real and material. Track the fund's tax distribution to understand how much is ordinary income versus capital gain (ordinary income is less favorable in taxable accounts).

Monitor Janus Henderson's credit quality and any news about the equity-linked notes — unusual terms or defaults elsewhere in the structured-product market could signal broader risks. Finally, ask whether the higher current yield justifies the missed capital appreciation and the complexity and tax drag of the strategy for your specific situation.
