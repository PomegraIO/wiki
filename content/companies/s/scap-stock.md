*Infrastructure Capital Small Cap [Income ETF](/income-etf/) (SCAP) is a U.S.-focused equity fund that screens small-cap companies for reliable [dividend](/dividend/) payments and financial discipline, offering investors a way to collect income from a corner of the market overlooked by most mega-cap equity indices.*

## What the fund holds and why

SCAP tracks companies with [market capitalizations](/market-capitalization/) broadly between one and ten billion dollars — the realm where you find household names that fell out of the S&P 500, regional financial institutions, industrial manufacturers, and utilities. The fund's selection screen is simple and deliberate: it seeks stocks that have paid and are likely to sustain meaningful dividends, while screening out those carrying excessive debt or showing signs of financial strain.

Small-cap dividend stocks occupy a distinctive niche. The giant technology companies that dominate broad market indices return cash to shareholders primarily through buybacks, not dividends. Regulated utilities and real-estate investment trusts do pay dividends, but those are captured by other specialized funds. The small-cap dividend universe, by contrast, includes thousands of profitable, mature firms — banks, insurers, manufacturers, consumer companies — whose business models generate stable cash that they return to shareholders as a matter of habit or regulatory requirement.

The fund typically holds between 50 and 100 stocks, meaning each position carries meaningful weight. That concentration means the fund's performance is more volatile than a broad index but gives each holding genuine influence on the bottom line. The [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) on the portfolio runs several percentage points above the overall market, a premium reflecting both the fund's deliberate focus and the fact that dividend-paying stocks often trade at lower prices than growth-oriented peers.

## Strategy: income with discipline

The fund's management team applies financial screens to avoid dividend traps — situations where a high yield masks deteriorating fundamentals and a dividend cut. They look at debt levels, the ratio of dividend payments to earnings (the [payout ratio](/payout-ratio/)), and whether cash flows support the distributions actually being made. This filtering costs in some years: stocks that cut their dividends underperform, and screens that exclude the most levered borrowers mean SCAP will miss some of the highest-yielding names.

The trade-off is meant to be genuine yield, year after year. A fund that owns dividend aristocrats and consistent payers, held long enough, composts those dividends into reinvested shares, amplifying total return. Someone buying SCAP at a dividend yield of 3–4 percent is betting not just on annual income but on the likelihood that those payments will still be there in five and ten years, intact or growing.

The fund's value orientation — its bias toward undervalued small-cap stocks — also introduces style risk. During periods when growth stocks and mega-cap technology dominate, dividend-focused value strategies lag badly. Over the long run, growth and value returns tend to cycle, and investors who can tolerate multiyear stretches of underperformance may be rewarded when value rebounds. But it is not guaranteed, and it is a real hazard for anyone needing the fund to keep pace with the overall market.

## Costs and who it serves

SCAP trades on an exchange like any stock, with [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and commissions that vary by [broker](/broker/). The fund's annual [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically low, in the range of 0.35 to 0.50 percent, competitive with other actively managed small-cap income products. That cost, combined with the dividend income, means an investor needs the fund to slightly outperform its peer group — other small-cap [income funds](/income-fund/) — just to break even after fees.

The fund is designed for investors who want to build a small-cap position with a steady income stream, who have the tax awareness to hold it in retirement accounts if possible (to defer taxes on dividends), and who are comfortable with the lag that value strategies carry during growth-dominated markets. It is not appropriate as a core broad-market holding and would need to be one piece of a diversified portfolio that also includes large-cap exposure, [bonds](/bond/), or other asset classes.

## Researching and monitoring the fund

To understand SCAP's current holdings and performance, start with the fund's fact sheet and prospectus, available from the fund provider and from financial platforms. Look at the portfolio composition: what sectors it favors, how much debt the average holding carries, and what the weighted average dividend yield is. Compare the fund's one-year, three-year, and five-year returns to other small-cap income ETFs and to the Russell 2000 Value Index, which represents its rough peer group.

Watch for dividend cuts or suspensions within the portfolio. When small-cap companies face economic headwinds, they often cut dividends before they cut products or jobs, signaling trouble ahead. If cuts cluster, that is a warning that the fund's income thesis is weakening. Similarly, track the fund's turnover — how often it replaces holdings — because high turnover erodes returns through trading costs and potential tax inefficiency. A stable, low-turnover portfolio is usually a sign of disciplined, patient management.
