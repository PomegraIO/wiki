Stoneridge Inc., headquartered in Novi, Michigan, manufactures the electrical and electronic systems that make modern commercial trucks and off-highway equipment safer, smarter, and more connected. Founded in 1965, the company has spent nearly six decades building expertise in components ranging from driver displays and diagnostic systems to camera-based vision platforms and electronic control units. Its customers are the original equipment manufacturers — Daimler, Volvo, [Caterpillar](/cat-stock/), John Deere, and others — who integrate Stoneridge's systems into the cabs and bodies of vehicles sold globally. It is a business that touches millions of machines in constant use: long-haul rigs rolling across highways, excavators on construction sites, agricultural combines in fields.

The company's recent history traces a sharp pivot that reshapes what Stoneridge intends to become. For decades, Stoneridge operated a diversified portfolio that included Control Devices, a segment focused on electromechanical switches, relays, and control modules for commercial vehicles. These were steady-margin businesses serving a commoditizing market. In 2024 and 2025, Stoneridge made a deliberate choice: divest Control Devices and concentrate on three advanced electronic pillars — Vision and Safety Systems, Connectivity Solutions, and Vehicle Intelligence. The sale of the Control Devices segment raised capital and sharpened the company's strategic focus toward the technology-intensive end of the market, where margins are stronger and growth runs faster.

This narrowing is strategic, not desperate. [Commercial vehicle](/cvgi-stock/) electrification, autonomous driving, and real-time telematics are reshaping the industry. Cameras and radar that see hazards; wireless modules that connect trucks to fleet-management software; intelligent processors that interpret sensor data and adjust braking or steering — these are the next layer of value creation in automotive electronics. Stoneridge is positioning itself to capture more of that value by exiting lower-tech commodity businesses and investing in platforms where differentiation still matters.

The business model is pure original-equipment manufacturing: Stoneridge designs and manufactures to specification, sells primarily on contract through the vehicle-maker's supplier selection process, and competes on engineering capability, reliability, and cost efficiency. The margin structure is typical for the tier-one automotive supplier: gross margins in the low-to-mid 30s, with operating leverage that lets strong volume translate into disproportionate profit growth. Revenue comes largely from long-term contracts with customers, providing visibility several years out, though the contracts themselves are often subject to annual price-down clauses — a structural pressure endemic to the automotive supply chain.

The competitive landscape is crowded. Stoneridge competes against larger, more diversified suppliers like Aptiv, Continental, and Denso, which have broader product portfolios and deeper customer relationships. It also faces smaller, nimbler specialists focused on specific subsystems. Stoneridge's edge rests on specialized expertise in commercial and off-highway electronics — a narrower domain than its massive competitors, but one where the company has built deep relationships and substantial engineering know-how. The company's shift toward Vision, Connectivity, and Vehicle Intelligence is an attempt to deepen that edge by moving toward the higher-value features that define next-generation vehicles.

Revenue remains exposed to vehicle production cycles, which depend on economic health, freight demand, and capital-expenditure patterns among fleet owners. A [recession](/recession/) that curtails trucking activity would ripple directly through Stoneridge's sales. Electrification is also a structural headwind: as vehicles transition from internal combustion to electric powertrains, entire categories of components become obsolete, and the electrical architecture itself changes in ways that can advantage or disadvantage existing suppliers. Stoneridge's pivot toward safety, connectivity, and intelligence is partly a hedge against this shift — these systems matter regardless of powertrain — but the transition itself is expensive and uncertain.

Understanding Stoneridge requires following the company's 10-K filing, available through the SEC, to track whether the three-pillar strategy is accelerating as intended and whether the margin profile of the divested Control Devices business was indeed a drag on profitability. The quarterly earnings calls offer color on customer wins, platform adoption rates for new systems, and the pace of electrification in key markets. Watch also the company's research and development spending as a percentage of revenue, which signals how aggressively Stoneridge is investing in the next generation of systems that will define its competitiveness five years forward. The company's stated target of 8 to 12 percent cumulative annual revenue growth through 2030 sets a measurable bar against which to assess whether the strategic shift is delivering on its ambitions.
