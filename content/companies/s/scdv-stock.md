**What does this fund hold?**

SCDV owns around 100 small-cap U.S. companies — firms with [market capitalizations](/market-capitalization/) in the $1 billion to $10 billion range — that pay [dividends](/dividend/). The universe is filtered by a value lens: the manager, Bahl & Gaynor, a Cleveland-based investment firm with a long history of disciplined stock-picking, screens for companies trading below what they believe is [intrinsic value](/intrinsic-value/). The portfolio tilts toward industrial, financial, and consumer sectors where dividend-paying small firms concentrate. Unlike a passive index that owns every small dividend payer, SCDV is actively managed, which means the portfolio reflects the manager's views on which small companies are mispriced.

**How is the fund strategy different from a passive small-cap dividend index?**

A passive small-cap dividend fund would own hundreds or thousands of stocks according to a mechanical rule — every stock in the Russell 2000 that pays a dividend, weighted equally or by market cap. SCDV owns far fewer stocks and only those that meet a value screen. This concentration gives the manager the ability to outperform if their stock picks are good and to underperform if they are wrong. Bahl & Gaynor's track record spans decades, and the firm has demonstrated discipline in sticking to value principles — buying low, avoiding momentum, selling when prices spike. That conviction is embedded in the fund.

The tradeoff is cost. SCDV carries an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) in the 0.60–0.85 percent range, substantially higher than a passive small-cap dividend ETF, which might cost 0.35 percent. The manager must add roughly 0.50 percent of outperformance each year just to break even after fees. Over time, this is a stiff hurdle. Some active managers clear it; many do not. Bahl & Gaynor has a reputation for discipline that has attracted long-term investors, but historical outperformance is never a guarantee of future results.

**Who should own this fund, and what are the real risks?**

SCDV is appropriate for investors who believe that active management can add value in small-cap stocks and who are willing to pay for active stock selection. It is less appropriate for someone who believes most stock pickers fail to beat the market or for someone who is cost-sensitive and wants the broadest possible [diversification](/diversification/) for the lowest possible fee. In a small-cap [value fund](/value-fund/), there is real dispersion — some managers genuinely beat the index, and some truly lag. You are betting on Bahl & Gaynor being in the former camp.

The fund's main risks are the standard small-cap risks: these are volatile stocks, illiquid relative to mega-caps, and subject to wide swings in sentiment. Dividend-paying small caps often trade at lower prices than growth-oriented peers, which means the fund will underperform during extended periods when growth beats value. From 2015 to 2020, for instance, growth stocks vastly outpaced value stocks, and SCDV likely lagged significantly, despite being a well-managed fund. That period lasted long enough that many value managers lost assets and endured years of underperformance. A second risk is that a small-cap company's dividend can be cut quickly if earnings deteriorate, eroding the income thesis. And a third is [execution risk](/execution-risk/): if the manager leaves, the firm loses institutional knowledge, and performance can suffer.

**How would you monitor this fund?**

Look at the fund's one-, three-, and five-year returns against comparable benchmarks: the Russell 2000 Value Index (the broad passive small-cap value benchmark) and other active small-cap dividend funds. If SCDV has consistently outperformed, that is evidence the manager is adding value. If it has consistently underperformed, that is reason to question whether the high fees are justified. Also check the fund's turnover — how much it buys and sells each year. High turnover erodes returns through trading costs and can generate unnecessary taxes if the fund is held in a taxable account.

Examine the portfolio composition quarterly. Is the fund still focused on dividend-paying small caps, or has it drifted? Are there signs that the manager is moving away from value discipline toward higher-valuation growth stocks, a common mistake among active managers chasing recent performance? And watch for dividend cuts within the portfolio — they signal that the manager's fundamental analysis has missed signs of financial weakness. If cuts accumulate, that raises questions about the quality of the research behind the stock picks.

The fund is held on exchanges and trades daily. Check that the fund's price does not significantly deviate from its [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) (NAV) — the true value of the underlying stocks. Large discounts or premiums suggest liquidity problems or mispricing. In normal markets, the premium or discount should be tight, generally less than 0.1 percent.
