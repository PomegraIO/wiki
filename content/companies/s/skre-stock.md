## What does SKRE actually do?

SKRE is an exchange-traded fund sponsored by Tuttle Capital Management that aims to deliver twice the opposite daily return of a regional banking stock index. If the regional bank index falls 1 percent on a given day, SKRE targets a 2 percent gain. If the index rises 1 percent, SKRE targets a 2 percent loss. The fund uses derivatives to construct this inverse-leveraged exposure rather than owning bank stocks directly. It is explicitly designed as a short-term tactical tool — intended for days or weeks of use, not buy-and-hold investing.

## How do the leverage and inverse mechanics actually work?

SKRE uses equity index futures and total-return swaps to replicate an inverse-leveraged daily return. Each day at market close, the fund rebalances its positions to reset the leverage. This daily reset structure is critical: the fund does not simply aim to be negative two times the cumulative return of the index over months. Instead, it targets negative two times the *daily* return, resets at day's end, and starts fresh. This creates a mathematical quirk called volatility decay. Suppose the regional bank index rises 10 percent one day then falls 10 percent the next day, returning to its starting level. SKRE would have fallen 20 percent on day one, then risen 20 percent on day two. The gain of 20 percent applied to a value that fell 20 percent yields a net loss — SKRE ends underwater even though the underlying index ended flat. That decay accelerates in choppy, high-volatility periods.

## What does the fund actually hold?

SKRE does not own bank stocks. Instead it holds a portfolio of equity index derivatives — mainly futures contracts and total-return swaps on regional bank indices — that mechanically produce the desired negative-two-times daily return. This derivative-based approach is more efficient than trying to short bank stocks directly, which would require borrowing shares and paying borrowing costs. The underlying index typically tracks mid-sized and smaller regional banks across the United States — the institutions distinct from the largest nationwide banks like JPMorgan Chase or Bank of America.

## What are the costs and trading characteristics?

SKRE carries an expense ratio that reflects the cost of the derivative instruments and the daily rebalancing machinery. Leveraged and inverse funds typically run higher expense ratios than plain-vanilla index funds, often ranging from 0.5 to 1.0 percent annually or higher. This ongoing drag compounds over time. The fund trades on an exchange during normal market hours with moderate liquidity, so entering and exiting positions is usually straightforward. However, during market stress — when volatility spikes and bid-ask spreads widen — liquidity can dry up suddenly, making it difficult or expensive to exit when most needed.

## What are the real risks?

Volatility decay is the primary structural risk. In any sideways or rising market, SKRE bleeds value over time regardless of the index's absolute return. An investor holding SKRE while regional banks trade flat for six months will experience losses from the daily rebalancing mathematics alone. The effect worsens during high-volatility periods, when each day's swings work against a leveraged inverse fund.

Tail risk in a market rally is the second critical danger. If regional banks experience a sharp, sustained rally — the opposite of SKRE's bet — the fund can lose money with alarming speed. A 20 percent sustained rise in regional bank stocks would translate to a 40 percent loss for SKRE. If the rally continues, losses compound catastrophically.

A third risk is structural and operational. The fund's derivatives positions must be managed and rolled daily. During market dislocations, when volatility spikes and liquidity tightens, the cost of derivatives rises sharply and the rebalancing mechanics themselves can become expensive or problematic. The fund may also track its intended index imperfectly if the derivatives it relies on fail to perfectly replicate daily moves.

## Who actually uses SKRE?

SKRE is a tool for short-term bearish traders or risk managers who believe regional bank stocks will decline over days or weeks and want to amplify that thesis using leverage. Professional hedgers sometimes use it as a temporary tactical brake against regional bank exposure elsewhere in their portfolio, though at substantial cost. It is absolutely unsuitable for long-term holding. An investor seeking a durable short exposure to regional banking should consider other strategies — a traditional short sale through a brokerage, buying put options, or simply avoiding the sector — rather than attempting to hold an inverse leveraged fund for months or years.

## How would you research this fund?

The fund's prospectus and fact sheet lay out the index composition, the daily-reset mechanics, the leverage methodology, and the cost structure. A multi-year fact sheet showing returns across various market regimes — both rising and falling — illustrates how dramatically the fund can underperform in choppy or bullish environments. Examining the specific regional banks in the underlying index reveals which exposure the fund actually tracks. Finally, checking the current implied volatility environment on equity derivatives and the bid-ask spreads on the underlying index futures informs whether now is a tactically sensible time to use this instrument for hedging purposes.
