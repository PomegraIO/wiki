*The **Simplify DBi CTA [Managed Futures](/managed-futures/) Index ETF** (ticker: SDMF, listed on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/)) is an exchange-traded fund that invests in an index of managed-futures strategies. Rather than buying and holding stocks or [bonds](/bond/), it systematically trades across many asset classes — equities, bonds, currencies, and commodities — using rules-based trend-following and momentum principles that historically profit during both strong [bull markets](/bull-market/) and significant downturns.*

## What is a managed-futures strategy?

Managed futures are a category of investment approach inspired by [commodity trading advisors](/commodity-trading-advisor/) (CTAs) who have historically earned returns not by predicting market direction but by identifying and riding trends. If [crude oil](/crude-oil/) is rising, a trend-following system goes long. If equities are falling, it goes short. The approach does not require a view on which direction is "right" — only on which way markets are currently moving and whether momentum suggests that movement will continue.

SDMF's underlying index, the SG DBi Managed Futures Index, encodes this philosophy as a rules-based algorithm. The index systematically analyzes price trends across dozens of liquid instruments across multiple asset classes and allocates capital according to whether trends are present and how strong they are. When many asset classes are in uptrends simultaneously, the index is long. When reversals appear, the index reduces exposure or moves to short positions. The result is a portfolio that tends to capture large rallies and protect principal during sharp declines.

## The components in action

The fund's exposure includes global equities (large-cap and international), government bonds (Treasuries and [sovereign debt](/sovereign-debt/)), [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), commodities (energy, metals, agriculture), and currencies (major pairs like EUR/USD). At any point in time, the fund is long some of these, short others, and flat on still others, depending on the trend-following signals.

In a typical strong bull market, SDMF would be meaningfully long equities and short bonds (because rates are rising and bond prices falling). During a flight to safety when equities crash, the model would reverse: it would exit long equity positions, cover short-bond positions (pocketing gains on the way up), and potentially go long [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/). The magic, if it works, is that the rebalancing happens systematically before human investors have fully processed the reversal.

In sideways or choppy markets with no clear trend, the fund tends to have smaller exposures and lower returns — the strategy works when trends exist and fails when markets chop randomly.

## Leverage and volatility characteristics

The index employs leverage — it can hold notional exposure exceeding 100% of the fund's assets. This amplifies returns in both directions. A 20% gain on a leveraged portfolio might become a 30% gain; a 20% loss becomes a 30% loss. The leverage is not borrowed cash but rather achieved through [futures contracts](/futures-contract/) and other derivatives that give the fund outsized economic exposure without debt on the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/).

This is fundamentally different from a [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) (2x or 3x leverage on a single asset class), which suffers from daily reset decay when held over periods longer than a few days. SDMF's leverage is dynamic and applied across multiple asset classes whose correlations shift, so the decay problem is less acute — but it is not absent. Holding SDMF for decades and expecting the leverage to compound perfectly is naive.

## Diversification and correlations

The fund's attraction lies in its low correlation to traditional stock-and-bond portfolios. When equities and bonds are both falling (a particularly painful scenario), trend-following strategies often rise, because they have shifted to being net-short in both. Conversely, in strong equity bull markets, the fund may lag because it is also long bonds (which rise but more slowly) and other assets. This orthogonal return pattern makes SDMF useful as a diversifying sleeve in a broader portfolio — not as a replacement for stocks and bonds, but as something that dances to a different rhythm.

## Costs and liquidity

Simplify charges an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) that reflects both the fund's index-tracking nature and the computational complexity of the algorithm. Because SDMF involves frequent rebalancing and trading in derivatives, costs are higher than a simple stock-index ETF but competitive within the alternative-strategies space. The fund is liquid on the exchange, with reasonable [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and billions in assets.

## Drawbacks and limitations

The strategy's greatest weakness: it works beautifully in the rare environments where strong trends occur (2008 financial crisis, 2020 COVID crash, major commodity bull runs) but struggles in range-bound or mean-reverting markets. Technology stocks have spent many years in a choppy sideways band after rallying for a decade; a trend-following model would have repeatedly bought high and sold low. Similarly, the strategy can whipsaw in markets that reverse sharply — it jumps long into the bottom of a crash or goes short into a top.

There is also [model risk](/model-risk/): the rules that define trends might be effective in one market regime and broken in another. A shift to persistently high [interest rates](/interest-rate/), deflationary shocks, or structural market changes could render the algorithm less effective. Past performance of trend-following strategies is not a guarantee; the algorithm's future performance depends on markets continuing to trend in recognizable ways.

Holding SDMF over a full [market cycle](/market-cycle/) (especially a long bull market) can be emotionally taxing, because the fund will underperform simple buy-and-hold stocks during those periods. Many investors buy trend-following strategies at the worst times — after a crash, when they are eager to protect downside — and then hold through a recovery, where the protection becomes a drag.

## How to research SDMF

Start with Simplify's prospectus and fact sheet, which explain the index methodology and the current allocation across asset classes. Review the fund's recent performance over several different market cycles — bull markets, [bear markets](/bear-market/), and sideways periods — to understand its actual behavior. Compare SDMF to other managed-futures and alternative-strategy ETFs (DBMF, QAI, and others) to understand where Simplify's version sits in terms of cost, risk, and expected returns.

Understand your own purpose: SDMF is a portfolio hedge or diversifier, not a core holding. A reasonable allocation might be 5% to 15% of a portfolio to capture downside protection and [diversification](/diversification/). Overweighting it in pursuit of higher returns misses the point; the strategy's value is what it does *differently*, not how high it might return in the best years.

Track the fund's year-to-date returns in major market segments (equities up, equities down, bonds up, bonds down) to confirm that its diversification properties are still intact. If SDMF and stock indices begin moving in lockstep, the diversification benefit has evaporated and the strategy may no longer be worth holding.

SDMF is appropriate for sophisticated investors with a long investment horizon who understand derivatives and leverage, for those building diversified portfolios where protection matters, and for those who can tolerate underperformance during strong bull markets in exchange for reduced losses during crashes. It is not appropriate for conservative investors uncomfortable with leverage, those requiring predictable income, or those who cannot tolerate the complexity of explaining the strategy to themselves and others.
