*The **[State Street](/stt-stock/) SPDR Portfolio Short Term [Corporate Bond](/corporate-bond/) ETF** (SPSB) holds investment-grade corporate [bonds](/bond/) with short maturities — principally those maturing within one to three years — and trades like a stock. It offers exposure to corporate-credit risk without the duration or interest-rate sensitivity of longer bonds.*

## The fund and its holdings

SPSB tracks the Bloomberg Corporate Bond 1–3 Year Index, which covers U.S.-issued corporate bonds rated investment grade (BBB– or higher by Standard & Poor's) and maturing between one and three years. The fund holds hundreds of individual bonds issued by corporations across industries: financials, technology, industrials, utilities, consumer goods, and so forth. Because the bonds are short-dated, the fund's interest-rate sensitivity is low — a one-percentage-point rise in yields changes the fund's value far less than it would change a longer-duration bond fund.

The portfolio is rebalanced to track the index, so holdings turn over periodically as securities mature or fall out of the eligible range. The fund itself trades on an exchange with ordinary intraday prices.

## Income and expense structure

SPSB's income comes from the [coupon payments](/coupon-payment/) the underlying bonds pay. These coupons are collected and passed through to shareholders, usually monthly or quarterly. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is lean — State Street's scale and the fund's mechanical index-tracking approach keep costs low.

[Bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) are tight because SPSB is widely traded. Entry and exit costs are minimal for ordinary investors, and the fund is liquid enough that large positions can be built or unwound without meaningful market impact.

## Why short-term corporate bonds?

Corporate bonds are the debt of companies, backed by their ability to generate earnings and their [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/). Short-term bonds mature soon, so they carry less [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) than longer bonds — if rates rise tomorrow, a bond maturing in one year will reprice much less than a bond maturing in ten years. That makes short-term corporate bonds a middle ground: more yield than cash, less volatility than long-dated bonds.

For investors seeking current income with moderate risk, short-term corporate bonds are a staple. They also serve as a "[bond ladder](/bond-ladder/)" rung — part of a portfolio designed to have bonds maturing at different times.

## Credit risk and rate risk

SPSB holds [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/), which means the issuers are rated reasonably stable by the major rating agencies. But investment-grade is a broad category: a bond rated BBB– is investment-grade but closer to speculative territory than a bond rated AA. If the economy deteriorates or a company faces trouble, bond prices can fall even if they do not default. The [diversification](/diversification/) across hundreds of bonds across many industries does spread [idiosyncratic risk](/idiosyncratic-risk/), but during a general credit panic, even investment-grade corporate bonds can suffer.

Interest-rate risk is lower than for long bonds but not zero. If market yields fall, SPSB's holdings will appreciate slightly; if yields rise, they will depreciate. The fund's short duration means the moves are gentler than in longer-term bond funds, but they are present.

## Who uses SPSB and how to research it

SPSB appeals to conservative-to-moderate-risk investors seeking current income with lower interest-rate sensitivity, retirees or endowments needing near-term liquidity, and portfolio managers using it as a stable, liquid substitute for money-market funds or short-term bonds. It is a core holding in many fixed-income portfolios precisely because it balances yield against rate stability.

To understand SPSB, read the fund's prospectus and review the Bloomberg Corporate Bond 1–3 Year Index methodology. Monitor the credit-quality breakdown — what percentage is rated A or higher versus BBB? Track implied spreads (the extra yield corporations pay above [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/)) as an indicator of [credit risk](/credit-risk/) appetite. And watch macroeconomic data: corporate-bond spreads widen in [recessions](/recession/) and narrow in expansions, so the fund's value and income potential reflect broader economic health.
