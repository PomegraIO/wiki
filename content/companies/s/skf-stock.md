*The **SKF ETF** is a leveraged bearish bet on financial stocks — it aims to move in the opposite direction of the financial sector index at twice the daily amplitude, so when banks and insurers sell off 1%, the fund targets a 2% gain.*

## The mechanic: shorting via leverage

SKF is not a straightforward short. ProShares does not simply borrow financial stocks and sell them (the mechanical short). Instead, it uses derivatives — swaps and options — to engineer a daily reset mechanism. The fund is rebalanced daily to chase a 2x inverse return on the financial sector index, which means it resets its position at the closing bell every afternoon.

This daily reset is why understanding SKF requires thinking in daily chunks. If the financial index falls 2%, SKF targets a 4% gain. If it rises 3%, SKF targets a 6% loss. Day in, day out, reset to zero, start fresh.

## Who uses SKF and when

Tactical traders use SKF as a hedge. A portfolio manager long a diversified set of stocks might buy SKF to short out the financials sector without selling the names outright. Bearish traders use it when they believe banks and insurance companies are about to deteriorate — rising [credit spreads](/credit-spread/), a yield-curve inversion signaling [recession](/recession/), deteriorating loan quality, or a specific banking shock. The 2x leverage amplifies moves, so a 10% sector decline becomes a 20% fund gain — useful for punchy one- or two-month bets.

Long-term holders should steer clear. SKF is a weapon for a specific, time-bound thesis, not a hold-forever position. Leverage decay — the way compounding works against a leveraged fund during sideways or choppy price action — erodes value over months and years.

## The volatility decay trap

This is where SKF bites investors who are not careful. Suppose the financial index trades sideways but volatile — up 2% one day, down 2% the next. SKF would gain 4% on day one, lose 4% on day two. That looks neutral, but it is not: a 4% gain on a smaller base gets you less money than you lost on the 4% decline from the higher base. Over a long stretch of mean-reverting chop, SKF decays toward zero even if the index goes nowhere.

This decay accelerates in markets where sectors swing in and out of favour. Financials have periods where they rotate in (value rally), and SKF hemorrhages capital during those stretches because it is positioned backward. The fund works best in sustained downtrends where the financial sector gets hit hard day after day, amplifying the 2x relationship. It fails in noisy, sideways, or mean-reverting environments.

## Leverage and liquidity

SKF is highly liquid — it trades millions of shares daily — so entry and exit are not the problem. The problem is what leverage does to your effective cost. The fund must hold swaps and options to maintain the 2x inverse exposure, and those derivatives cost money. SKF's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) reflects that daily rebalancing overhead and is qualitatively higher than a standard short-financials fund would be. Over time, even in a perfectly sideways market, that drag accumulates.

## The sector bet baked in

Using SKF is a two-part bet. First, you are betting that the financial sector underperforms the broader market. Second, you are betting that your timing is right — that the downward move happens soon, before decay eats into your gains. There is no free leverage; the daily reset mechanics extract a steady cost. Financials can also have explosive rallies when risk assets recover or when regulators ease restrictive rules, and those rallies hollow out leveraged inverse positions very quickly.

## How a reader would research SKF

Read the ProShares fact sheet to see the fund's daily reset mechanics spelled out plainly, and understand that the expense ratio is higher than a plain short. Compare SKF's performance against the Financials Select Sector SPDR (XLF, which holds the stocks the index tracks) over various market periods — especially note the periods when XLF was choppy or rising, where SKF lagged or lost money. Look at rolling returns over 1-, 3-, and 6-month windows to see how decay compounded during sideways action. Run a scenario: if the financial sector fell 20% in a straight line, SKF would target a 40% gain; if the decline was choppy and mean-reverting, SKF would be smaller. This asymmetry is the fund's core property. Use SKF as a tactical short, not an evergreen position, and exit when your thesis plays out or your timeline expires.
