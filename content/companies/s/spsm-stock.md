*The **[State Street](/stt-stock/) SPDR Portfolio S&P 600 Small Cap ETF** (SPSM) holds the 600 smallest stocks in the S&P 1500 Composite Index — the third rung of the S&P's tiered US equity ladder — offering broad exposure to a mid-sized segment of the US [stock market](/stock-market/) that sits between mega-cap giants and true micro-caps.*

> "Small-cap does not mean small risk." SPSM is volatile, more sensitive to economic cycles, and populated by faster-growing but less-predictable businesses than large-cap indexes.

## The S&P 600 and what SPSM holds

The S&P 600 Small Cap Index is the smallest of the S&P's three main US equity tiers (alongside the S&P 500 and the S&P MidCap 400). It comprises 600 US-listed companies with [market capitalizations](/market-capitalization/) typically between $2 billion and $10 billion, though the exact threshold floats with market conditions. These are real operating companies — not [penny stocks](/penny-stocks-investor/), not shell companies — traded on major exchanges and followed by research analysts, but smaller and younger on average than S&P 500 constituents.

SPSM holds all 600 stocks, rebalanced mechanically to track the index. The portfolio spans all major sectors: technology, healthcare, industrials, consumer goods, financials, energy, utilities, and materials. Because it covers the entire small-cap segment, SPSM avoids picking winners and instead delivers the performance of small-cap stocks as a whole — which means both the gains and the losses.

## Size, liquidity, and costs

SPSM is a large, heavily traded ETF issued by State Street, one of the world's largest index-fund managers. It trades with tight spreads and high daily volume, making it liquid for ordinary retail and institutional investors. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is among the lowest in the ETF industry, reflecting State Street's efficiency and the mechanical nature of index tracking.

The underlying S&P 600 stocks vary in liquidity. The largest constituents (near the $10 billion cap threshold) trade actively; smaller constituents have thinner markets. SPSM's [diversification](/diversification/) and size smooth out these individual-stock liquidity differences, but the fund is not as liquid as a mega-cap fund would be. Entry and exit costs are still low for ordinary positions.

## Why small-cap exposure and the volatility trade-off

Small-cap stocks as a group have historically delivered higher long-term returns than large-cap stocks, though with greater volatility. That premium has not been consistent — sometimes small-caps outperform, sometimes large-caps do — and the relationship can flip for years. The logic behind the premium is straightforward: smaller companies carry higher business risk, less financial stability, and more sensitivity to economic cycles, so investors demand higher expected returns to hold them. Smaller companies also have more room to grow, and some of them become tomorrow's giants.

But volatility is real. Small-cap stocks plunge harder in [recessions](/recession/), bounce higher in rallies, and respond more dramatically to [sector rotations](/sector-rotation/) and sentiment shifts than large-cap stocks. SPSM's value swings with this volatility. For an investor with a long time horizon and the stomach for 20–30% drawdowns, the higher expected return can be worth it; for someone needing stability or approaching retirement, small-cap exposure is less suitable.

## Sector and economic sensitivity

SPSM's 600 holdings span sectors, but because small-cap stocks are more cyclical, the fund's performance is closely tied to economic growth expectations. In recessions, small-caps typically underperform large-caps. In expansions, especially early-cycle expansions when growth is accelerating, small-caps tend to outperform. Technology and discretionary consumer stocks are overweight in the small-cap universe compared to the overall market, which adds a growth tilt.

Individual small-cap stocks also carry more [idiosyncratic risk](/idiosyncratic-risk/) — the risk that a single company's troubles ripple through the fund. With 600 holdings, that diversification is substantial, but it is not as robust as a mega-cap fund with 500 constituents where each holding is much larger.

## Research and monitoring

SPSM is appropriate for growth-oriented investors, retirement savers with decades until they need the money, and portfolio managers using it as the small-cap sleeve of a larger equity portfolio. It is less suitable for conservative investors, those with a short time horizon, or those seeking income (small-cap stocks pay low [dividends](/dividend/) on average).

To understand SPSM, read the S&P 600 index methodology and review the fund's sector and size breakdown. Compare SPSM's returns to other small-cap funds and to the S&P 500 to see how small-caps perform in different market environments. Monitor economic data — recessions, growth rates, employment trends — because small-cap performance is more sensitive to the [business cycle](/business-cycle/) than large-cap is. And because SPSM's constituents are less widely covered by Wall Street analysts than S&P 500 companies, the market sometimes misprice small-cap stocks, creating both opportunity and risk.
