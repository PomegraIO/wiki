*The **ProShares Ultra S&P 500** (SSO) is a leveraged exchange-traded fund designed to deliver twice the daily performance of the Standard & Poor's 500 Index. It holds a mix of S&P 500 stocks and derivatives, rebalancing each day to maintain its 2:1 [leverage ratio](/leverage-ratio-forex/). It trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) under the ticker SSO and is meant for tactical traders making short- to medium-term bets on broad US equity markets, not for buy-and-hold investors.*

## The rise of leveraged ETFs

Leveraged exchange-traded products arrived in the mid-2000s as a way to offer amplified market exposure in a simple, liquid wrapper. Before them, individual investors wanting leveraged bets had to use options, futures, or margin accounts — each with higher costs and more complexity. ProShares launched its Ultra series in 2006, and SSO followed as one of the foundational products. The appeal was straightforward: on days when the S&P 500 rose by 1 percent, SSO would aim to rise roughly 2 percent, and on down days it would fall roughly twice as much. For traders betting the market would move up in the near term, it promised a sharper upside without having to learn derivatives.

The structure evolved quickly. ProShares and competing sponsors realized that maintaining leverage was technically tricky — stock prices move throughout the trading day, and a fund that rebalances intraday faces significant trading costs. The solution was daily reset: rather than maintaining constant leverage at every moment, the fund would rebalance once at the close of each day to restore its 2:1 ratio. This made the mechanics cheaper and the execution simpler, but it introduced a hidden cost: in choppy, sideways markets, that daily rebalancing compounds losses in a way that can erode returns even if the underlying index ends flat.

## How SSO tracks and rebalances

SSO holds a portfolio of S&P 500 stocks and employs derivatives — primarily swap contracts with major banks — to amplify its exposure. The fund targets a 2:1 leverage ratio, meaning it aims to hold $2 of S&P 500 exposure for every $1 of net assets. On a day when the S&P 500 returns +1 percent, the fund's holdings would move +2 percent before any rebalancing. If market moves cause the leverage to drift — to 1.9:1 or 2.1:1 — the fund rebalances at the close by trading stocks and adjusting swap positions to restore exactly 2:1.

This daily reset mechanics matter far more than most investors realize. In a market that zigzags — up 2 percent one day, down 2 percent the next — the S&P 500 might end unchanged over the week, but SSO will have lost money. That is because leverage amplifies both the up moves (+4 percent) and the down moves (−4 percent), and the compounding works against you. This phenomenon, called volatility decay, is a tax on holding leveraged funds through volatile periods. It is worst in choppy sideways markets and least harmful in strong one-directional rallies.

## The real costs and risks

SSO's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is around 0.90 percent annually — roughly 1.5 to 2 times higher than an unleveraged S&P 500 ETF — to pay for the daily rebalancing, the swap contracts, and the fund's operation. Beyond that explicit fee, investors face the volatility decay just described, which has no stated cost but can erode long-term returns substantially. Over a typical year with moderate market swings, a buy-and-hold investor in SSO can easily lag the 2x of the S&P 500's return by 5 to 10 percentage points or more, depending on how choppy the year was.

SSO is also subject to the normal risks of any S&P 500 fund: if large-cap US stocks fall, SSO falls twice as hard. In a major [bear market](/bear-market/), SSO can lose 4 to 6 percent on a day when the S&P 500 loses 2 to 3 percent, which can create [margin calls](/margin-call-forex/) for leveraged investors or force sales at the worst times. The leverage can also make SSO's price move very quickly during periods of high volatility, creating liquidity challenges if you try to buy or sell large blocks.

There is also [counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/) in the swap contracts that power the leverage. If a major bank — typically one of a handful of dealers that SSO contracts with — were to fail or face credit stress, the fund could face delays or losses in unwinding those positions, though regulators have worked since 2008 to reduce this risk through clearinghouses and collateral rules.

## Who owns SSO and why

SSO trades with decent daily volume — typically millions of shares change hands each day — making it liquid enough for tactical traders. It attracts two types of investors. The first is day traders and swing traders who are explicitly betting the market will rally over days or weeks and are comfortable managing the leverage and volatility as part of their tactical positioning. The second, less intentionally, is buy-and-hold investors who buy SSO believing it will deliver 2x the S&P 500's long-term return — a miscalculation that has cost many investors real money when volatility decay compounds over years.

For long-term investors, unleveraged [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) funds are almost always a better choice. For tactical traders with a specific short-term market view, SSO can offer a cheaper and simpler way to express that view than using options or futures, and the leverage amplifies both gains and losses in proportion. The key is understanding that SSO is a tactical instrument, not a long-term wealth builder.

## How to research SSO

SSO's prospectus, available on the SEC's EDGAR system and ProShares' website, lays out the exact mechanics: the daily rebalancing schedule, the use of [derivative contracts](/derivative-contract/), and the fee structure. The fund's factsheet shows the current leverage ratio, the expense ratio, and the underlying swap counterparties. For tracking performance, compare SSO's returns versus 2 times the S&P 500 index return over various time horizons — you will notice the divergence grows with volatility. Any [broker](/broker/) offering ETF trading can execute buys and sells; the real research is understanding whether you need leverage at all and, if so, whether daily reset leverage is the right tool for your time horizon.
