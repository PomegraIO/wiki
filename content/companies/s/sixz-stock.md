SIXZ is a sibling to SIXP. It is the same Allianz buffer strategy—holding large-cap U.S. stocks and wrapping them in a six-month protective collar—but on a different calendar. Instead of March and September, SIXZ rebalances every six months in May and November. That is the only structural difference. The mechanism, the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), and the risk profile are nearly identical.

Why does the calendar matter? It depends on your life. If you inherited money in April and want protection starting soon, you can buy SIXP, knowing a rebalance will arrive within a month. Or you can buy SIXZ if you want the buffer to reset in May. An investor who feels a particular dread of summer market crashes can time the purchase to have fresh buffer protection in May. An investor who suspects autumn volatility can buy SIXZ to ensure a reset in November.

This is not a consequential difference for most long-term holders. Both funds perform the same economic function. The calendar distinction is meaningful only if an investor is timing purchases relative to known rebalance windows or prefers to anchor their protection to specific months.

## The buffer mechanism in plain terms

Both SIXP and SIXZ limit losses to 10% within each half-year window. That means if the broad U.S. [stock market](/stock-market/) falls 15%, a unit of SIXZ is down 5%. If the market rises 20%, SIXZ rises maybe 10% or 15%, capped by the [call options](/call-option/) the fund sold. That trade-off is the entire point.

The funds rebalance on fixed dates. On those dates, the fund closes out the old options contracts (locking in any gain or loss from the hedge) and sells fresh contracts for the next six months. For a moment in time, the fund's true market exposure is naked — it has no options protection, just stocks. That window lasts hours or less, but it exists.

Inside each six-month window, the buffer is sacred. The fund will absorb losses up to 10%. Beyond that, losses fall through at one-to-one. That is not ambiguous or negotiable.

## Who this fund is designed for

SIXZ fits an investor in a narrow band: someone who owns large-cap U.S. equity exposure (because they believe stocks should be a core holding), who sleeps poorly when the market is volatile (because they want a cushion against sharp near-term declines), and who is comfortable forgoing some upside as the price of that cushion.

A retiree living partly on distributions might own SIXZ to ensure that a brutal market crash in the first six months of the calendar year does not force her to sell at terrible prices to fund living expenses. A younger investor with a lump sum who is daunted by [market timing](/market-timing/) might buy SIXZ to blunt the impact of a sudden drawdown while staying invested. A pension fund with a two-year time horizon might prefer SIXZ to raw equity because the buffer reduces the odds of a career-threatening loss.

What SIXZ is not: a get-rich-quick vehicle, a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) substitute, a tactical trading instrument, or a way to beat the market. It is a defensive posture, and it comes with a cost.

## Costs and the mechanics

Allianz charges an expense ratio that includes the cost of running the options strategy. That expense is not hidden; it appears in the fund's factsheet. In dollar terms, on a 100,000 investment, it might be $200–300 per year more than a plain vanilla large-cap [index fund](/index-fund/) would cost. Over a decade, that is a meaningful drag if the buffer never actually protects you (i.e., if the market never falls more than 10% in any six-month period).

The fund trades on an exchange with liquidity sufficient for individual investors but not so immense that large institutional purchases can slip through unnoticed. The spread between bid and ask is a few basis points wider than a mega-cap index fund.

Tax efficiency is moderate. The rebalancing on fixed dates can crystallize gains, and the collar sales themselves generate short-term capital gains within the fund. In a taxable account, that is a slight drag relative to a passive buy-and-hold index fund, though not onerous.

## The limitations and genuine risks

The 10% buffer is not a floor on absolute value. If you buy at the peak and hold for one six-month cycle, a 15% market collapse hits SIXZ for 5%. That is real protection, but it is not perfection.

The cap on gains is an asymmetric cost. Over a full [market cycle](/market-cycle/) with both up and down periods, a SIXZ holder will have given back roughly the cost of the hedge through underperformance in the up periods. The buffer only "pays for itself" if a significant decline occurs.

The fixed rebalance dates create a timing risk. An investor who buys the day before a rebalance has the full six months of fresh protection ahead. An investor who buys the day after has only five months and 29 days. Neither is wrong, but the timing does matter.

Finally, the issuer risk: Allianz must continue to exist and honor these structures. In financial extremis—a severe crisis that threatens major financial institutions—one cannot take a buffer strategy for granted. For ordinary market cycles, that risk is negligible, but it is not zero.

## How to compare SIXP and SIXZ

SIXP and SIXZ are operationally identical except for rebalance dates. If you have no particular preference for May and November over March and September (or vice versa), either fund serves the same purpose. If you are managing money that flows in during particular months, or if you have a tax reason to prefer one calendar over another, the choice is straightforward.

Do not own both simultaneously for their buffer properties; you would pay double the hedging cost. If you own one for a six-month window, decide at rebalance time whether to renew or exit.

Read the [fund prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) to understand the exact mechanics of the collar, the current buffer and cap figures, and the rebalance schedule. Look at the holdings, which should be a large-cap diversified basket, not a concentrated bet. Check the factsheet for the latest expense ratio and performance figures.

Remember that SIXZ is a tool for a specific use case: blunting the impact of near-term volatility while maintaining broad equity exposure. It is not a secret way to beat the market, and the costs are real and measurable. Buy it if the protection matches your needs; skip it if you can tolerate full market volatility without losing sleep.
