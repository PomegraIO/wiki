*The **ProShares Short Financials** ([NASDAQ](/nasdaq/): SEF) is a simple, direct [inverse ETF](/inverse-etf/) — it is built to go up when financial stocks go down and go down when financial stocks go up. It is not a leveraged fund, just a straightforward bet against the financial sector.*

## What SEF does

An inverse ETF does the opposite of its underlying index. SEF tracks the inverse of the Financials Select Sector Index (XLF), which holds large-cap U.S. banks, insurance companies, investment firms, and financial-services providers. If the financial sector rises in value, SEF falls. If the financial sector declines, SEF rises.

This is mechanically simple: ProShares uses derivatives — primarily [equity swaps](/equity-swap/) or short positions in the constituent stocks — to create a portfolio that moves opposite to the index. When you buy SEF shares, you are paying for a position that will profit if financial stocks decline and lose money if they rise.

## When and why investors use short funds

Investors use SEF for three main reasons. The first is hedging: a portfolio manager who holds many financial stocks and expects them to struggle in the near term might buy SEF to offset potential losses. The second is tactical bearing: an investor who is bearish on the financial sector can buy SEF instead of shorting stocks individually, which avoids margin requirements and the hassles of [short selling](/short-selling/). The third is portfolio diversity: an uncorrelated or negatively correlated position (one that tends to move opposite to the rest of a portfolio) can reduce overall portfolio volatility, though SEF's performance will lag in strong [bull markets](/bull-market/).

## Daily reset and return decay

This is the critical fact about any non-leveraged inverse ETF: it is reset daily to match the inverse of the index return for that day alone. This means it does not track the inverse of the index return over longer periods with perfect precision. If the index goes up 1 percent and then down 1 percent (ending flat), an inverse ETF should end flat too. But because it reset daily, it would have gone down 1 percent (inverse of +1) and then up 1 percent (inverse of -1), ending flat but having incurred [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and slippage along the way.

Over longer [holding periods](/holding-period/), especially in markets with significant daily volatility, this daily reset causes drift. The fund's performance will diverge from the simple inverse of cumulative index returns. This drift is called volatility decay or return decay. Investors in SEF should not expect to hold it for months or years with precision matching to inverse performance — the fund is designed for short-term tactical positions, not long-term hedges.

## The financial sector and what drives it

The financials sector includes retail and commercial banks, investment firms, insurance companies, and financial-services providers. The sector's profitability moves with the shape of [interest rates](/interest-rate/) (banks earn wider spreads when rates rise), [credit cycles](/credit-cycle/) (losses increase during [recessions](/recession/)), and asset values (insurance companies' investment portfolios fluctuate with stock and [bond](/bond/) markets). The sector also carries regulatory risk — changes in capital requirements, stress tests, or lending rules can affect profitability.

When you buy SEF, you are betting that financial stocks will decline. That might happen if interest rates fall sharply (widening profit margins narrows), if credit conditions deteriorate (loan losses rise), if stock and bond markets decline (lowering the value of insurance portfolios and bank investment holdings), or if regulatory pressure increases.

## Costs and bid-ask spreads

Like all ETFs, SEF incurs a small [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) for management and operations. The more meaningful cost is the bid-ask spread — the difference between the price you pay to buy and the price you receive when selling. For highly liquid ETFs, this is trivial; for less-traded inverse ETFs like SEF, the spread can be meaningful, especially for large positions. Always check current bid-ask spreads before entering or exiting.

## Risk: unlimited losses if financial stocks soar

If you buy a regular stock at 100 dollars and it falls to zero, your loss is 100 percent. If you short a stock at 100 dollars and it rises to 200, your loss is 100 dollars but your loss percentage is unlimited (you owe more than your position was worth). SEF does not allow losses greater than 100 percent (you cannot lose more than your initial investment), but in a strong bull market for financials, SEF can decline sharply.

More realistically, a sustained rise in financial stocks over many years — driven by strong economic growth, rising interest rates, and credit stability — will drive SEF's value down. If you hold SEF for years while the financial sector rallies, your position will decay significantly. This is not a bug; it is the design. SEF is a tactical hedge or a tactical bet on near-term financial weakness, not a long-term holding.

## How it fits in a portfolio

For long-term buy-and-hold investors, SEF is not appropriate. The fund decays over time in a rising market (which is what long-term equity investors expect), and it introduces complexity and costs. For traders or portfolio managers making tactical bets on financial sector weakness, or for investors hedging an existing portfolio of financial stocks, SEF is a direct, low-cost tool.

If you are considering SEF as a hedge against a decline in financial stocks you own, be aware that the correlation is high but not perfect — in market stress, both stocks and SEF might move in ways the daily reset does not capture perfectly. And if you are considering SEF as a tactical bearish position, understand that the bet has a time limit before decay becomes significant.

## How to research SEF

Start by understanding the composition of the Financials Select Sector Index (XLF). SEF holds derivatives designed to move opposite to that index, so knowing what is in the index tells you exactly what SEF is betting against. Check the current volatility of the financial sector — in periods of high volatility, return decay accelerates.

Review SEF's performance over the past month, quarter, and year against the inverse of financial-sector returns to see the magnitude of decay in actual practice. Compare the bid-ask spread on SEF to the bid-ask spread on alternatives like direct short positions in financial stocks or puts on XLF, to decide which tool is cheapest for your use case.

Understand your own time horizon. SEF is a tool for bets measured in weeks to months, not years. For longer hedges or directional bets, consider alternatives like leveraged inverse ETFs (which reset daily but aim for 2X or 3X inverse returns, and decay faster) or [put options](/put-option/) (which have defined time limits and risk profiles).
