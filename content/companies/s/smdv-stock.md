*The **ProShares Russell 2000 [Dividend](/dividend/) Growers ETF** (ticker: SMDV) is an exchange-traded fund that invests in a curated subset of small-cap U.S. companies — specifically those with a track record of increasing their dividend payouts year after year. It targets investors who want the growth potential of smaller companies but also the steady income that comes from holding shares in firms disciplined enough to raise their dividends.*

## The index behind the fund

SMDV is built around the Russell 2000 Dividend Growers Index, which starts with the Russell 2000 — the broadest measure of small-cap U.S. equities, covering companies with [market capitalizations](/market-capitalization/) roughly between $300 million and $2 billion. From that universe, the index applies a dividend-growth filter: it includes only companies that have raised their dividends in at least ten of the most recent twelve years. This discipline is strict. A firm that stops raising its dividend for even a single year can drop out, ensuring the holdings are genuinely committed to the practice rather than simply paying a static payout.

The result is a portfolio of smaller companies that happen to be mature enough and profitable enough to reward shareholders with both capital growth and a rising income stream. These are not the high-growth startups or speculative ventures that the broader small-cap market includes; they are established operators in sectors like industrials, consumer staples, healthcare, and energy that have found sustainable ways to grow earnings and return cash to owners.

## Small-cap dividend payers: the niche explained

Large-cap dividend stocks are familiar to most investors — names like Coca-Cola or [Procter & Gamble](/pg-stock/) that have raised dividends for decades. Small-cap dividend growers are a far narrower segment, which is partly what makes them interesting and partly what makes them risky.

The advantage is clear: small companies with room to grow their earnings can compound that growth, so a firm raising dividends from a small base might see the payment double or triple over ten or fifteen years in ways a mature large-cap cannot. The income is real — the fund yields enough to matter — but it carries the potential for significant capital appreciation if the underlying businesses execute well. That combination of yield and growth potential appeals to investors who want more than just the returns of large-cap dividend aristocrats but less volatility than a growth-only small-cap fund would carry.

The risk is equally real. Small-cap companies are more exposed to economic downturns, less able to weather supply-chain shocks or interest-rate surprises, and more likely to cut their dividends if earnings weaken. A dividend-growth filter provides some reassurance — a firm that has raised its payout for ten consecutive years is typically stable — but it offers no protection against the inherent uncertainty of smaller operations. During [recessions](/recession/) or market downturns, these firms can see both earnings and dividends compress sharply, creating a double hit to shareholders.

## Concentration and geographic base

Because the fund invests in a filtered slice of the Russell 2000, it naturally carries more concentration than a broad small-cap fund. The dividend-growth screen tilts the portfolio toward sectors with mature, stable cash flows — utilities, consumer staples, energy, industrials — and away from technology and healthcare, which generate growth but often reinvest all earnings rather than paying out dividends. That sectoral tilt is baked in and matters more than in a broad equity fund, where [diversification](/diversification/) comes from sheer number of holdings.

All the holdings are U.S. domiciled companies, so SMDV is entirely a U.S. play. An investor holding this fund is betting on American small-cap economic growth and the ability of smaller U.S. firms to sustain rising dividends through various economic cycles. That focus offers clarity — the fund is not a hedge or a geographic diversifier — but it also concentrates risk into a single economy and a narrower set of sectors than the broader market.

## How to evaluate and research SMDV

The fund's performance hinges on two separate bets: the growth and profitability of small-cap companies, and the specific advantage conferred by the dividend-growth filter. Over long periods, dividend-paying stocks have delivered returns in line with or slightly ahead of non-payers, and stocks with rising dividend histories have beaten peers that froze or cut payouts. But these edges are not guaranteed, and small-cap selection has historically been harder to time than large-cap.

An investor considering SMDV should check the prospectus and fact sheet to understand the exact dividend-growth rules and how often they screen (annual is standard). Watch the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) relative to other small-cap [equity ETFs](/equity-etf/) to verify it is competitive. Track the underlying index's holdings and sector weights to see whether the composition aligns with your own economic outlook — if you are pessimistic on energy or utilities, the overweight to those sectors may not suit you. And pay attention to the [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) and total return over trailing three-, five-, and ten-year periods, noting that those figures include periods of both rising and falling valuations, and so are realistic.

Like any single security, SMDV shares trade on an exchange at market-set prices, and this entry is not a recommendation to buy or hold — only a guide to how the fund works and where its edges and concentrations lie.
