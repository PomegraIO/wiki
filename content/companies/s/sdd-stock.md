## What exactly is SDD?

SDD is an exchange-traded fund that bets against small-cap stocks — specifically, the [Russell 2000 index](/russell-2000/) of the 2,000 largest publicly traded U.S. companies outside the top 500. If the Russell 2000 falls 1%, SDD aims to rise roughly 2%. The mechanism is leverage: SDD uses borrowed money and derivatives (swap contracts, options, index futures) to amplify the inverse return. It is designed for investors who believe small-cap stocks are heading lower and want to profit from that decline.

## How the daily reset mechanism works

SDD uses something called daily compounding. Each day, it resets its positions to maintain a 2x inverse exposure. Here is the critical point: **the fund does NOT simply buy and hold a static inverse hedge**. Instead, it rebalances daily. If the Russell 2000 rises on day one, SDD falls 2%, and the fund sells some holdings to rebuild its leverage. If it rises again on day two, SDD again falls 2%, and the fund rebalances again. This daily reset works wonders if the market trends steadily in one direction — each day's loss compounds. But in choppy, sideways markets, the rebalancing creates drag.

Consider an example: suppose the Russell 2000 rises 1%, then falls 1%, ending flat over two days. SDD would fall 2% on day one (good for an inverse bet), then rise 2% on day two (bad, because the market bounced). The fund ends roughly flat, but the holder has lost money due to the rebalancing costs. This is called **volatility decay** — the enemy of leveraged and inverse funds in sideways markets.

## Why traders use it and when it works

SDD appeals to tactical traders who believe a small-cap downturn is imminent — perhaps weeks or a few months. A trader might buy SDD ahead of an earnings miss, Fed tightening cycle, or [recession](/recession/) fear. If small caps fall sharply and steadily, SDD amplifies those gains. In the March 2020 COVID crash, when the Russell 2000 plummeted, SDD soared — a 2x inverse fund shone in a plunging market.

SDD is also used for hedging. A portfolio manager who holds a lot of small-cap stocks might use a small position in SDD as insurance against a rapid decline. The cost of that insurance is the drag from volatility decay in normal markets, but it pays off in a crisis.

## The leverage and the risks

SDD's leverage cuts both ways. On the upside, if small caps rally, SDD loses twice as much as a single-inverse (1x) fund would. This is devastating in a [bull market](/bull-market/). The Russell 2000 has outperformed or been flat in many years; a holder of SDD in such periods loses money — not just from market movement but from decay.

The prospectus and fact sheets emphasize this repeatedly: SDD is designed for **very short-term** traders, typically holding for days or weeks, not months or years. A hold period of even a few months in a choppy market can compound losses through volatility decay far faster than most investors realize.

## Costs, trading, and the bid-ask spread

SDD has an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) around 0.95% annually, higher than most funds because of the active daily rebalancing and the use of derivatives. It trades continuously during market hours with liquid spreads, but during market turmoil spreads widen. Because it is an inverse fund, it does not pay [dividends](/dividend/); the entire return (or loss) comes from price movement.

One hidden cost for some [brokers](/broker/): some financial advisors will not hold SDD in certain accounts (like retirement accounts) because of its leveraged and inverse nature. A trader must confirm eligibility before opening a position.

## What does NOT make sense

Holding SDD as a long-term position is a recipe for erosion. Even if the Russell 2000 stays flat over several years, SDD will decline due to daily rebalancing drag. If small caps rally, SDD gets demolished. Over decade-long periods, leverage and inverse positioning almost always destroy capital.

Tax implications can also be thorny. The daily rebalancing generates turnover, and the derivative activity can create capital gains that are taxable, even in years when the fund's price falls.

## What to watch when using SDD

Before buying, confirm that small-cap weakness is your genuine conviction. Monitor the Russell 2000 daily — the underlying index — and the fund's daily returns. Compare SDD's daily performance to -2x the Russell 2000's move; it should track closely. Watch the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/), especially during market stress. Exit the trade if the thesis changes — do not hold a hedging position past its useful life.

## How to research SDD

Start with ProShares' prospectus and fact sheet, which explain the daily reset mechanics and volatility decay in detail. The fund's holdings are not traditional — they are [derivative contracts](/derivative-contract/) that track the Russell 2000 inverse, not stocks. Review SDD's performance history, especially in choppy periods, and compare the returns to the theoretical -2x Russell 2000 move to see how much decay has eroded performance. Read academic papers on [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) decay if you want to understand the mathematics. And be honest: if you do not actively monitor positions and have a clear exit plan, SDD is not for you. It is a tactical tool for traders with short time horizons and active discipline.
