## What exactly does SCHR hold?

The Schwab Intermediate-Term U.S. Treasury ETF owns a diversified basket of U.S. Treasury securities maturing between three and ten years. This is the middle belt of the [yield curve](/yield-curve/) — longer than [Treasury bills](/treasury-bill/) or notes, but shorter than long-term [bonds](/bond/). The fund maintains this maturity band passively, adding new securities at the long end as existing ones mature. It is not attempting to predict where rates are headed; it simply holds a ladder of bonds across this duration window and collects the [coupon payments](/coupon-payment/) as they arrive.

## How does SCHR differ from owning Treasuries outright?

Buying individual Treasuries means choosing specific maturities and holding them to maturity. SCHR lets you own many maturities at once through a single ETF purchase. The fund rebalances automatically as bonds roll off, so you maintain steady intermediate exposure without managing a portfolio yourself. There is also no minimum purchase size — you can own a fraction of a share — and trading happens on any [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) during market hours, with continuous pricing and tight [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/). For most individual investors, that liquidity and flexibility matters far more than the modest [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) Schwab charges.

## Why would someone choose SCHR over SCHQ (the longer-term version)?

Duration sensitivity. When [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise, longer bonds fall further than intermediate bonds. SCHR's three-to-ten maturity band gives you meaningful exposure to Treasury returns without the full volatility of the seven-to-twenty year SCHQ. If you want income and a modest hedge against equity weakness, but fear that rate hikes would hurt too much, SCHR sits in the middle. It still moves when rates move — that is the nature of any bond — but the swings are dampened. It also typically carries a higher yield than shorter-maturity Treasuries, so the income cushion offsets some of the duration risk.

## What happens to SCHR when the Fed is raising rates?

Existing bonds fall in price, and SCHR's share price declines along with them. The newer bonds added to the fund as old ones mature will carry higher yields, which is good for future income, but the unrealized loss on holdings bought at lower rates is real and shows up immediately on your statement. This is why many investors hold SCHR (or any Treasury fund) through rate cycles — you earn the coupon while waiting for rates to stabilize, and the income smooths out the volatility.

## What if the Fed starts cutting rates?

The opposite occurs. Existing bonds become more valuable, and the fund's share price rises. New bonds added at lower rates reduce the overall yield slightly, but capital gains from existing holdings typically more than compensate in the near term. This is why Treasury funds are often bought when rates are seen as having peaked or when [recession](/recession/) fears mount — they offer the potential for both steady income and capital appreciation if the macro outlook shifts.

## Is SCHR tax-efficient?

Relative to actively managed bond funds, yes. Turnover is minimal because the fund simply rebalances as maturities expire. Most of the return comes from coupon payments, which are ordinary income at the federal level (though Treasury interest is exempt from state tax). If SCHR is held in a taxable account, the annual distributions are taxable. In a tax-deferred account like a 401(k) or Roth, the tax impact is deferred or eliminated, making Treasury funds particularly valuable there.

## How does SCHR compare to corporate bonds or other fixed income?

Treasuries carry zero [credit risk](/credit-risk/) — the government will not default — so their yield is lower than comparable-maturity [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), which carry the risk that a company may struggle to pay. SCHR's trade-off is safety and simplicity for yield. If you want higher income and can accept credit risk, corporate bond funds or [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) offer more. SCHR is the conservative choice, suited for investors who prioritize capital preservation over maximum income.

## How would you research SCHR as an investment?

Start with the fund's factsheet, which shows the [current yield](/current-yield/), average maturity, duration, and the top holdings by maturity band. Compare SCHR's yield to shorter-duration Treasuries and longer-duration alternatives like SCHQ to see where value sits on the curve. Watch the Fed's policy stance and [inflation expectations](/inflation-expectations/) — these drive the demand for Treasuries. A rising yield curve (where longer Treasuries pay much more than shorter ones) typically makes SCHR attractive; a flat or inverted curve changes the calculus. The fund trades like any ETF, and its performance will track closely to a broad intermediate Treasury index. No individual security analysis is needed — the risk lies in macro interest-rate movements, not in any company's ability to pay.
