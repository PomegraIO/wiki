*The **ProShares UltraPro Short S&P 500** (ticker SPXU) is a 3-times leveraged [inverse ETF](/inverse-etf/) that seeks to move 300% opposite the S&P 500 on a daily basis, turning a 1% market decline into a roughly 3% gain for the fund. Like all daily-reset leveraged inverse funds, it is a tactical short-term tool for betting against the broad U.S. [stock market](/stock-market/), not a hedge for long-term portfolios.*

## The three business segments of leveraged inverse engineering

SPXU, sponsored by ProShares, uses three distinct mechanical approaches to achieve its 3x inverse exposure. The first segment is short equity positions — the fund borrows and sells shares of S&P 500 component companies, profiting when those shares fall. The second segment is index [futures contracts](/futures-contract/), which allow the fund to hold a large notional short position without needing to borrow and hold the physical stock. The third segment is [total return swap](/total-return-swap/) agreements, derivatives that pay the fund 3x the S&P 500's daily loss (or charge it 3x the gain) in exchange for a fee. Together, these three mechanics let ProShares construct the fund's daily rebalancing in ways that balance trading costs, tax efficiency, and execution feasibility across different market conditions.

None of these mechanisms is perfect. Borrowing costs for short equity positions rise in tight lending markets. Futures contracts incur [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and [basis risk](/basis-risk/). Swaps incur [counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/) and ongoing financing costs. The fund's daily rebalancing compounds all three, which is why SPXU's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) of roughly 1.1% per year is materially higher than a plain [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) ETF. An investor in SPXU is paying not just for the ongoing cost of holding the fund, but for the daily reset mechanics that make the leverage work.

## Volatility decay: the cost of leverage over time

The signature risk of SPXU is **volatility decay** — the tendency for the fund to lose money to rebalancing and compounding as time passes and markets churn, independent of the market's direction. A stylised example: if the S&P 500 is down 5% year-to-date but has lurched up and down 10 times along the way (small rallies and small drops), SPXU may be flat or down slightly despite its benefiting from the negative overall trend, because the daily rebalancing locked in losses during the up days and only captured partial gains on the down days.

This decay is not a conspiracy or a hidden cost — it is a mathematical property of leveraged daily rebalancing. The longer the [holding period](/holding-period/), the steeper the decay. A fund held for a few days or weeks is relatively safe from decay; a fund held for months or years is almost certain to lose money to decay unless the market falls in one consistent, uninterrupted direction. This is why SPXU prospectuses in bold letters warn that the fund is not suitable for [buy-and-hold investing](/buy-and-hold/).

## Leverage and forced liquidations

A second, more acute risk is that sharp market moves trigger losses large enough to impair the fund's capital base or create operational stress. If the S&P 500 surges 10% in a single day, SPXU falls 30%, potentially wiping out more than a quarter of the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/). That kind of decline can trigger [margin calls](/margin-call-forex/) for investors holding SPXU on leverage and can force redemptions if the fund's size contracts below operationally viable levels. In the most extreme scenario, during a historic market panic, a 3x inverse fund could even breach zero — lose more than 100% of its value — if the S&P 500 moves more than 33% in one day, which is theoretically possible but has never happened in modern U.S. markets.

## When traders use SPXU

SPXU is held by investors with a specific short-term bearish conviction — a trading desk expecting a market correction within days or weeks, a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) layering on downside protection for a specific portfolio, a tactical allocator making a call that equities are due for a pullback. It is not held by buy-and-hold investors in diversified portfolios, and financial advisors typically warn clients against it outside of narrow tactical windows.

A reader considering SPXU should study the fund's prospectus for the precise daily rebalancing methodology and should calculate backward: if the market has moved 10% in a month, did SPXU move minus 30% as theory predicts, or less, because of decay? That backward calculation is the best guide to whether the fund is performing as expected. Options on the S&P 500 or short equity index futures offer alternatives for bearish positioning that avoid the decay penalty altogether, worth comparing before committing to a daily-reset leveraged inverse ETF.
