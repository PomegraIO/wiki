Skillsoft operates one of the largest software-as-a-service platforms for enterprise learning and workforce development. The company sells subscriptions to Fortune 1000 companies and mid-market organizations, offering on-demand courses, live instructor-led training, hands-on labs, and skill assessments—all increasingly powered by artificial intelligence. What defines Skillsoft's position in a crowded field is not just the breadth of its learning library but the explicit pivot toward solving what enterprises now call the AI skills crisis: organizations know their workforces need to understand and work with new AI tools, yet the supply of training and the practical ability to reskill at scale remain constrained.

## The AI inflection: what has shifted

Skillsoft's moment centers on an acute market pressure. In 2024 and 2025, enterprise demand for AI literacy exploded. Employers across sectors—finance, retail, manufacturing, healthcare—suddenly faced the reality that their workforce either upskills on generative AI tools or risks obsolescence. Skillsoft has responded by embedding AI throughout its platform: not just teaching *about* AI, but using AI to power the platform itself. The company reported a 74 percent year-over-year surge in learners taking AI courses in 2025, a 158 percent jump in AI learning hours, and flagged that AI now drives over half of its content production. This is not a marginal trend for Skillsoft; it is the center of gravity of the business right now.

The shift has also redefined who pays attention to the company. Large enterprises have moved from treating learning as a nice-to-have HR tool to viewing it as essential infrastructure for keeping their workforce current. Contract values have grown and renewal rates have strengthened as a result. At the same time, the platform's reach has expanded through partnerships: Skillsoft announced a strategic partnership with edX (a university-led online education provider) in late 2025, aiming to integrate university-developed courses directly into the Skillsoft platform. That move signals both Skillsoft's confidence in its market position and its hunger to deepen the perception that the platform is the central place where enterprise employees go to reskill.

## How Skillsoft makes money

Skillsoft operates a straightforward subscription model. Organizations purchase seats or licenses, typically on annual or multi-year contracts, that give their employees access to the Skillsoft Percipio platform and its content library. The library spans thousands of courses in technical skills (software development, cloud platforms, cybersecurity), business skills (leadership, communication, project management), and digital tools. Much of that content is created or licensed by Skillsoft; increasingly, it is also synthesized or adapted by AI models Skillsoft operates.

The pricing model is flexible by design. Customers can buy per-seat licenses for specific employee populations or negotiate broader institutional access. Renewal rates matter more than headline growth in SaaS businesses, because a contract renewed at the same price is cheap growth. Skillsoft has historically kept renewal rates high by making itself sticky: if tens of thousands of an enterprise's employees build learning transcripts and track progress on the platform, replacing it is costly. The arrival of AI-driven features—personalized learning recommendations, adaptive content pathways, AI tutors that answer questions about course material—has made stickiness even more pronounced.

## The competitive terrain and the moat

Skillsoft competes in a landscape populated by smaller specialized platforms and by larger generalist HR software vendors, each eyeing the learning space. LinkedIn Learning reaches employees who are on the LinkedIn network. Coursera and Udemy target both individual learners and bulk customer licensing. Competitors like Degreed and Cornerstone also sell into the enterprise segment. Yet Skillsoft has advantages that explain its broad penetration: the installed base of 95 million learners represents years of data and habit; the partnership with edX brings university-grade content that smaller competitors cannot easily match; and the shift to AI-powered authoring raises the bar for cost-efficient content production, a domain where Skillsoft's scale now compounds its edge.

That said, the moat is not unassailable. A well-resourced competitor—or an existing HR software platform expanding into learning—could theoretically replicate the offering. What Skillsoft relies on instead is the switching cost built into deep organizational adoption. Once a large customer has integrated Skillsoft into its talent development process, retrained its HR teams on the platform, and built learning budgets around it, the pain and expense of switching become real. That is the strongest defense most SaaS companies have.

## The pressure and the risks

Skillsoft's dependence on the AI inflection is also a risk. If the urgency of AI reskilling fades—if enterprises decide the wave has crested and in-house training becomes adequate—the growth tailwind weakens. The company's growth has been real, but Skillsoft is not yet a large-cap blue chip, and sentiment in the software space can shift quickly.

A second risk is content and brand liability. Skillsoft represents itself as a trusted partner in workforce development. If its AI-powered content generation produces flawed or biased material, or if its learning recommendations steer employees toward outcomes that don't serve them, the brand damage could be material. Quality control at scale is a constant challenge for any platform that relies heavily on algorithmic content synthesis.

Third is the integration of edX. Strategic partnerships are often announced at peak enthusiasm and executed with friction. If Skillsoft and edX struggle to migrate university content onto the Skillsoft platform in a way that feels natural to enterprise customers, the expected synergies could disappoint. That would be visible first in renewal rates or in slower growth in technology courses.

## How to research Skillsoft as an investment

Skillsoft files its annual 10-K with the SEC (CIK 0001774675), which details revenue growth, customer concentration, churn, and the breakdown of spending across customer [acquisition](/acquisition/) and content production. The quarterly earnings calls are the best place to track the three metrics that matter most: customer count growth, net revenue retention (the extent to which existing customers spend more year over year), and gross margins. The AI content production initiative should show up as an improving gross margin if Skillsoft is genuinely achieving cost benefits from algorithmic content creation; if margins compress, it signals either heavy reinvestment or execution friction.

Watch also for any change in the balance of new business versus renewal growth. A company can show topline growth while churn accelerates and profitability weakens; quarterly earnings reports make both visible if you read the customer metrics alongside the revenue numbers. Finally, track the edX partnership through conference calls and customer win announcements. If Skillsoft is integrating the content and winning logo deals using the edX partnership as the anchor, the signal is positive. If the partnership remains a footnote, it likely did not move the needle.
