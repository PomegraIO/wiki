STXU uses borrowed capital and index derivatives to double the daily moves of an underlying index. On days when the index rises 1%, STXU targets a 2% gain. On days when the index falls 1%, STXU targets a 2% loss. The leverage is embedded in the fund itself — the fund borrows money or enters into futures and swaps contracts to hold positions worth twice the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/). Every night at market close, the fund rebalances: if the index moved up during the day, the fund reduces its position back to 2x the index value; if the index fell, it increases the position back to 2x. This mechanical nightly reset is what separates STXU from simply holding the index on margin.

## The mathematics of daily reset and volatility decay

A 2x leveraged fund does not deliver twice the *long-term* return. It delivers twice the *daily* return. Those are not the same, and the difference is volatility decay — a mathematical drain that grows with market choppiness.

Consider a simple example: an index that gains 10% on Monday, then loses 10% on Tuesday, ending flat. A trader holding the index breaks even. A trader holding a 2x leveraged fund gains 20% Monday (2x the 10% gain), then loses 20% Tuesday (2x the 10% loss). The Monday gain is 20% of $100 = $20, landing at $120. The Tuesday loss is 20% of $120 = $24, landing at $96. The trader loses 4% even though the index is flat. This is volatility decay — pure mathematics, not a hidden fee. The longer the [holding period](/holding-period/) and the higher the volatility, the worse the decay becomes.

In a strongly trending market — straight gains day after day — the fund approaches the mathematical doubling of returns. In any sideways, choppy, or reversing market, decay is the investor's enemy. Most equity markets spend more time in chop and drawdown cycles than in clean uptrends, which is why [leveraged ETFs](/leveraged-etf/) are designed for *tactical* positions measured in days or weeks, not strategic core holdings.

## Why these funds exist and how traders use them

Leveraged ETFs were created for traders who want amplified exposure without [broker](/broker/) margin. Instead of calling a broker and putting up margin to borrow money for twice-sized bets, a trader can buy shares of a leveraged fund. The leverage is inside the fund; no [margin call](/margin-call-forex/) can be triggered, and interest costs are rolled into the fund's operational expenses rather than charged separately by a broker.

A trader who expects the market to rally sharply over the next few days might buy STXU in the morning and sell it a week later. If the index rallies 5% over that week without much choppiness, the fund might deliver close to a 10% gain (roughly 2x the 5%). If the index rallies 5% but with significant daily reversals, the fund might deliver only 8% or 7%, or in extreme cases might even decline if volatility is high enough.

These funds are not suitable for passive investors. Holding STXU for months or years — even if the underlying index trends upward — is almost certainly a wealth-destroying trade because volatility decay will eat away returns relative to holding the unlevered index or a simple 2:1 margin position renewed infrequently.

## The daily rebalancing mechanism

At the close of each trading day, STXU's fund manager calculates the net asset value of all underlying holdings, computes what 2x that value would be, and adjusts the fund's leverage accordingly. If the underlying index rallied during the day, the fund trims its position down to 2x. If the index fell, it adds leverage back up to 2x. This is an automatic mechanical process, not a decision by the fund manager.

This nightly rebalancing is what creates the volatility decay. By selling high (when the index rallies) and buying low (when the index falls), the fund locks in losses on reversals. Over many trading days, these tiny losses compound.

## Costs embedded in the fund

STXU carries explicit costs (the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), stated in the prospectus) and implicit costs (interest on borrowed money, [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) from frequent trading, fees for index futures and swaps). These costs do not appear as a single line item; they are absorbed into the fund's operational drag. An investor should compare STXU's actual performance to double the performance of the underlying index. If the fund is consistently lagging (earning less than 2x the index return), that shortfall is the cost of leverage and volatility decay.

## Risks and suitability

STXU amplifies both gains and losses. If the underlying index falls 10%, STXU will fall approximately 20%. For a trader expecting a sharp rally and committing capital for a few days, that amplification is the whole point. For a retiree, a young saver, or anyone not actively managing a trading position, STXU is a wealth-destroying trap.

Brokerages increasingly display risk warnings when leveraged products are purchased, and for good reason. Studies of leveraged ETF performance show that retail investors who hold these products for extended periods reliably underperform, because volatility decay compounds over time. The fund is not broken; the investor's time horizon is.

## How to research STXU

Read the prospectus in full before buying. The prospectus explicitly warns that the fund aims to deliver twice the *daily* return, and that long-term returns may lag that doubling due to daily reset mechanics and volatility decay. The fact sheet shows the underlying index being tracked and the fund's current expense ratio and holdings (if any specific positions are held; some leveraged funds hold mostly derivatives).

Monitor the fund's actual performance versus 2x the underlying index performance. Over a few days or a week, the fund should track close to the mathematical doubling (minus a small amount for costs). Over months, watch for the decay to become visible — the fund will lag the mathematical doubling by an increasing margin.

If you hold STXU, treat it as a tactical position with a clear exit plan. A holding period of weeks or months is stretching the product's design; months to years is almost certainly a mistake.
