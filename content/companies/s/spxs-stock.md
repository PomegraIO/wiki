*A **Direxion Daily S&P 500 Bear 3X ETF** (ticker SPXS) is a 3-times leveraged inverse exchange-traded fund that bets against the S&P 500 on a daily reset basis. It is designed to deliver roughly negative 300% of the S&P 500's daily return — three times the broad market's loss, or three times its gain backward — making it a tool for tactical shorting in the event of market sell-offs, not a buy-and-hold investment.*

## What this ETF tracks and how it works

SPXS tracks the S&P 500, the broad index of the 500 largest publicly traded U.S. companies, but it does so in reverse with a 3-to-1 multiplier. If the S&P 500 falls 1% in a day, SPXS should gain roughly 3%. If the S&P 500 rises 1%, SPXS should drop roughly 3%. The mechanism uses index futures, swaps, and short equity positions to achieve that inverse move, and it resets daily — meaning the fund is recalibrated each trading day to maintain the 3x inverse relationship relative to that day's close, not the original opening position.

That daily reset is the crucial distinction. Over a single trading day, the leverage works predictably. Over weeks and months, however, market volatility erodes returns in a process called **decay**. If the S&P 500 rises 50%, then falls 50% (returning to its start), the index ends unchanged; but SPXS, being leveraged daily, will have lost money on both the way up and the way down. This pattern — small daily moves across many days — causes the fund to underperform its theoretical 3x inverse target over longer periods. SPXS is therefore strictly a short-term tactical vehicle, not a hedge for a multi-year portfolio.

## The real risks

The most obvious risk is losing money if the market rises, which defeats the purpose of most investors who buy protective bearish positions. That is intentional — the fund is for traders who expect a near-term sell-off, not for buy-and-hold defensive holdings.

Volatility decay is more subtle and more damaging than a simple market rise. Even if the market ends the year down 10%, repeated daily rebalancing may cause SPXS to post a smaller loss, or even a gain, than the simple 3x-inverse math would suggest. The fund bleeds value in sideways, choppy markets where the broad index lurches up and down without a clear direction. The longer the [holding period](/holding-period/), the steeper the decay.

Leverage also amplifies intraday volatility. SPXS can swing 5–10% or more in a single trading session during market panic, which can trigger forced selling if the fund is held on margin or within constrained risk-management systems.

## Who holds SPXS and how to read it

SPXS is held primarily by [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/), options traders, and sophisticated retail investors using it as a tactical short-term bet or a temporary hedge against a perceived imminent market correction. Long-term buy-and-hold investors in diversified portfolios have no business owning it — it will almost certainly lose money against their holding period.

A reader tracking SPXS should understand that the fund's daily price reflects the market's short-term direction and volatility texture, not a fundamental thesis about stock valuations. If SPXS is surging, it means the S&P 500 is dropping sharply or the market is expected to drop soon. If SPXS is collapsing, the S&P 500 is rallying. The [fund prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) and the sponsor's fact sheet clarify the daily reset mechanics and the impact of leverage. Most important is to read the fine print on [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) — the ongoing cost of holding leveraged positions — and to run simple decay calculations before committing capital for more than a few weeks.
