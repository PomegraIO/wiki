Sun Country Airlines is an airline that does two things at once: it operates scheduled passenger flights on regular routes, mostly to leisure destinations, and it runs a separate charter-flight business renting out entire aircraft to tour groups and corporations. That dual model is unusual. Most airlines either run scheduled service or charter operations, but rarely both. For Sun Country, the combination gives it flexibility — charter revenue helps fill idle capacity during slow seasons, while scheduled service gives it predictability and brand visibility.

## From charter specialist to hybrid airline

Sun Country started in 1984 as a charter operator based in Minneapolis. For decades it flew tour groups and private charters, building a reputation for reliability in the niche market of group travel. The airline was privately held through most of its life, catering to package-tour companies and corporate teams. That model was profitable but narrow. In 2021, the company went public and began to pivot. It launched scheduled service, adding regular flights from Minnesota to leisure destinations like Las Vegas, Fort Lauderdale, and the Caribbean. The scheduled routes gave Sun Country a second growth lane and a way to reach individual leisure travelers, not just group bookings.

## How it makes money

Scheduled service and charter are very different businesses. Scheduled flights run on a fixed timetable whether the seats fill or not, so they require discipline on pricing and route selection. The airline must compete with larger carriers on price while managing the risk that demand might be soft. Charter flights work the opposite way: a corporate client or tour operator contracts for an entire aircraft and pays whether it is half-full or packed. That creates a very high-margin revenue stream, but it depends on the airline having unbooked capacity and on customers willing to pay the charter price.

Sun Country's strategy is to operate the two in tandem. Off-peak capacity gets chartered out. Peak travel days go to scheduled service. In theory, this smooths revenue and keeps aircraft utilization high. In practice, the airline has to guess correctly about which weeks will charter well and which will sell via scheduled flights, which is harder than it sounds.

The airline also operates some service for other carriers — called capacity purchase agreements — where Sun Country flies a route on behalf of a larger airline and splits the revenue. This too is a way to fill planes that might otherwise fly empty.

## The economics of regional airlines

Regional airlines like Sun Country operate in a pinched margin environment. Fuel is one of the largest costs and moves with global oil prices. Labor is another major expense, and pilot and flight-attendant wages have risen sharply in recent years as competition for crew tightened. Maintenance, airport fees, insurance, and catering all add up. The profit margin is razor-thin, often in the low single digits, which means the airline must execute flawlessly on cost to survive a downturn.

Sun Country's size — far smaller than Southwest, United, or American — means it has less negotiating power with aircraft suppliers and less ability to absorb a fuel-price spike or a sudden drop in leisure travel. It also means the airline cannot afford the slick loyalty programs and broad route networks that larger carriers use to lock in premium customers. The niche of leisure travel to warm destinations is competitive, and so is charter.

## Risks and the path forward

The airline faces standard airline risks: exposure to fuel prices, labor costs, and demand shocks. Leisure travel, the core market, is cyclical — it falls sharply when consumers feel economically pinched. The charter business depends on [discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending/) by tour groups and corporations, which dries up in a [recession](/recession/). Sun Country also competes with low-cost carriers like Southwest and Spirit, which operate on thinner margins and have more scale.

A deeper question is whether the hybrid model actually works or is just a stopgap. If scheduled service fails to reach the scale of profitability, the airline could be forced to shed it and revert to pure charter. If charter demand weakens, Sun Country loses the buffer it was designed to provide. The airline must prove it can make money on both legs simultaneously, not just substitute one for the other.

For someone tracking Sun Country as an investment, the key metrics are load factor (percentage of seats filled), available seat miles, revenue per available seat mile, and cost per available seat mile. The quarterly earnings calls reveal demand trends and fuel-price exposure. The 10-K filing details the charter customer mix and the health of the scheduled routes. Watch whether the airline is adding new markets or retreating, whether fuel-hedging contracts are protecting margins, and whether charter revenue is stable or lumpy.
