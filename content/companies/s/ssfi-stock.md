**SSFI** (Day Hagan Smart Sector Fixed [Income ETF](/income-etf/)) is a rules-based fixed-income fund that applies a quantitative rotation strategy to [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/). Rather than holding a static basket, the fund rotates its sector allocations — tilting more to industrials [bonds](/bond/) in one environment, more to utility bonds in another — based on relative-value metrics and yield signals. It is designed for investors seeking fixed-income exposure that adapts mechanically to changing credit and economic conditions.

## The index at work

The fund tracks the Day Hagan Smart Sector Income index, built by a quantitative framework that evaluates the relative attractiveness of five key bond sectors: industrials, utilities, financials, consumer, and energy. The methodology examines spreads (the yield premium [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) offer over Treasuries), valuations relative to history, and credit fundamentals to determine whether a sector is cheap or rich.

When the model flags industrials as particularly cheap relative to their risk, the fund overweights that sector. When utilities have priced in exceptional value already, it trims that position. The rotation happens mechanically, without portfolio manager discretion — the rules determine the allocation. In practice, the fund maintains a continuous exposure across all five sectors, but the weights shift quarterly or when the signals change.

All holdings are investment-grade — rated BBB− or higher by major rating agencies — so the strategy steers clear of high-yield ([junk) bonds](/junk-bond/). This keeps [credit risk](/credit-risk/) moderate and the fund's volatility more manageable than a high-yield or unconstrained bond fund would offer.

## Cost and mechanics

The fund charges an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) typical of rules-based bond strategies — usually between 0.30% and 0.50% annually. It trades on an exchange with moderately tight spreads for a [fixed-income ETF](/fixed-income-etf/). Because fixed-income ETFs trade less frequently than [equity ETFs](/equity-etf/), spreads are slightly wider; still, a retail investor can execute a reasonable-sized trade without incurring excessive transaction costs.

## The pivot to sector selection

Corporate bonds are credit instruments — they promise to pay interest and return principal. Beyond that, they are also sensitive to sector-specific risks. A utility bond's price moves on different signals than an industrial bond's: utility spreads often tighten in [recessions](/recession/) (because they are stable, defensive assets), while industrial spreads tend to widen. A financial-sector bond behaves differently depending on whether [interest rates](/interest-rate/) are rising or falling; an energy bond is hostage to commodity prices.

By rotating between sectors based on their relative value, SSFI aims to own the sectors most likely to outperform in the next period without requiring an active manager's judgment. A mechanical rotation based on spreads and valuations, the theory goes, can capture some of the benefit of sector timing while keeping costs low and removing behavioural bias.

## For whom?

SSFI is appropriate for investors who want fixed-income exposure but believe a static allocation to all sectors equally is inefficient. It appeals to those comfortable with mechanical rule-based investing — accepting that the signals are sometimes right and sometimes wrong, but trusting that they add value over a full cycle.

It is less suitable for investors seeking simplicity or income maximisation (the strategy sometimes sacrifices yield to maintain balance), and it is not a high-yield or credit-strategy play — the investment-grade mandate keeps it conservative.

The fund often appears in balanced portfolios alongside equity holdings, or as a fixed-income core for investors sceptical of the old idea that bonds should simply be held and held.

## The rebalancing timeline

Rebalancing usually occurs quarterly, when the index is recalculated and the fund adjusts its sector weights to match the new signals. Trading is minimal compared to actively managed bond funds but more frequent than a static buy-and-hold [index fund](/index-fund/). This modest turnover keeps costs contained relative to active management but still allows the strategy to rotate as conditions change.

## How to evaluate it

Start with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet to understand the exact index methodology — which spreads are tracked, how valuations are calculated, and how frequently the rebalancing occurs. Compare SSFI's returns against a broader investment-grade bond index (the Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index, for instance) and against competing fixed-income sector-rotation strategies to see whether the tilt has added value net of fees.

Because [sector rotation](/sector-rotation/) works cyclically — sometimes the rotations enhance returns, sometimes they lag — a single year or even three years is a short window. A five-year or longer view shows more realistically whether the mechanical rules are adding value.

Check the current sector breakdown in the holdings detail to see what the model is positioning for right now. And review the [index provider's](/index-provider/) publications on the historical performance of each sector signal to understand which factors have driven returns in the past.

Fixed-income investors historically tolerate lower returns than equity investors because bonds provide stability and income. This fund aims to provide both while also capturing a bit of the benefit of tactical rotation — a middle ground between passive and active.
