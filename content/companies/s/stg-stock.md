Sunlands Technology Group operates an online education platform serving students across China. The company's core business is straightforward: it offers courses and educational content designed to help students prepare for standardised examinations and professional certifications, and to acquire skills for career advancement. Students pay tuition to enrol in courses delivered through Sunlands' online platform, which provides video lectures, practice problems, interactive learning tools, and instructor support. The company is fundamentally a digital delivery mechanism for education — something that has become possible and valuable only as high-speed internet penetration in China has reached the point where millions of people can reliably stream video and participate in online learning.

The educational space Sunlands addresses is large and competitive. In China, as in most countries, there is persistent demand for educational services that help people pass exams and improve their job prospects. Students compete for university entrance through the Gaokao (the national college entrance examination), professionals seek certifications to advance careers, and employers want workers with marketable skills. This creates a multi-billion-dollar market for test preparation, professional development, and skills training. Sunlands attempts to capture a portion of that market through an online model that scales more efficiently than traditional brick-and-mortar tutoring centres.

Sunlands' strategy rests on three interconnected advantages of the online delivery model. First, cost efficiency: a single course video can be viewed by thousands of students without additional instructor effort, which makes the unit economics of online education superior to in-person tutoring. Second, reach: students anywhere with internet access can enrol, which eliminates geographic constraints that limit traditional tutoring businesses to their local market. Third, data and measurement: an online platform naturally generates information about student progress, which learning methods work, and where students struggle, allowing continuous iteration and improvement of course content.

The company's course portfolio spans test preparation (for exams like the Gaokao and postgraduate entrance exams), professional certifications (accounting, law, engineering, teaching), and general skills training (business, technology, language learning). This [diversification](/diversification/) across multiple exam and professional categories reduces dependence on any single market segment and allows Sunlands to cross-sell to students pursuing multiple credentials. A student preparing for a professional accounting certification, for example, might later enrol in a business management course offered by the same platform.

Revenue comes almost entirely from course tuition. Students typically pay several hundred to a few thousand yuan per course, with the amount varying by course difficulty, duration, and instructor prestige. Most students view education spending as an investment in future earnings — if a certification helps someone get a better job, paying the course fees represents good value. This mindset supports relatively stable demand even during economic slowdowns, though discretionary educational spending does tend to decline in severe [recessions](/recession/).

The cost structure of an online education platform is characterised by high fixed costs and low variable costs. Sunlands must invest in platform development and maintenance, content creation (video production, course design), and instructor compensation. It must also spend substantially on customer [acquisition](/acquisition/) — marketing the platform to students who might otherwise use competitors. Once a student is acquired and enrolled, the marginal cost of serving that student is low (it's largely server costs and some customer support).

This cost structure creates powerful economics at scale. A company with one hundred thousand enrolled students can spread fixed platform costs across that base, making the cost per student low. A company with a million students spreads those same costs far more thinly. For online education companies, size matters enormously for profitability. Sunlands' challenge is to grow the enrolled student base and repeat enrolment rate while managing customer acquisition costs — the amount the company spends to acquire each new student.

The student acquisition channel is digital marketing, primarily through online advertising and social platforms popular in China such as WeChat, Douyin (Chinese TikTok), and Xiaohongshu. Sunlands also relies on organic word-of-mouth and repeat enrolment from existing students. The cost of acquiring a new student must be lower than the lifetime value of that student — the total profit the company earns from all the courses that student will take over time. If customer acquisition costs are too high relative to lifetime value, the unit economics don't work and growth becomes unprofitable.

Retention and repeat enrolment are critical metrics for Sunlands' profitability. If a student enrols in one course, completes it, and never returns, the company must continually acquire new students to sustain revenue growth. If students tend to enrol in multiple courses over time, either as they progress in their careers or as they prepare for successive exams, that dramatically improves the lifetime value calculation. Sunlands appears to focus on course completion quality and student outcomes, which would naturally improve repeat enrolment: students who succeed on their exam after taking Sunlands' course are more likely to recommend it or to use it again.

The competitive environment is intense. China's online education market includes hundreds of companies, from massive platforms like New Oriental and [TAL Education](/tal-stock/) (which dwarf Sunlands in scale and resources) to highly specialised niche providers. Some competitors are funded by venture capital and can sustain losses to build market share; others are state-owned or state-backed, which provides access to capital and distribution. Sunlands must compete on content quality, student outcomes, price, and user experience. The company also faces competition from free or low-cost alternatives, including YouTube tutorials and open educational resources.

Regulatory risk is meaningful for China-based edtech companies. The Chinese government has periodically introduced restrictions on for-profit education, consumer protections requiring escrow of student tuition, and limitations on tutoring for certain subjects. These regulations can be material to a company's operations and profitability. Sunlands must navigate this regulatory environment carefully and ensure compliance with any restrictions on how education can be delivered and funded.

The business also faces the structural challenge of the education market in China. As more of the population gains access to education and as the job market matures, growth in the core test-preparation market may moderate. Younger cohorts are smaller in China due to the former one-child policy, which means fewer students sitting the Gaokao each year. Sunlands' growth therefore depends increasingly on expand into professional certifications and skills training where the addressable market is larger and less affected by demographic trends.

To understand Sunlands as a business, review the company's annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001723935), which details revenue by course category, customer acquisition costs, and retention metrics. The quarterly earnings releases highlight enrolment numbers, average revenue per user, and profitability trends. Key metrics to monitor include course completion rates (which reflect content quality and student satisfaction), repeat enrolment rates (which drive lifetime value), and customer acquisition cost relative to lifetime value (which determines whether growth is sustainable and profitable). The company's regulatory compliance and any changes in Chinese education policy are also important to track, as policy shifts can dramatically affect the business.
