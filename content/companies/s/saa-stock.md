*The **ProShares Ultra SmallCap600**, ticker SAA, is a leveraged exchange-traded fund that bets on small-cap stocks with a 2-to-1 multiplier. It aims to track twice the daily return of the [Russell 2000 index](/russell-2000/), which represents roughly the smallest 2,000 publicly traded U.S. companies by market value. If the Russell 2000 goes up 1 percent in a day, SAA is designed to go up 2 percent. If it falls 1 percent, SAA falls 2 percent. This amplification comes with a price: the fund is volatile, expensive to hold for long periods, and can diverge sharply from twice the index's return over weeks or months. It is for active traders or short-term tactical positioning, not for buy-and-hold retirement portfolios.*

## How the leverage works

SAA uses derivatives — futures, swaps, and other financial instruments — to amplify its exposure to the Russell 2000 index. Instead of holding the 2,000 stocks themselves, the fund holds a mix of index futures and cash that replicates a 2-to-1 leveraged bet on daily index moves. Every day, the fund resets its positions to maintain that 2-to-1 ratio. If the index rallies, SAA's leverage increases in value; if the index falls, the leverage deepens losses.

This daily reset is critical to understand. SAA is designed to track twice the daily return, not twice the cumulative return over months. In a market with big swings in both directions, that difference becomes huge. Picture a small-cap index that goes up 10 percent one week, then falls 9 percent the next. Over two weeks, the index is up roughly 0.1 percent. But SAA, resetting daily, will be up roughly 0.2 percent. Now add a market with 5 percent daily swings in both directions, and the divergence grows. This is called volatility decay, and it is the hidden cost of leveraged funds.

## Why ProShares, why SAA

ProShares is one of the largest issuers of leveraged and [inverse ETFs](/inverse-etf/) in the United States. SAA is one of their straightforward 2x leveraged small-cap products. The Russell 2000 is the standard small-cap benchmark — a broader index than the S&P SmallCap 600. SAA gives traders liquid access to an amplified small-cap bet without requiring a margin account or the daily management of leverage themselves. The fund trades like any other ETF, with [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and volume that allow quick entry and exit during market hours.

## The expense ratio and hidden costs

SAA's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is higher than a simple [small-cap ETF](/small-cap-etf/), reflecting the cost of maintaining derivatives positions, frequent rebalancing, and daily reset mechanics. But that is just the visible fee. The real drag comes from holding leverage — the fund must constantly buy high and sell low (in tiny increments) as it resets each day. In a choppy market, that friction adds up.

Over a multi-year period, SAA will underperform twice the Russell 2000's return, sometimes by a lot. This is not because the fund is poorly managed; it is mathematical. Leverage and volatility do not mix well. The longer you hold SAA, the more likely this decay becomes significant.

## Who uses SAA and why

SAA is used by traders who believe the small-cap rally is steep and imminent, want amplified upside for days or weeks, and are comfortable exiting if the thesis breaks. It is also used tactically in a portfolio as an oversized small-cap bet during a bull phase, then sold when the trader wants to reduce exposure.

SAA is not designed for anyone holding it for a year or more, and it is especially unsuited to a retirement account where leverage drag compounds over decades. If you are in it, you should know why, watch it, and know when to get out.

## Risks and how they bite

The primary risk is volatility decay — the slow erosion of value relative to twice the index's cumulative return. In a sideways or choppy market, this happens automatically, even if the index ends flat. You lose money not because the index fell but because leverage and daily resets destroyed value.

A second risk is sudden market gaps. If the market opens with a 5 percent gap down after a shock, SAA opens with a 10 percent gap down, relative to the prior close. If you had planned to exit at a small loss, you might find yourself locked in a 10 percent loss before you can trade.

A third risk is structural leverage risk. SAA uses derivatives to maintain its 2x exposure. If the derivatives market freezes or the counterparty to those swaps has trouble, SAA can diverge from its target and lose value faster than expected.

## How to evaluate it

Before buying SAA, run a simple mental test: Do I believe the small-cap market will rally significantly in the next few weeks to months? Do I have a timeline for exiting? Am I comfortable with twice the daily volatility, knowing that some of that daily movement will decay over time? If the answer to any of these is no, do not buy SAA. Use a regular small-cap fund instead.

If you do trade it, watch the performance relative to twice the Russell 2000's return over your [holding period](/holding-period/). Expect underperformance, and exit if your thesis changes. SAA is a tool for a specific job, and that job is not long-term investing.
