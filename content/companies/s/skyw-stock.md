SkyWest Inc. operates regional aircraft on behalf of larger carriers, flying passengers from smaller US cities into major airline hubs where they connect onto long-haul flights. The company is not known to the general public the way major carriers like United or American are; most SkyWest passengers never know they are flying on a SkyWest plane because the aircraft carry the logo and branding of a larger partner airline. Yet for more than fifty years, SkyWest has quietly dominated one slice of the airline business — the operation of routes that are too short or too thin (carrying too few passengers) for the major carriers to fly profitably themselves.

## From startup to code-share specialist

SkyWest began in 1972 as a small commuter airline in Utah, operating a handful of aircraft on local routes. For most of the 1980s and early 1990s, the company remained regional and scrappy, competing in what was then a fragmented commuter-airline market where many small carriers ran point-to-point flights or operated as independents. The turning point came in the mid-1990s when the major carriers — faced with pressure to reduce costs and a need to feed traffic into their hubs — began shifting regional flying to specialized partners rather than operating those routes themselves. SkyWest saw the opportunity and began building capacity agreements with major carriers.

By the late 1990s, SkyWest had transformed itself from an independent regional airline into a contract carrier flying under capacity-purchase agreements, primarily with Delta and United. This shift was profound. Instead of owning the route, the revenue, or the pricing power, SkyWest agreed to fly specific routes for a fixed fee per flight hour or per passenger, with the major carrier retaining all the commercial relationship with the customer. In exchange, SkyWest gained reliability: predictable contracts worth billions of dollars over multi-year terms, with the major carriers bearing demand risk. The company expanded rapidly throughout the 2000s, and by the early 2010s had become one of the largest regional carriers in the United States.

The 2008 financial crisis and subsequent industry consolidation tested the model but ultimately reinforced it. When passenger demand collapsed, the major carriers (which had contracts with SkyWest) had pricing power and could weather the downturn; SkyWest, bound by fixed-fee contracts, faced margin pressure. But the carrier did not disappear—the majors needed SkyWest's capacity for their hub operations, and SkyWest had no economic incentive to abandon the market. The company took on debt, cut costs, and endured. Many of its smaller competitors did not survive.

## How the business actually works

SkyWest's revenue comes almost entirely from capacity agreements with major carriers—chiefly United, Delta, and American. Under a typical agreement, SkyWest commits to fly a specific number of daily flights on routes the major carrier designates. SkyWest owns or leases the aircraft and employs the crews. The major carrier sets the ticket price, determines the schedule, handles marketing, and bears the revenue risk. SkyWest receives a payment per flight hour, per available seat mile (ASM), or per passenger—in any case, a fixed or near-fixed fee that does not rise if the plane is full or drop if it is half-empty.

This structure makes SkyWest a service contractor rather than a traditional airline. The major carriers call the shots. If a route underperforms, the major carrier shrinks it or cuts it; SkyWest must redeploy the aircraft elsewhere under contract or reduce capacity. SkyWest cannot raise prices to compensate for higher fuel costs or labor expenses; instead, it absorbs margin pressure or renegotiates with its partners, who have little incentive to improve terms. In return for that subordination, SkyWest gets a predictable volume of flying, minimal revenue risk, and multi-year contracts that provide stability.

The costs SkyWest controls are labor, fuel, and maintenance. The company negotiates with pilot unions and flight attendants, manages fuel hedging, and maintains the regional jets and turboprops in its fleet. The aircraft are typically smaller, newer narrow-body jets (such as Bombardier CRJ900s and Airbus A220s) and turboprops designed for short routes. Fuel is a major expense. Labor—particularly crew wages—is tightly watched. As a regional operator, SkyWest pays less than major carriers but faces wage pressure as the regional-flying pool tightens and pilots use regional slots as stepping stones to higher-paid major-carrier positions.

## The moat—and its limits

SkyWest's competitive advantage is primarily operational: the company has built over fifty years of expertise in running regional flights efficiently, training crews for short-haul operations, and maintaining the hub-and-spoke system that the majors depend on. The major carriers trust SkyWest to show up, move passengers safely, and keep planes on schedule. That operational reliability and scale—SkyWest is among the largest regional carriers in North America—makes it valuable to the majors and a difficult partner to replace.

But this moat is fragile. SkyWest has no direct relationship with customers, no pricing power, and no control over the routes it flies. The major carriers could theoretically consolidate regional flying in-house or move capacity to competitors; they are constrained only by economics and the time required to ramp up alternative suppliers. SkyWest's revenues depend entirely on its contracts with United, Delta, and American, and a loss of material capacity from any major partner would be severe. Historically, major carriers have preferred to work with established regional partners rather than build regional operations themselves, because it preserves labor separation and allows them to shed capacity quickly in downturns. That dynamic favors SkyWest, but it is not irreversible.

## Where pressure comes from

SkyWest faces several headwinds. Labor is the most acute. Pilot shortages have driven up regional wages, and SkyWest must compete with other carriers for crew talent. The company has a high fixed-cost structure (crews, aircraft leases, maintenance) relative to the revenue it receives, leaving limited room for error on utilization or cost control. Fuel prices remain volatile and difficult to hedge over long contract periods. And the business sits at the mercy of economic cycles: when the major carriers retract, SkyWest contracts with them, with no insulation from demand shocks.

Aircraft orders and the composition of the fleet add another risk. SkyWest commits to orders for new aircraft years in advance, betting on demand. If a major partner suddenly reduces the number of regional flights it wants, SkyWest is locked into lease payments for aircraft it cannot easily sublease or redeploy. Conversely, if the regional-flying pool shrinks due to consolidation among the majors or a shift to larger aircraft on thin routes, SkyWest has no fallback—it does not own the customer, it does not control the route, and it cannot unilaterally change the business model.

## How to research SkyWest

SkyWest's annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0000793733) details the company's contract capacity, the percentage of revenue from each major carrier, aircraft orders and leases, labor agreements, and insurance exposures. The quarterly earnings calls are where management discusses capacity trends—whether the majors are adding or reducing regional flying, aircraft deliveries, utilization rates, and negotiation updates with partners. Watch particularly for any announcement of contract renegotiations, reductions in allocated capacity, or changes in the fleet plan. These are the levers that drive SkyWest's fortunes.

Key metrics to follow: the number of departures per day (a proxy for activity), revenue per available seat mile (a measure of pricing and utilization), [operating margin](/operating-margin/) (thin relative to major carriers), and leverage (SkyWest carries debt to finance aircraft). Fuel prices matter more here than for passengers using major carriers, because SkyWest's revenue does not adjust when fuel spikes. And the health of the major carriers matters directly—if United or Delta is cutting regional capacity or facing a [recession](/recession/), SkyWest will feel it acutely and quickly.
