> "Most people walk past Simpson every day and never know it."

Simpson Manufacturing makes the metal hardware that actually holds buildings together—the connectors that join beams, the nails that fasten joists, the brackets that stop a roof from sliding off. These products are engineered for specific loads and failure modes: a roof connector must resist the lateral force of a hurricane or the vertical weight of snow; a joist hanger must support the weight of an entire floor without failing under vibration. A house or commercial building might contain hundreds of Simpson fasteners and connectors, each one rated and tested to code. Yet Simpson's name appears nowhere on the final structure; the connectors are buried inside walls, bolted beneath floors, or hidden in truss systems. This invisibility is actually a profound advantage, because Simpson competes on engineering, reliability, and code acceptance rather than brand recognition. A builder chooses Simpson because the local building inspector knows the part works, because the engineering is proven, and because if it fails, the builder faces liability.

The company traces its roots back to 1956, when founder Del Simpson began manufacturing nails in a garage in Richmond, California. The business expanded into specialized metal connectors and fasteners for construction as post-war residential building boomed and the industry standardized on engineered joint systems rather than the old craft of cutting and mortising wood by hand. By the 1980s and 1990s, Simpson had become a recognized name among builders and engineers for innovative fasteners and connectors; it pursued a strategy of vertical integration (making the fasteners in-house rather than outsourcing) and expansion into adjacent products (cold-formed steel components, anchor systems, fire-safety products).

The business model is refreshingly straightforward. Simpson manufactures fasteners and connectors in factories (primarily in North America, with some offshore production), sells them through distribution channels (lumber suppliers, home centers like [Home Depot](/hd-stock/), specialty suppliers) and directly to larger contractors and builders, and collects payment. Gross margins are respectable but not lavish because fasteners are ultimately commodities competing on performance and price; the company's profit comes from volume, operational efficiency, and the premium pricing it can command for engineered products with superior reliability. For decades the business was steady: construction cycles up, Simpson sales up; construction cycles down, sales down. The company weathered the 2008 financial crisis, survived the years of depressed residential building in the 2010s, and emerged as an even stronger player after housing recovered.

What distinguishes Simpson from a commodity nail factory is the engineering and code acceptance moat. Building codes—the rules that govern what materials are acceptable in construction—are updated every three years in the US. Simpson has invested heavily in testing, engineering, and relationships with code bodies to ensure its products are listed and approved. When a builder or engineer specifies a Simpson connector, they are not just saying "a connector"; they are saying "the connector that has been tested to resist X pounds of lateral force and is listed in the International Building Code and accepted by the inspector in my jurisdiction." A competitor cannot simply undercut Simpson on price because the builder would then need new engineering drawings, new code approvals, new inspector acceptance—a bureaucratic and liability nightmare. This makes Simpson's customer relationships durable and its pricing power real.

The company's [primary markets](/primary-market/) are residential construction (single-family homes, multifamily apartments) and commercial construction (office buildings, industrial structures, infrastructure). Its geographic footprint is primarily North America, though it has expanded internationally through [acquisitions](/acquisition/) and manufacturing partnerships. Within residential construction, Simpson has exposure across the housing cycle: new construction (the builder buys fasteners upfront), renovation and remodeling (homeowners hire contractors who buy Simpson connectors), and manufactured housing (mobile home and prefabrication factories buy in volume).

Simpson's primary competitive pressure comes from international manufacturers (primarily Chinese and Indian fastener makers) that can undercut on price, especially for commodity fasteners. For engineered and code-listed products, Simpson remains defensible; Chinese nail makers cannot easily enter because they would need to navigate code approvals and build relationships with inspectors and engineers. A secondary pressure is substitution: as building techniques evolve, some traditional connectors are displaced by new methods (like spray-foam sealants replacing mechanical fasteners, or changes in framing technique). The company must innovate continuously to stay relevant. Labor costs in North American manufacturing are higher than offshore, which creates chronic pressure to automate or offshore production, yet Simpson has maintained significant US manufacturing, partly because lead times matter to construction—a builder needs fasteners fast, not weeks from China.

Working capital can be lumpy. Simpson carries inventory of thousands of fastener SKUs (stock-keeping units) to meet builder demand, and payment terms from large customers (Home Depot, for instance) can be 60 or 90 days, meaning the company funds a large inventory base for extended periods. In booming construction cycles, this inventory can balloon; in downturns, it can become a liability. The company's capital expenditure is modest but steady—manufacturing equipment upgrades, facility improvements—and it has a history of returning cash to shareholders through [dividends](/dividend/) and buybacks when leverage is comfortable.

The quarterly earnings call typically includes management commentary on residential and commercial building trends, customer inventory levels, pricing actions, and operational efficiency. Key metrics to track are organic revenue growth (excluding acquisitions), gross margins (which reflect pricing power and manufacturing efficiency), SG&A [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) (operating leverage), and operating cash flow and working capital (construction businesses can be very profitable on paper but generate little actual cash if inventory and receivables spiral out of control). Watch the company's capital-return strategy: does management invest in R&D and innovation, or does it harvest cash and slide into commodity status? Finally, monitor housing starts and building permits as leading indicators; a sharp drop in housing starts foreshadows a revenue decline for Simpson within quarters.
