*The **VanEck Morningstar SMID Moat ETF** (SMOT) owns small and mid-sized US companies that Morningstar's research team has identified as having **wide economic moats**—real, durable competitive advantages that let them earn excess returns for years or decades. A moat might be a brand that customers trust, a proprietary technology rivals cannot copy, network effects that get stronger as the user base grows, or simple dominance in a local market. SMOT is built on the conviction that a company's ability to defend its profitability is as important as its current size or growth rate.*

Small and mid-cap stocks (SMID) are often bought for growth—the idea that smaller companies have more room to expand. SMOT inverts that emphasis. It does not chase growth; it hunts for durability. A small regional bank with unshakeable local deposits has a wider moat than a high-flying app-development firm with thin margins and ruthless competition. A niche manufacturer that dominates its specialized market with high switching costs has a wider moat than a generic producer in a commoditized industry. SMOT's screen is designed to find the former type of company and skip the latter, regardless of recent growth rates or momentum.

## Morningstar's moat methodology

Morningstar is a research firm that has spent decades analyzing individual companies. Over time, the firm developed a systematic framework for assessing competitive moats: asking, for each company, *why can this firm sustain higher returns than rivals, and for how long?* The framework looks for specific sources of advantage: switching costs (it is painful for customers to leave), network effects (the product gets better as more people use it), cost advantages (the firm can undercut rivals and still profit), brand trust, pricing power, [intangible assets](/intangible-assets/) like patents or licenses. Morningstar rates each company's moat as wide, narrow, or nonexistent.

For SMOT, VanEck partnered with Morningstar to use that research. VanEck selects companies from the US small and mid-cap universe that Morningstar rates as having a **wide moat**. The selection is not passive—it requires Morningstar to actively assess each company—but the holdings are then held as an index that rebalances periodically. This is active analysis married to passive holding, a middle ground between fully active stock-picking and pure index management.

## The SMID universe and what makes a durable small company

Small and mid-cap stocks are often small for reasons: they operate in local or specialized markets, they lack the scale economies of large rivals, they are young and unproven. But some small companies have found defensible positions. A dental-practice-management software maker might have zero growth but very high switching costs—a practice cannot easily abandon years of patient records and billing history, so it sticks with the incumbent. A regional real-estate company might have deep relationships with local developers and lenders, making it hard for a national rival to displace it. A specialty-chemicals producer might own a patent or proprietary process that gives it a cost advantage or unique capability.

These companies are often unglamorous. They are not venture-backed, not chasing viral growth, not shooting for a ten-bagger in three years. But they can compound steadily for decades if their moat protects them from commoditization. SMOT is built on the bet that these durable, defensible, boring small companies will deliver better long-term returns than small-cap glamour stocks that lack defensibility and face constant competitive pressure.

## Size and liquidity within the SMID category

Small-cap stocks ([market cap](/market-capitalization/) from roughly two billion to eight billion dollars) are less liquid than large-cap stocks. Individual securities can have wide [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and choppy trading, especially in down markets. Mid-cap stocks are bigger and easier to trade. SMOT spans both, so the liquidity of individual holdings varies. As an ETF, SMOT itself likely trades at reasonable spreads because it holds dozens or hundreds of stocks, so the portfolio as a whole is more liquid than any single holding might be. But buying or selling a large block of SMOT could encounter some friction relative to a broad-market large-cap ETF.

The fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) reflects the work Morningstar does to maintain the moat ratings. It is higher than a purely passive small-cap [index fund](/index-fund/) but reasonable relative to actively managed alternatives that employ similar fundamental research.

## The risks and the limits of moats

The clearest risk is time. A moat that feels wide today can erode. Technology disrupts competitive advantages faster than it did a generation ago. A brand that was unbeatable gets toppled by a cheaper competitor or an upstart with a viral product. Network effects can reverse if users flee to a better-connected platform. Patents expire. Morningstar's assessments are current, not prophetic, so a company rated as having a wide moat can lose it faster than the researchers expect.

A second risk is that the moat does not guarantee growth. A utility with a deep moat in its market—stable, predictable earnings, strong switching costs—might be a perfectly safe holding but will not outpace a high-growth rival that lacks a moat at all. SMOT is tilted toward quality and durability, not toward speed. In environments where growth wildly outpaces value, the fund will lag.

The third risk is the Morningstar analyst team itself. The moat ratings are judgments made by human researchers, not objective formulas. Reasonable people can disagree about whether a company's advantage is wide or narrow. Over time, analytical errors will accumulate: a moat rated wide that turns out to be narrow, a company promoted into the fund whose advantage crumbles, an industry trend that Morningstar did not fully foresee. The process is thoughtful but not infallible.

Finally, there is [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/). The SMID universe is smaller than the large-cap universe, so dominance in a small market can translate into a larger fund position. If a handful of moat-rated companies outperform, the fund can become lumpy. [Diversification](/diversification/) within SMOT depends on how many stocks Morningstar's universe contains and how carefully the index construction enforces balance.

## Evaluating SMOT as an investment

Start with Morningstar's moat definitions, available on their website. Understand what wide, narrow, and no-moat actually mean in practice, with examples. Then pull SMOT's latest holdings list and read Morningstar's moat rating and bull case for a handful of them—ten percent of the portfolio, say. Ask yourself whether you agree with the moat assessment. Do you genuinely believe this regional bank or specialty manufacturer will resist commoditization for the next decade?

Compare SMOT's rolling returns against a plain SMID index fund (like IJH or VBR) and against a large-cap moat-tilted fund if one exists (some funds now exist that screen for moats across all sizes). That comparison shows whether Morningstar's specific research adds value net of fees, and whether the small-cap tilt is a help or a drag relative to a mid-cap or large-cap moat strategy.

Check the fund's prospectus for the rebalance schedule. If Morningstar's moat ratings change frequently, the fund rebalances often, incurring tax and trading costs. If the ratings are sticky, the fund is more stable. Either approach has merits; the key is understanding the pace of change.

SMOT is best for a long-term investor who believes in competitive advantages, who is comfortable holding small and mid-cap stocks through drawdowns, and who trusts Morningstar's research team. It is not suitable for someone seeking broad market exposure or for investors uncomfortable with lower liquidity in individual holdings. It is a conviction play: you are betting that the companies Morningstar says have moats actually do, and that those moats will compound into outperformance over years.
