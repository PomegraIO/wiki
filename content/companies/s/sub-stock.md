*A **[municipal bond](/municipal-bond/)** (or muni) is a [bond](/bond/) issued by a U.S. state, city, county, or other local government authority to raise money for infrastructure, schools, or other public projects. The **iShares Short-Term National Muni [Bond ETF](/bond-etf/)** is a fund that owns hundreds of these bonds with the shortest maturities — bonds that will be repaid in one to five years. Interest paid on municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax, which is why they appeal to high-income earners. SUB is a simple, low-cost way to buy that tax-free income across a diversified portfolio of local governments and agencies.*

## What municipal bonds are and why they exist

States and cities need to borrow money to pay for highways, bridges, water systems, school buildings, and hospitals. Rather than raise funds from the federal government, they issue bonds — they borrow directly from the public and promise to repay with interest. Those bonds are then traded in the bond market like any other security.

The catch is that Congress has declared the interest on municipal bonds exempt from federal income tax. This tax break makes munis attractive to wealthy individuals and institutions that would otherwise pay high federal rates. As a result, municipalities can borrow at lower [interest rates](/interest-rate/) than they would otherwise, which makes it cheaper for them to build infrastructure.

## How SUB works

SUB holds over 500 municipal bonds issued by different issuers — states, counties, cities, school districts, public utilities, and other authorities across the country. Each bond is a short-term obligation, maturing between one and five years out. Because the maturities are short, the fund's portfolio does not swing wildly in value when interest rates change, unlike longer-term bonds, which can fall sharply if rates rise.

The fund is passively managed: it tracks an index of short-term municipal bonds, maintaining roughly the same holdings and weightings as that index. [BlackRock](/blk-stock/) rebalances the fund periodically to keep it aligned with its benchmark. This passive approach keeps costs minuscule — the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is among the lowest for any fixed-income fund.

## The tax advantage and who benefits

The interest SUB receives from its municipal bonds is federal tax-exempt. On a monthly basis, the fund distributes this interest as a [dividend](/dividend/) to shareholders. For a shareholder in a high federal tax bracket, that tax-free income is worth materially more than taxable interest would be, because the shareholder avoids paying 24, 32, or 37 percent federal tax on the income.

However, the tax advantage only matters if you are in a high enough tax bracket. For someone in the 12 or 22 percent bracket, or who has little federal tax liability, a taxable bond fund might offer a higher after-tax yield. Additionally, some states exempt munis issued within that state from state and local income taxes as well, which creates an even larger advantage for residents. SUB holds bonds from across the country, so most shareholders will not receive state-tax exemption on all of the fund's holdings — only on munis issued in their home state, if they live in a state with income tax.

## Interest rate risk and the virtue of short maturities

All bonds are sensitive to interest rates. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises rates, existing bonds fall in value, because the interest they pay is no longer competitive with new bonds offering higher rates. When rates fall, existing bonds rise in value.

SUB's short [maturity structure](/maturity-structure/) insulates the fund from extreme interest-rate swings. A bond maturing in two years will have lost far less value than a bond maturing in 20 years if rates rise by the same amount. The trade-off is that SUB also benefits less from falling rates than a longer-term bond fund would. In practical terms, SUB is a low-volatility way to own municipal bonds, suitable for investors who want to avoid large price swings and simply collect monthly tax-free income.

## Credit risk — the health of the issuers

SUB owns bonds issued by hundreds of different governments and agencies. Some are wealthy cities with strong budgets; others are smaller municipalities dealing with [pension obligations](/pension-obligation/), aging infrastructure, and slower population growth. The bonds in SUB's portfolio all meet an investment-grade standard, meaning they carry a relatively low risk of default, but they are not risk-free.

If a municipality faces severe fiscal stress — declining revenues, missed payments, or actual default — its bonds can lose substantial value, particularly if other investors try to sell at the same time. The 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic both raised awareness of municipal [credit risk](/credit-risk/). SUB's [diversification](/diversification/) across hundreds of issuers reduces the impact of any single default, but a broad [credit event](/credit-event-sovereign/) affecting many smaller municipalities could materially harm the fund.

## Liquidity and transparency

Municipal bonds are less liquid than [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) or [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/). When you own an individual muni bond directly, selling it before maturity may require accepting a wider [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/). SUB, by contrast, is an exchange-traded fund: it trades on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) at prices set by supply and demand, and the bid-ask spread is typically tight because the fund is large and heavily traded.

The fund's holdings are transparent — you can see the specific bonds it owns and their characteristics. BlackRock publishes detailed fact sheets and prospectuses explaining the fund's construction and risk factors.

## The investor it is built for

SUB is designed for investors in high federal tax brackets who want a low-volatility, diversified source of tax-free income. It appeals to retirees with substantial income, high-earning professionals, and households in wealthy areas with high state and local tax rates. For such investors, the monthly distributions from SUB provide a materially higher after-tax yield than comparable taxable bond funds.

For lower-income taxpayers or those with little federal tax liability, SUB offers no special advantage and may carry worse [real yields](/real-yield/) than taxable alternatives. Additionally, SUB is not appropriate for someone needing to preserve capital in the short term for a specific goal, because interest-rate moves can still cause meaningful price swings over a few months, even with a short-maturity portfolio.

## How to research SUB

Investors should start with iShares' fund fact sheet, which details the current holdings, credit quality breakdown, maturity distribution, and expense ratio. The prospectus explains the specific municipal bond index being tracked and the fund's management approach. It is worth comparing SUB's yield against taxable short-term bond funds and other muni funds to understand the after-tax advantage. Finally, review the credit quality of the bonds — the percentage in AAA, AA, A, and BBB ratings — to gauge the risk profile relative to your comfort level.
