SLR Investment Corp. is a specialty finance [business development company](/business-development-company/) (BDC) that invests in secured loans, structured credit instruments, and other middle-market lending opportunities. As a regulated investment company, it generates returns for shareholders primarily through interest income on its loan portfolio and occasional capital gains, operating in the gap between traditional bank lending and private [equity financing](/equity-financing/) where borrowers cannot easily access conventional credit lines.

## What exactly is SLR, and why does it exist?

SLR Investment Corp. occupies a specific niche: it lends to companies that fall between the upper limit of community-bank lending and the lower threshold of private-equity interest. These are typically small-to-mid-sized businesses in the United States that need capital but find traditional bank financing either unavailable or mismatched to their risk profile. By law, as a Business Development Company, SLR must invest at least 70 percent of its assets in eligible portfolio companies and other qualifying investments. This mandate forces disciplined focus: the firm cannot become a broad asset manager; it must remain committed to direct lending and related credit strategies.

## How does SLR make money?

The income streams are straightforward. SLR earns interest on its portfolio of loans—typically floating-rate instruments tied to base rates such as [SOFR](/sofr/) or Prime, plus a spread. Because these are secured loans (backed by collateral such as equipment, real estate, or business cash flows), default risk is lower than unsecured lending, but recovery is never guaranteed. The company also earns fees from loan originations and management, and occasionally realises gains when loans are repaid ahead of schedule or when portfolio companies exit successfully. A portion of earnings is distributed to shareholders as [dividends](/dividend/), while [retained earnings](/retained-earnings/) are reinvested to grow the loan book. The dividend is a core draw for investors; as a regulated investment company, SLR is required to distribute at least 90 percent of net investment income to shareholders annually.

## What makes SLR different from a bank or a private-equity firm?

Banks are heavily regulated deposit-takers; they hold capital against expected losses and must meet strict [leverage ratios](/leverage-ratio-forex/). SLR, by contrast, is funded largely through debt raised in capital markets (unsecured notes and [preferred stock](/preferred-stock/)) and equity, but it is not a bank—it does not take deposits and faces different regulatory constraints. Its leverage is typically higher than a bank's, and its willingness to lend to less creditworthy borrowers (or borrowers in transition) reflects that higher-risk appetite.

From private equity, SLR differs in that it does not typically take equity stakes or control positions. It lends secured money and expects repayment; it is not in the business of owning companies, restructuring them, and selling them for gain. That distinction matters because it means SLR's returns are capped by the coupon on its loans, whereas private equity can capture equity upside if the underlying business thrives. It also means SLR's approach is less adversarial: lending does not require operational control.

## What is the investment case and the risks?

SLR's appeal lies in yield. In a low-rate environment, traditional [bonds](/bond/) offer little; SLR's loans offer mid-single-digit to low-double-digit returns, attractive to income investors. The secured nature of the loans and the [diversification](/diversification/) across many borrowers offer some insulation against individual defaults. However, the risks are material. Economic downturns hit the mid-market hard; a [recession](/recession/) can cause a wave of borrower distress, forced asset sales, and losses that erode shareholder capital. SLR's leverage amplifies both gains and losses. Interest-rate movements also matter: if rates fall sharply, the value of floating-rate loans can reset lower, and the [refinancing risk](/refinancing-risk/) for borrowers increases. Additionally, in a credit crisis, the [secondary market](/secondary-market/) for distressed loans can dry up, leaving SLR with illiquid positions and no easy exit.

## How would a researcher approach SLR as an investment?

Start with SLR's annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001418076), which breaks down the composition of the loan portfolio by industry and credit quality. Pay close attention to the concentration—what percentage of assets are backed by a single borrower or sector? Watch the yields on new loans being added to the portfolio; falling yields can signal that credit has loosened and future returns are at risk. The quarterly earnings releases disclose the portfolio's unrealised gains and losses, a leading indicator of stress. Monitor the ratio of non-accrual loans (those on which borrowers have stopped paying) to total loans; rising non-accrual can warn of coming write-downs. Finally, track the dividend: as a BDC, SLR's ability to sustain its payout depends on a steady stream of loan interest; a cut or suspension signals trouble.
