*The **SPDR Portfolio Ultra Short T-Bill ETF** (SPTU) holds a rolling basket of U.S. [Treasury bills](/treasury-bill/) — debt instruments with maturities between one and three months — and trades on an exchange like a stock. It is meant to act as a liquid, transparent alternative to money-market funds or ultra-short [bond](/bond/) funds, capturing the yields of short-dated government debt without the opacity of traditional cash management products.*

## What SPTU holds and why

The fund contains a ladder of Treasury bills across the one-to-three-month maturity band. At any given time, the holdings sit very close to that specified range, rolling forward as bills mature. The actual composition shifts constantly — bills tick down to expiry and get replaced with fresh issuances further out the curve — but the fund is rebalanced to keep the maturity profile tight and predictable.

Treasury bills are the shortest-dated government debt the U.S. Treasury issues. They carry no coupon; you buy them at a discount and receive full face value at maturity, the difference being your return. This creates a clean, mechanical risk profile: a T-bill paying off in 90 days will deliver almost exactly that face value no matter what happens to [interest rates](/interest-rate/) in the interim. The price sensitivity is minimal.

SPTU's holdings are backed by the full faith and credit of the United States government, which makes them among the lowest-credit-risk securities on Earth. That assurance comes at a cost — T-bills offer lower yields than [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), municipals, or longer-dated Treasuries — but the trade-off is simplicity and certainty.

## How it differs from money-market funds

A traditional money-market fund is a [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/) that invests in very short-term debt and money-market instruments. It offers convenience and a familiar interface, but it is an opaque wrapper: the fund company manages the portfolio, publishes a daily [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), and moves money in and out at fund prices set once a day after market close. You do not own the underlying bills directly.

SPTU is an exchange-traded fund, which means you own a proportional slice of the actual Treasury bills in the portfolio, and the fund trades continuously on the open market throughout the day. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically low because the fund structure is simple — just a pass-through to the holdings — and there is no active manager choosing which bills to buy. The fund is instead rules-based, holding whatever falls into the designated maturity band.

This structure creates real differences in practice. Because SPTU trades intraday, you can buy or sell it whenever the [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) is open, at a price set by supply and demand in the moment, not a single daily price set after market close. For large investors or those who need to move in and out on specific terms, that liquidity and transparency can matter. For a retail saver putting money away for three months, the advantage over a money-market fund is less clear.

## Yield, costs, and the tax story

SPTU's yield mirrors whatever short-term T-bill rates happen to be at any moment. As the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) has raised rates from near-zero in recent years, the fund's yield has risen correspondingly, making it competitive with and often better than the interest rates offered on traditional savings accounts or money-market deposit accounts at banks. When rates fall, so does the fund's yield — that is the trade-off of simplicity.

The expense ratio is straightforward and very low, typically well under 5 basis points annually, because there is minimal overhead. The fund does not employ a stock-picker or a portfolio manager; it is a passive trawl of bills in its target maturity range.

From a tax perspective, Treasury interest is exempt from state and local taxes — a meaningful advantage for residents of high-tax states — but it remains subject to federal tax. For a fund held in a taxable account, that federal tax liability arrives annually, even though the holdings are rolled constantly and you may never actually receive a bill that matures in your hands.

## Volatility, duration, and what moves the price

Because Treasury bills are so short-dated, their prices are almost unmoved by changes in interest rates. A one-month bill that moves one percentage point in rate will fall perhaps 0.08 per cent in price — barely noticeable. This is the whole reason the fund exists: it captures yield with vanishingly small duration risk.

However, that does not mean the fund price is literally fixed. SPTU can trade at a small premium or discount to its net asset value, depending on intraday supply and demand. If everyone suddenly wants to buy Treasury bills on a given morning, the fund's share price might pop above the underlying value; if everyone is selling, it might dip below. These discounts and premiums are typically tiny, but they can matter if you are trading frequently or in large size.

The real risk in ultra-short Treasury funds is not volatility but opportunity cost. When short rates are very low, the fund yields little. You are sacrificing potential return for certainty and liquidity — a sensible trade for money you will need within a few months, less so for a five-year time horizon.

## How investors use SPTU

The fund works well as a parking place for cash in a brokerage account, especially when Treasury-bill yields are attractive relative to money-market funds or bank savings accounts. It is transparent, liquid, and tax-efficient relative to alternatives. An investor building a laddered Treasury portfolio can use SPTU for the shortest rung. A pension fund or foundation can hold it as the stable, safe bucket of a larger allocation.

For a long-term investor, SPTU is not a destination — the yields are too modest to serve as a growth engine — but it is a sensible way to hold idle cash without simply leaving money in a checking account.

## Researching SPTU

The fund's prospectus and fact sheet are the starting point: read the stated objective, the maturity policy, and the expense ratio. SPTU's price and yield are published daily; watch the spread between the bid and ask prices on the exchange to understand the trading cost if you are moving in or out in size. Compare the fund's [current yield](/current-yield/) to whatever you can earn on a Treasury-bill ladder (the yield should be nearly identical if the fund is doing its job). For context on how short-term rates are trending, track the 3-month T-bill yield from the Federal Reserve's data portal. If you are evaluating it as a cash alternative to a money-market fund, subtract State Street's expense ratio from what the money-market fund advertises, and compare the nets.
