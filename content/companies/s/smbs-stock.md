## The fund and what it holds

The Schwab Mortgage-Backed Securities ETF invests in mortgage-backed securities—[bonds](/bond/) secured by pools of residential home loans. Most of the fund's holdings are agency MBS, meaning they carry explicit or implicit government backing through [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/), [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/), or Ginnie Mae. This reduces [credit risk](/credit-risk/) sharply compared to non-agency mortgages; the risk is not that homeowners will default, but that the market value of the bonds will fluctuate with [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and that mortgages will be paid off faster or slower than expected.

SMBS is designed for investors who want exposure to the mortgage market as a fixed-income holding. It sits between a [Treasury bond](/treasury-bond/) fund—which carries no default risk but lower yields—and a corporate-bond fund, which carries more default risk but often more yield. Mortgage-backed securities occupy the middle ground: government-backed safety, reasonable income, and a distinct interest-rate and prepayment profile.

## Income and yield mechanics

The fund distributes cash regularly to shareholders, a mix of principal repayment and interest collected from the underlying mortgages. The yield is higher than comparable Treasury bonds because of the additional risk from prepayment uncertainty, but lower than [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/). The actual monthly distribution amount varies because mortgage prepayments are lumpy—when interest rates fall, homeowners refinance their loans, returning principal to bondholders sooner than scheduled. When rates rise, prepayments slow, stretching the bond's life longer.

Investors in SMBS receive this income as [dividends](/dividend/) paid to the fund, which distributes to shareholders. The distributions are taxed as ordinary income in a taxable account, which is a disadvantage compared to holding Treasuries in a low-tax-bracket situation. In a tax-advantaged retirement account, this matters much less.

## Interest-rate and prepayment risk

The fund's value moves inversely to interest rates. When rates rise, existing mortgage-backed bonds become less attractive, and their market price falls. When rates fall, bonds become more valuable, and the price rises—but homeowners simultaneously refinance, shortening the fund's [effective duration](/effective-duration/) and capping gains.

This "[negative convexity](/negative-convexity/)" is the defining challenge of mortgage-backed securities. Imagine you buy a bond priced to yield 4% over ten years. If rates fall to 3%, two things happen: the bond's price rises (good), but the mortgages underlying it are refinanced (bad), cutting short your 4% yield stream. If rates rise to 5%, the price falls (bad) but you are locked into a 4% yield for longer (somewhat mitigating). The asymmetry—you get capped upside in a falling-rate environment but full downside in a rising-rate environment—is the cost of the higher yield.

## Costs and trading

SMBS carries a competitive [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) reflecting Schwab's efficient fund management. The fund trades on an exchange like a stock, so you can buy and sell shares at market prices throughout the day. Liquidity is good; as a large-capitalization [fixed-income ETF](/fixed-income-etf/), it has tight [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) and is easy to enter and exit.

The fund is appropriate for long-term, buy-and-hold income investors, as well as for traders who want to express a tactical view on the mortgage-bond market or adjust fixed-income allocation. For buy-and-hold investors in taxable accounts, its tax efficiency is moderate—distributions are ordinary income—but for tax-deferred accounts, it is a straightforward way to add mortgage exposure without owning individual bonds.

## Who it is for and how to research it

SMBS suits conservative investors seeking fixed income beyond Treasuries, portfolio managers balancing risk and yield, and individuals assembling a [bond ladder](/bond-ladder/) or fixed-income portfolio. It is not for investors seeking growth or high yield; it is for those who want steady, predictable distributions backed by a stable mortgage pool.

To research SMBS, start with Schwab's fund fact sheet, which lists the portfolio composition, duration, yield, and expense ratio. Check the fund's performance against the Bloomberg Mortgage-Backed Securities Index or a comparable benchmark. Monitor the mortgage market backdrop: as interest rates and housing conditions shift, the appeal and risk of mortgage-backed securities shift with them. If you hold SMBS in a taxable account, be aware that the distributions are taxed as ordinary income, not capital gains.
