*The **Themes US Small Cap Cash Flow Champions ETF** — ticker SMCF — is a thematic fund that hunts for small-cap companies (those with market capitalizations typically under USD 5 billion) that excel at one specific skill: turning revenue into actual cash they can distribute or reinvest, without relying on borrowed money.*

The premise is elegant: profitable is not enough. A company can report strong earnings on paper but burn cash on working capital, inventory, or capital expenditure. A company that generates cash — the kind it can actually use to pay dividends, buy back shares, or reduce debt — is fundamentally different. SMCF screens the small-cap universe for companies that meet that test, then weights them by cash flow metrics.

## What SMCF is hunting for

SMCF looks for companies that pass a set of filters. First, they must be generating positive free cash flow — the cash left over after paying bills and maintaining the business. Second, they must have a reasonable balance sheet (not drowning in debt). Third, they must be returning cash to shareholders through buybacks, dividends, or debt paydown. These are intentional policy choices, not accidents.

The appeal of this screen is that it favors disciplined, mature small-cap operators over speculative growth stories. A high-growth startup burning cash might have a lower margin of safety. A small company with a long history of generating cash and returning it to shareholders is demonstrating a sustainable business model.

Small-cap stocks are inherently more volatile than large-cap stocks. They have less analyst coverage, smaller trading volumes, and greater exposure to operational shocks. But they also offer higher growth potential and are often overlooked by institutional investors. A small-cap that has mastered cash generation and shareholder returns can be particularly attractive because it combines the upside of a smaller company with the discipline of a mature operator.

## How the fund works

SMCF is an actively managed ETF, not a passive index tracker. Themes' investment team screens the thousands of publicly traded US small-cap stocks, identifies those that meet the cash flow and capital allocation criteria, and constructs a portfolio. The screening process is systematic: it can be back-tested, monitored, and refined over time.

The fund holds roughly 100 to 150 stocks, which provides diversification without becoming so broad that it just mirrors the small-cap market. Holdings are weighted based on various metrics — free cash flow yield, balance sheet strength, or buyback intensity — rather than simply by market cap.

Because SMCF is actively managed, it can rotate its holdings as circumstances change. If a company's cash generation falters, or if management stops returning cash to shareholders, the fund can replace it. This is different from a passive small-cap index, which would hold everything regardless.

## Costs and the active management question

SMCF's expense ratio runs roughly 0.50 to 0.65% per year. That is higher than a passive small-cap index ETF (which might cost 0.05%), but it is competitive with other actively managed small-cap funds. The question is whether the active screening adds enough value to justify the extra cost. In periods when small-cap value stocks (which correlate with cash generation) outperform, SMCF may deliver excess returns. In other periods, it may underperform.

The fund is relatively liquid. SMCF trades daily on NASDAQ, though with lower volume than a large-cap or broad index fund. An individual investor can buy or sell without trouble, though an institution buying a very large block might move the market slightly.

## Why small-cap cash generators appeal

Small-cap stocks as a group have historically delivered higher long-term returns than large-cap stocks, though with more volatility. The theory is that smaller companies have more room to grow, face less competition from consolidation and regulation, and can execute strategy more flexibly. A small-cap that also generates cash and returns it to shareholders combines that upside potential with capital discipline.

For investors tired of "growth at any cost" narratives, SMCF offers a different lens: companies with real business momentum, real profitability, and real management discipline. They may not be household names, but they are businesses that work.

## Risks and limitations

SMCF is still a small-cap fund. It will be more volatile than the broad market in downturns. If investors flee small stocks in a risk-off environment, SMCF will fall hard. A recession can quickly undermine cash generation, as revenue contracts and companies cut capital spending. The disciplined payers of dividends can slash dividends when times get tough. Buyback programs can be suspended.

The cash flow metrics can be manipulated or distorted. A company that reduces capital expenditure can temporarily boost free cash flow at the expense of future competitiveness. Working capital changes can create lumpy cash flows that may not persist. The fund's screening process is systematic, but no screen is perfect.

There is also the question of whether cash generation is a sufficient criterion for future outperformance. A small company generating cash today might face disruption from a newer, faster-growing competitor. The fund's backward-looking screening might not predict which companies will still be generating cash five years from now.

## How to research SMCF

Review Themes' prospectus and holdings list. Understand what specific metrics the fund uses to screen for cash generation and balance sheet strength. Look at the largest holdings and understand what each company does — the fund should disclose its top 10 or top 20 holdings. Compare SMCF's performance to relevant benchmarks: a broad small-cap index (like the Russell 2000) and other small-cap value or quality funds. Over 3-, 5-, and 10-year periods, does SMCF's active selection have an edge, or does it merely track its benchmark after fees?

Examine the fund's cash flow profile and dividend/buyback history. If the fund is successfully capturing small-cap companies generating cash and returning it, that should show up in the fund's own dividend payments. Monitor whether the fund's holdings are turning over significantly (trading in and out of companies frequently) or whether it is more stable. High turnover increases costs and tax inefficiency. Finally, consider whether small-cap stocks in general fit your portfolio: they can be rewarding but are not a substitute for diversification across market caps and styles.
