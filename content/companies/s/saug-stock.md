*FT Vest U.S. Small Cap Moderate [Buffer ETF](/buffer-etf/) - August (SAUG) is an exchange-traded fund that gives investors exposure to U.S. small-cap stocks while wrapping that exposure in a structured downside buffer — a mechanism that absorbs losses up to a defined limit but caps how much an investor can gain in strong years. The buffer resets every August, making SAUG a hybrid between a traditional [index fund](/index-fund/) and a protected note.*

## The buffered small-cap trade

SAUG sits in the intersection of two separate market ideas: exposure to U.S. small-cap equities and structured downside protection. Most investors think of these as opposed — you get either upside (with risk) or protection (with forgone gains) — but SAUG attempts to hold both at once, for a price.

The fund's underlying index is the Russell 2000, the broad benchmark for U.S. small-cap stocks (companies roughly in the $300 million to $2 billion market-cap range). Small-caps are more volatile than large-cap stocks; they tend to outperform in strong [bull markets](/bull-market/) and underperform or decline sharply in downturns. They also offer thinner liquidity and higher trading costs than mega-cap names. For some investors, that volatility is the whole appeal — the chance for outsized returns. For others, it is a risk they would rather not take.

The buffer mechanism is SAUG's answer to that second group. In a down year, the fund does not participate in losses below a certain threshold — the "buffer" — typically somewhere around 15% to 20% depending on market conditions and the year of the buffer period. If the Russell 2000 falls 25%, the fund might only decline 10%. But if the Russell 2000 rises 30%, SAUG might only gain 15%. The investor trades some of the outsized upside that small-caps can offer in exchange for real downside cushioning.

## How the buffer works — and why it resets

The buffer is not passive insurance; it is the output of options overlay. FT Vest synthetically buys [put options](/put-option/) (which gain value when prices fall) and sells [call options](/call-option/) (which the investor sells upside to pay for). The effective cap on losses and the ceiling on gains depend on the options market at the time the fund is constructed. More expensive puts (higher insurance) mean tighter buffers; cheaper puts mean looser ones.

Because options expire, the buffer resets annually — in SAUG's case every August. When the reset happens, the fund's put and call positions unwind, and new ones are initiated for the next twelve months. The buffer levels can change from year to year because option prices fluctuate with volatility and [interest rates](/interest-rate/). A year when volatility spikes — making puts expensive — might feature a smaller buffer or a tighter cap. A quiet year might allow looser parameters.

This reset mechanism matters. It means SAUG is not a "set and forget" fund in the way a traditional index fund is. A holder needs to understand that the protection and cap active for the next twelve months depend on options prices *right now*, and at the August reset, new terms apply. The fund's prospectus and fact sheet disclose the current buffer and cap; those figures roll forward each August.

## Costs and the drag of protection

Buffered ETFs charge higher fees than vanilla index funds because the options overlay costs money. SAUG's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) reflects both the small-cap index tracking cost and the expense of buying and maintaining the protective options. The cost is material — typically several times higher than a plain-vanilla small-cap index fund. This drag cuts into returns in up years (when you are already capped) and eats into the cushion in down years.

Investors pay this cost explicitly through the expense ratio and implicitly through the cap on gains. In a 20% small-cap rally, SAUG might only capture 12%, and the fee eats another 0.5% to 1%, leaving the net closer to 10.5%. Over many years, the small-cap index's average returns minus the [buffer fund's](/buffer-fund/) drag can be substantial. The buffer protects downside only if it is actually exercised — that is, only in years when small-caps decline. In choppy or flat years, and especially in strong bull years, the cost of the buffer is not recouped.

## The investor use case — and its limits

SAUG appeals to a specific investor profile: someone who wants small-cap exposure but cannot tolerate the emotional or financial shock of a 30% or 40% decline. Retirees beginning to draw on portfolios, or investors who have experienced the volatility of 2008 or 2020 and cannot stomach it again, can see the attraction of a smaller potential loss. The trade-off — accepting a cap on gains — feels less painful if you expect modest returns or are chiefly concerned with capital preservation.

The trap is assuming the buffer is a free lunch. It is not. Every percentage point of cap you accept is money left on the table in good years, and the fee that makes the buffer possible compounds that cost over time. If the small-cap index returns 10% annually on average and SAUG returns 6% after the cap and fee, that two percentage point annual drag compounds into a material difference over a decade. Conversely, if a severe [bear market](/bear-market/) strikes and small-caps fall 35%, the buffer might limit SAUG to a 15% loss, which is a very real win.

The decision hinges on whether you believe the downside protection is worth the long-term cost. For buy-and-hold small-cap investors, a plain index fund usually makes more sense mathematically. For investors who might panic-sell at the bottom, or who have a genuine floor below which they cannot afford losses, the insurance value of the buffer may justify the drag.

## Tracking and liquidity

SAUG trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) under that ticker. As a structured ETF with options mechanics underneath, liquidity depends on the options market's depth and the fund's assets. Small-cap exposure is naturally less liquid than large-cap, and overlaying options adds another layer. The fund is not illiquid, but trading spreads (the gap between bid and ask prices) may be wider than a plain [Russell 2000 index](/russell-2000/) fund.

The buffer itself creates a tracking challenge. In theory, the fund should track the Russell 2000 with the buffer applied — capped upside, cushioned downside. In practice, the options may drift from their theoretical values, the fund's rehedging decisions can lag market moves, and fees drag returns. Comparing SAUG's actual returns to (Russell 2000 return with the stated buffer applied) is one way to detect whether the fund is tracking its mandate or leaking performance.

## When to research SAUG

Start with the fund's fact sheet on the issuer's website (Fortify Trust), which states the current buffer percentage, cap percentage, and the date of the current reset. The prospectus (filed with the SEC) lays out the fund's mechanics, fees, and risks. Watch the quarterly or [annual report](/10-k/) for actual performance versus the Russell 2000 and any slippage in the buffer or cap.

If considering SAUG for a portfolio, ask yourself: Is the buffer the reason you are buying, or does the small-cap index appeal to you and the buffer is just a nice-to-have? If the buffer is the main draw, you are paying for insurance you hope never to use. If small-cap exposure is the core thesis, the buffer probably costs more than it is worth. Whichever way you lean, do the math: compare SAUG's historical returns (over periods including both up and down years) to the Russell 2000's own returns minus the stated cap and fee. That honest comparison reveals whether the buffer's value proposition adds up.
