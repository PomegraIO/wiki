SRTY is a triple-leveraged [inverse ETF](/inverse-etf/) that moves in the opposite direction from the Russell 2000, the main index of smaller American companies. When small-cap stocks drop, SRTY rises — and with 3x leverage, it rises three times as fast. It is a tool for traders who think the small-cap rally has gone too far and see a correction ahead.

## The small-cap universe and why shorting it matters

The Russell 2000 contains roughly two thousand U.S. companies with [market capitalizations](/market-capitalization/) from about 200 million to several billion dollars — the forgotten middle of the market. These are the companies you have never heard of, the regional manufacturers, the niche software firms, the middle-market financial-services providers that make up the bulk of the American economy. Unlike the megacap companies that dominate the S&P 500, small-caps are sensitive to economic cycles, credit availability, and investor sentiment. When money flows toward safety, small-cap stocks get hit hard. When the economy accelerates and investors hunt for growth, small-caps soar.

SRTY bets against this dynamic. It profits when small-caps decline. A trader would buy SRTY in the expectation that small-cap stocks are overvalued, that economic growth is slowing, or that investors are rotating into large-cap safety. The 3x leverage amplifies the gain if that thesis plays out over hours or days.

## How the leverage amplifies both gains and losses

SRTY uses a combination of [derivative contracts](/derivative-contract/) — index futures and swaps — to track three times the inverse daily return of the Russell 2000. If the Russell 2000 falls 2 percent in a day, SRTY aims to rise 6 percent. If the Russell 2000 rises 2 percent, SRTY aims to fall 6 percent.

The word "aims" matters. The daily reset mechanism means that SRTY does not actually deliver three times the annual return of the short position — it delivers three times the daily return, and then resets. Over a one-month period of random ups and downs, this difference compounds into real money. If small-caps bounce around without a clear downward trend, SRTY will lose value even if small-caps end up lower than they started. This is the cost of leverage and daily rebalancing, and it eats into returns in every sideways or choppy market environment.

## The cost of holding inverse leverage

Anyone holding SRTY for more than a few days should understand the headwinds. The fund charges a higher [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) than an ordinary ETF because the leverage — the borrowed capital and the derivative positions — costs money every day. Additionally, the derivatives that power the 3x inverse effect carry embedded costs that show up as tracking error or slippage relative to three times the theoretical short position.

For very long [holding periods](/holding-period/) — months or years — the math becomes brutal. Even if the Russell 2000 declines 20 percent over a year, SRTY might deliver only a small positive return or even a loss, because the daily reset, funding costs, and rollover costs on derivatives subtract from what the math otherwise suggests. This is why SRTY is a tactical instrument, not a strategic allocation. It is meant to be traded, held for days or weeks when a near-term thesis is in play.

## Why people use it and when

Traders and portfolio managers use SRTY for three main reasons. First, a short-term tactical bet: a trader who believes small-cap stocks are about to decline on earnings misses, economic weakness, or Fed policy can buy SRTY and profit from the next leg down. Second, a hedge: a portfolio manager heavy in small-cap stocks can buy a modest position in SRTY to offset the downside if the market turns sour. Third, a momentum trade: if small-caps begin to roll over (decline for a few days in a row), momentum traders pile into SRTY to amplify the trend.

None of these uses expect to hold SRTY for years. All of them are predicated on selling within weeks or months, ideally within days once the thesis plays out.

## What can go wrong

The obvious risk is that your bet is wrong. If you buy SRTY expecting small-caps to fall and they instead rally 10 percent, you will lose roughly 30 percent of your investment (the 3x inverse leverage on the upside). Losses can be substantial.

Beyond directional risk, there is volatility decay. A small-cap index that swings up and down randomly will eat away at SRTY's value through daily reset compounding, even if it ends at the same level. There is [counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/): the derivatives SRTY uses to achieve leverage depend on the creditworthiness of the counterparties issuing the swaps. There is [liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/): if you buy SRTY and later want to exit quickly, a wide [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) or low trading volume could make your exit expensive.

And there is the basic human risk: holding a leveraged inverse position requires discipline. It is easy to hold onto a losing position in the hope it bounces back, but with leverage, the losses accelerate. Anyone using SRTY must commit to predefined loss limits and exit rules before entering the trade.

## Research and appropriate use

Read the prospectus on ProShares' website for the full disclosure on fees, leverage mechanics, and risks. Check the rolling twelve-month return and compare it to three times the negative return of the Russell 2000; any meaningful gap is volatility decay at work. Look at the bid-ask spread and the daily volume before trading.

SRTY is appropriate only for traders and sophisticated investors with a clear, time-limited bearish thesis on small-cap stocks. It is not suitable for buy-and-hold portfolios, retirement accounts, or anyone uncomfortable with rapid losses. For buy-and-hold investors seeking small-cap exposure, a standard [Russell 2000 index](/russell-2000/) fund is far simpler. For those seeking downside protection, traditional hedges like [put options](/put-option/) or short positions in individual stocks are more cost-effective over long horizons.

Use SRTY as a tactical trade. Define your entry, your profit target, and your stop loss before you buy. Exit when one of them is hit. Do not hold on hope that small-caps will eventually fall. That way lies expensive lessons.
