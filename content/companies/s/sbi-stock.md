Western Asset Intermediate Muni Fund Inc. is a closed-end investment company whose sole business is to acquire and hold a portfolio of [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/) and deliver the tax-exempt income those [bonds](/bond/) produce to its shareholders. The fund's shares trade on an exchange ([NASDAQ](/nasdaq/): SBI) like a stock, but the fund itself is not an operating business; it is a legal container that pools investor capital, buys municipal debt, and distributes the interest payments to shareholders. The portfolio is actively managed by Western Asset Management, an affiliate of the fund, which selects which bond issuers to own and which to avoid.

## How the fund works

The fund operates like a [perpetual bond](/perpetual-bond/) portfolio. Investors buy shares at whatever price the market sets (which may be at a discount or premium to the underlying bond values, creating its own trading dynamics). The fund collects interest payments from the bonds in its portfolio each month and distributes nearly all of that income to shareholders as taxable to the shareholder only at the federal and state level, rather than at the corporate level — that is the entire point of municipal bonds and why they command lower yields than taxable bonds. The fund also gradually reinvests principal from maturing or called bonds into new securities, which resets the portfolio composition over time.

A closed-end fund differs crucially from an open-end [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/): shares do not grow or shrink based on investor demand. Instead, the fund issues a fixed number of shares, investors trade them among themselves on the exchange, and the fund's total assets grow or shrink only when securities mature or the fund raises or returns capital. This structure creates a static pool of capital that the manager can deploy consistently, without the burden of sudden investor redemptions (which plague [open-end funds](/open-end-fund/) when sentiment turns negative).

## What the fund holds

The portfolio consists of municipal bonds issued by states, cities, counties, and other local authorities. Munis finance schools, roads, water systems, and other public projects. Most of the fund's holdings are rated investment-grade (BBB or higher), with a managed allocation to lower-rated but higher-yielding securities for income. The "intermediate" label means the average [bond maturity](/bond-maturity-corporate/) is somewhere in the 5–10 year range, which gives the fund moderate interest-rate sensitivity — not as volatile as long-term bonds, but more exposed to rate moves than short-term money-market funds.

The manager's job is to spot [credit risk](/credit-risk/): identifying which issuers are deteriorating, which have hidden pension liabilities or budget pressures, and which are improving. A single major default or significant [credit event](/credit-event-sovereign/) can ripple across the portfolio. [Diversification](/diversification/) helps — the fund holds hundreds of positions, so any one issuer is a small slice — but municipal defaults are rare enough that the fund's real risk is not dramatic blow-ups but rather slow deterioration in credit quality that forces down bond prices year over year.

## The core vulnerabilities

The primary risk is rising [interest rates](/interest-rate/). When rates rise, existing bonds (which pay a fixed coupon) become less valuable. A municipal bond yielding 3% becomes less desirable if new bonds are being issued at 5%; investors will sell the old bond, pushing its price down. For a closed-end fund, that loss is marked in real time: the share price reflects the market value of the underlying bonds. A shareholder who bought at a time of low rates and then holds through a rising-rate environment will see the share price fall even as distributions continue. Over many years, if the fund reinvests into higher-yielding bonds, that loss can be partially recovered, but in the near term, rate risk is real.

The secondary risk is credit risk. Municipal budgets are cyclical; good times mask structural problems that emerge during [recessions](/recession/). If a major issuer defaults (rare but not impossible) or deteriorates significantly, the fund's portfolio loses value and distributions may fall. The fund tries to manage this through diversification and credit analysis, but [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) can appear unexpectedly — for example, if a sector like [pension obligations](/pension-obligation/) suddenly becomes underfunded across multiple issuers.

A third risk, unique to [closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/), is the discount/premium trap. If the fund's share price trades at a steep discount to its [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), the manager might decide to liquidate the fund and return the proceeds to shareholders, which would curtail the income stream. Conversely, if sentiment turns sharply negative, a large discount could be self-fulfilling: the discount itself drives down the share price independent of the bonds' credit quality.

## How a reader would research it

Start with the fund's [annual report](/10-k/) and fact sheet, which break down the portfolio holdings by state and sector, disclose the average [credit rating](/credit-rating/), and show the distribution history. The SEC filing (10-K for annual) is verbose but contains material details on credit policy and recent changes to the portfolio. Watch quarterly distributions and the fund's net asset value; a sustained decline in either is worth investigating. The fund's manager materials often explain the macroeconomic outlook for munis, interest rates, and specific state and local credit trends.

The most useful metric is the [current yield](/current-yield/) on the share price versus the net asset value yield; a wide gap signals either a discount opportunity or an expensive market valuation. For long-term holders, the real return depends on price appreciation (or [depreciation](/depreciation/)) plus distributions — far more on the former than most income investors admit.
