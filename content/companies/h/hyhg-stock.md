*HYHG is an exchange-traded fund that holds a portfolio of high-yield [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) while simultaneously using Treasury [futures contracts](/futures-contract/) to hedge away the interest-rate sensitivity of those holdings, leaving shareholders exposed primarily to credit-spread changes rather than Treasury-rate movements.*

## Why hedge interest rates in a bond fund?

[High-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) move on two axes. One is the level of Treasury yields: when the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises rates, all [bond](/bond/) prices fall because a new bond issued today can offer higher yield than yesterday's bond, making yesterday's bond worth less. The second axis is [credit spread](/credit-spread/) — the additional yield that investors demand to hold [junk bonds](/junk-bond/) rather than Treasuries. Credit spreads widen when investors get nervous about default risk and tighten when risk appetite returns.

A conventional high-yield fund captures both movements together. HYHG separates them. By shorting Treasury futures in proportion to the bond portfolio's duration, the fund offsets the effect of Treasury-rate changes. When rates rise, the bond holdings fall in value, but the short futures position gains, and the two cancel. When rates fall, the bond holdings rise, but the futures position loses. The net effect: HYHG's return depends almost entirely on whether credit spreads widen or tighten.

## The appeal and the cost

Isolating credit spread is useful for investors who believe they have an edge predicting spread direction but want to avoid the noise of interest-rate movements. If an investor thinks junk-bond credit is undervalued and spreads will compress, HYHG lets them make that bet without betting that the Federal Reserve will cut rates. Conversely, if an investor is unsure about the direction of rates and wants to focus on companies' actual credit quality, HYHG strips away the rate uncertainty.

The cost is complexity and [basis risk](/basis-risk/). The Treasury-futures hedge is not perfect. The exact duration of a high-yield bond portfolio fluctuates as credit spreads widen and narrow (bonds get shorter-duration when spreads widen, longer when they tighten). A futures position fixed at one point in time will drift away from perfect hedging over weeks and months. In extreme rate moves, the hedge can slip materially. Furthermore, fund expenses are incurred to maintain the futures positions and handle the daily settlement of mark-to-market variation, all of which erodes returns compared to a fund that simply holds bonds and does nothing else.

## When the hedge works and when it doesn't

HYHG thrives when credit spreads and [interest rates](/interest-rate/) move in opposite directions. That happens often: in risk-off moments, both rates and spreads typically move in the same direction (spreads widen, and the Fed cuts rates), so the hedge partially offsets losses. In risk-on rallies, rates rise (the Fed tightens) and spreads compress, so the fund captures the spread compression without the drag of rising rates.

The fund struggles when spreads and rates move together. In a classic credit crunch, spreads widen sharply even as rates fall, so HYHG captures the full spread widening. But in a scenario where [inflation surprises](/inflation-surprise/) upward, rates spike, and credit spreads tighten, the rising-rate headwind is exactly offset by the hedge, and the fund captures only the modest spread tightening. This can be slower progress than a leveraged junk-bond fund would deliver in the same scenario.

## Research and practical considerations

Examine the fund's prospectus to understand the hedging mechanics: how often the futures position is rebalanced, what Treasury maturity is used, and how duration is calculated. Compare HYHG's performance to an unhedged high-yield fund across different rate environments — when rates fell sharply, when they rose sharply, and when spreads moved without rate movement. That comparison will show you whether the hedge is adding or subtracting value in the regimes that matter most to you.

Track the fund's hedging ratio (position size as a proportion of holdings) and monitor its basis risk. In normal markets, HYHG should move closely with the underlying high-yield spread index. Large deviations suggest the hedge is slipping. Because the fund holds futures, it carries [counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/) — though ProShares is a large, regulated issuer and that risk is minimal in practice. Like any junk-bond fund, HYHG is vulnerable to a wide-scale [credit event](/credit-event-sovereign/); the rate hedge does not protect against that, only against interest-rate movements.
