*The **Hedgeye Quality [Growth ETF](/growth-etf/)** (ticker HGRO) is an exchange-taught fund that applies a proprietary quality and growth framework to select large-capitalization US companies. Rather than buying the entire market or following a fixed index, HGRO uses a systematic rules-based process — rooted in fundamental analysis and the independent research of its sponsor, Hedgeye Risk Management — to identify companies with strong profitability, efficient capital deployment, and durable earnings growth potential.*

## From hedge-fund research to ETF indexing

Hedgeye was founded as an independent research and advisory firm specializing in equity analysis and risk management. Its analysts studied individual companies in depth, assessed their competitive advantages, and developed conviction about which would outperform. For years, that work was primarily delivered as proprietary research to institutional clients and through public commentary. 

The transition to HGRO represents a shift: packaging the firm's investment philosophy into a transparent, liquid ETF vehicle available to individual investors. Rather than asking clients to pay for a [hedge fund's](/hedge-fund/) active management, HGRO offers a rules-based ETF that captures the essence of Hedgeye's stock-picking lens — a democratization of institutional research into a low-cost, holdings-transparent format. The fund launched as a way to scale Hedgeye's research approach across a broader audience while maintaining the discipline and framework that underpins it.

## The quality and growth screen

HGRO does not select stocks randomly. It applies a systematic framework that evaluates US large-cap stocks on a combination of quality and growth metrics. Quality, in Hedgeye's framework, typically refers to companies with high and sustainable [return on equity](/return-on-equity/) (the profit generated per dollar of shareholder capital invested), clean [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/), consistent earnings power, and evidence of durable competitive advantages — moats that keep competitors at bay.

Growth screens for earnings trajectory: the fund favors companies whose profits are expanding and whose growth rates outpace the broader economy or peer group. The exact weighting between quality and growth can shift based on market conditions and the fund manager's outlook, but both are essential components.

The screening process filters the universe of large US stocks down to a smaller, higher-conviction portfolio. The result is typically 50 to 100 or so holdings, far more concentrated than a total-market index (which holds thousands) but far more diversified than a traditional active hedge fund (which might hold 10 to 20 stocks).

## Systematic rules, human research behind them

HGRO is rules-based, meaning the criteria for inclusion are transparent and mechanical — you can understand exactly why a stock is held. But those rules were designed by Hedgeye's research team, informed by years of analyzing what actually drives long-term stock returns. This is not a black-box machine-learning model; it is a distilled set of heuristics grounded in fundamental value-investing principles.

The fund is rebalanced periodically to ensure existing holdings still meet the quality and growth criteria and to capture any changes in the opportunity set. Stocks that lose quality or whose growth slows are rotated out; those that now pass the screen are added.

## What kind of companies HGRO favors

The typical HGRO portfolio skews toward established large-cap companies in sectors like technology, financials, healthcare, and consumer — the sectors where quality and growth are most often found in the large-cap space. It is not a small-growth fund or a deep-value fund; it occupies the "quality growth" middle ground: profitable, growing, reasonably valued.

Technology companies with durable competitive advantages, strong profitability, and expanding markets often feature prominently. Financial institutions with high returns on equity and fortress balance sheets are common. Healthcare companies with pricing power and recurring revenue streams fit the mold. The fund naturally gravitates away from cyclical, capital-intensive, or low-margin businesses unless they show genuine quality and growth.

## Cost, transparency, and performance trade-offs

HGRO carries a moderate [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), higher than a passive broad-market index (which costs less than 0.05%) but far lower than a traditional active [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/) or hedge fund (which often charge 1% or more). The fund is transparent — holdings are published regularly, and the selection rules are disclosed. This transparency is a feature: investors know what they own and why.

The performance question is perennial: does quality and growth selection add value after costs? Some periods favor quality-growth strategies; others favor value, momentum, or equal-weight approaches. Over the long term, disciplined stock-picking with a clear framework has historically outperformed the market, but not by vast amounts, and not consistently year by year. The fee is modest enough that HGRO does not need to generate exceptional alpha (excess return beyond the market) to be worthwhile, but it must deliver competitive relative returns to justify the active management layer on top of a passive index structure.

## Who uses HGRO and why

HGRO appeals to investors who believe in fundamental stock-picking but want the discipline, transparency, and cost efficiency of an ETF structure rather than a hedge fund. It is used by those who trust Hedgeye's research framework and want exposure to US large-cap stocks filtered through that lens. It can serve as a core US equity holding in a portfolio, or as part of a multi-fund allocation if an investor wants systematic quality and growth exposure as a complement to value or dividend-focused strategies.

It is less suited for passive index advocates who believe no fund manager, no matter how skilled, consistently adds value net of fees. It is also not the right tool for investors seeking concentrated conviction bets or truly active, tactical portfolio management.

## Research considerations

Start with Hedgeye's public materials and the fund's prospectus, which explain the quality and growth criteria in detail. Compare HGRO's holdings and sector weightings against a broad large-cap benchmark or other quality-growth ETFs to understand how different the fund's picks are from the market.

Review rolling performance (one-year, three-year, five-year) versus the Nasdaq-100, the S&P 500, and other quality-growth competitors to assess whether the selection framework is working. Watch for style drift: if the fund's quality and growth criteria are not being consistently applied, holdings may diverge from the stated philosophy.

Monitor Hedgeye's public research and commentary; a divergence between the fund's actual holdings and the firm's stated research views is a red flag. And as always, compare the expense ratio and tax efficiency (capital gains realized from trading) to similar active and passive alternatives to ensure the cost-benefit trade-off is attractive to you.
