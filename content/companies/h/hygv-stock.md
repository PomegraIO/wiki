*The **FlexShares High Yield Value-Scored [Bond](/bond/) [Index Fund](/index-fund/)** (ticker HYGV) is a [high-yield bond fund](/high-yield-bond-fund/) built on a quantitative model that tries to identify which [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) offer the best value — which ones are paying investors the most per unit of risk taken — and tilts the portfolio accordingly. Instead of owning every high-yield bond equally weighted by size, as a traditional index fund would, HYGV leans toward the ones it calculates are undervalued and away from those that look expensive.*

## What exactly does "value-scored" mean?

Most high-yield [bond ETFs](/bond-etf/) hold all the bonds in an index with weights proportional to their size in the market. The largest issuers get the largest positions. This approach is passive and systematic, which is good for keeping costs low and avoiding manager bias. But it has a quirk: it means you automatically own more of whatever bonds are most expensive (because those issuers have the most debt outstanding) and less of whatever bonds are cheapest.

HYGV takes a different approach. Northern Trust, using a model built into the FlexShares framework, calculates a valuation score for each high-yield bond in its universe. The score is based on factors like yield-to-maturity relative to historical averages, [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) relative to peers, and the issuer's financial metrics. Bonds that score high on value — bonds that are paying you a lot of yield relative to their risk — get larger positions. Bonds that score low on value — bonds that are pricey and not paying enough yield to justify the risk — get smaller positions. The fund owns the same general basket of high-yield bonds, but the weighting is tilted toward value.

## Does tilting toward value actually work?

The theory is sound. In equity markets, tilting a portfolio toward cheap, undervalued stocks — rather than expensive, trendy ones — has historically produced better long-term returns. The idea is that you are buying dollar bills for fifty cents; over time the market recognizes the value and prices correct upward. There is no reason the same principle should not apply to bonds.

In practice, the evidence is mixed. Sometimes HYGV outperforms a traditional high-yield bond index. Other times it underperforms, particularly when market sentiment is strong and investors deliberately pay up for the safest or most popular bonds, regardless of valuation. A value tilt works best when the market is irrational; when the market is relatively rational, the extra cost of the quantitative model drags on returns.

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is slightly higher than a plain index high-yield fund like HYG, reflecting the ongoing cost of running the valuation model and rebalancing. Whether that extra cost is justified depends on how often the model's tilts actually add value versus how often they subtract it.

## How often does HYGV rebalance?

The fund rebalances regularly — typically quarterly or semi-annually — to re-score all holdings and adjust weights to match the current valuation rankings. This frequent rebalancing keeps the fund aligned with its stated strategy but also introduces friction and trading costs. Every rebalance involves buying some bonds (those that have become cheaper) and selling others (those that have become more expensive), and these transactions have costs. The fund absorbs these costs, which show up in the fund's net return relative to its benchmark.

This dynamic — constant rebalancing to chase the latest valuation signals — is one reason a value tilt in bonds is less proven than in stocks. In stocks, rebalancing between cheap and expensive names can be done with tight spreads and low costs. In bonds, which are less liquid, rebalancing into a specific bond because it looks cheap can be expensive, and the savings from holding it might not cover the transaction cost of the original purchase.

## Who is HYGV for?

HYGV is for investors who believe that quantitative valuation models can identify mispricings in the high-yield bond market and who are comfortable with the added complexity and cost of a rules-based value tilt. It is best suited for core portfolio holdings rather than tactical trades, because the model is designed to work over full [market cycles](/market-cycle/).

It is not suitable for investors who believe that bond markets are generally efficient and that tilting the portfolio based on a quantitative model is unlikely to add value after costs. It is also not suitable for someone who simply wants the broadest, cheapest possible high-yield exposure; a traditional index fund like HYG is better for that purpose.

## How does HYGV compare to its peers?

There are several other factor-based or smart-beta high-yield ETFs on the market, each with its own methodology. Some tilt toward bonds with lower volatility, others toward bonds with the highest yields, others toward issuers with the strongest cash flows. HYGV's specific angle is valuation. To evaluate whether it is worth owning, compare its performance to a plain high-yield index and to other factor-based alternatives over several market cycles. Look for whether outperformance is consistent or sporadic. And always verify that the outperformance, when it exists, is larger than the extra expense ratio and trading costs.

## What should you watch if you own HYGV?

Monitor the fund's composition relative to HYG or another broad high-yield index. If HYGV is tilted dramatically toward smaller, less-liquid issuers because they score well on valuation, that is a sign the model might be chasing risk rather than opportunity. Watch also the yield difference: if HYGV is yielding significantly more than HYG, it is tilted toward higher-coupon bonds, which might indicate the model is weighting older, less attractive credits. Conversely, if HYGV is yielding less, it might be tilted toward newer, tighter-spread bonds — which could be opportunity or could be overpaying for perceived safety. The prospectus and quarterly fact sheets explain the methodology; review them to understand the specific valuation factors being used.
