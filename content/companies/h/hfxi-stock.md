*The **NYLI FTSE International Equity Currency Neutral ETF** (ticker HFXI) is an exchange-traded fund that gives investors exposure to large-cap [stock markets](/stock-market/) across developed and emerging economies — Europe, Asia, the Americas outside the US — while stripping out the currency effect. When you own foreign stocks, your returns depend on two things: how much the stocks themselves rise or fall, and whether the foreign currency strengthens or weakens against the dollar. HFXI hedges away the currency swings, so an investor's gain or loss is purely from the stocks themselves, not muddied by foreign-exchange noise.*

> "The same companies. Without the currency casino."

## What the FTSE index holds

HFXI follows the FTSE Developed and Emerging Index (or a closely similar version), which is a broad measure of large-cap publicly traded companies in developed economies like the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Japan, Australia, Canada, and a selection of emerging markets such as India, Brazil, and South Korea. The index excludes the United States, so it is a pure non-US international play.

The fund holds hundreds of individual stocks, weighted by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) — the largest companies get the largest positions — so it naturally overweights economically dominant regions and sectors. Japan and Europe typically make up substantial portions; it is closer to a global index than a niche regional bet.

The stocks themselves span the full breadth of the economy: financial institutions, industrial manufacturers, pharmaceuticals, technology firms, energy companies, and consumer-goods makers. HFXI is diversified across sectors and geographies, which is why it is sometimes used as a core international holding rather than a targeted play on a single country or theme.

## Currency hedging: what it does

When you buy a stock listed in euros and the euro weakens against the dollar, your dollar-based return is diminished even if the stock itself held steady. That foreign-exchange swing is noise if you are trying to isolate the performance of the underlying stocks. HFXI hedges that away.

The fund accomplishes this through currency forwards — contracts that lock in an exchange rate. When the fund buys a Japanese stock, it simultaneously sells yen forward, locking in the rate at which it will convert the yen proceeds back to dollars later. If the yen weakens, the forward gain offsets the stock's currency-drag loss. If the yen strengthens, the forward is a drag, but the stock's currency gain is neutralized. The net effect: the fund's returns reflect the stocks' performance in their home currencies, translated to dollars at a fixed rate, unaffected by subsequent exchange-rate moves.

This comes at a cost. Maintaining currency forwards requires entering into a constant sequence of contracts, and the cost of those contracts — the spread between buying and selling rates — nibbles at returns. In periods when the dollar is strengthening, the hedges are protecting against real losses, so the cost feels justified. When the dollar is weakening and unhedged investors are gaining from currency appreciation, the hedges feel like a drag.

## Hedged versus unhedged: the tradeoff

An investor choosing between HFXI (hedged) and an unhedged [international ETF](/international-etf/) is making a statement about currency views. If you believe the dollar is likely to stay strong or strengthen further, hedging protects you; you get the stock returns without the additional currency hit. If you think the dollar is overvalued and will weaken, you would rather own unhedged international stocks and capture the upside from both rising stock prices and a rising foreign currency.

Unhedged international ETFs are often cheaper — no forwards to maintain — and they have historically slightly outperformed hedged versions over long periods, mainly because the dollar has not consistently strengthened enough to justify the hedging cost. But the real appeal of hedging is not about return predictions; it is about controlling what you are betting on. If you want exposure to European stocks specifically and not a compound bet on European stocks plus a weak euro, HFXI isolates the former.

## Who uses it and why

HFXI is often held by investors who already have substantial US equity exposure and want to add ballast in the form of foreign [dividend](/dividend/) payers and large established companies outside America without taking a currency bet. It can also appeal to those who think the dollar is near a peak and wish to avoid [currency depreciation](/currency-depreciation/) drag on their international allocation.

The fund can be part of a diversified global portfolio alongside US equity and [bond](/bond/) holdings. Because international developed-market stocks often move somewhat independently of the US market, especially during periods of regional stress (European sovereign-debt crises, Asian slowdowns), they provide [diversification](/diversification/). The emerging-market component adds an additional layer of growth and volatility.

## Risks and limitations

A hedged fund is not risk-free. The currency forwards create [counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/) — if the institutions on the other side of those contracts fail, the hedge can unwind badly, though regulators monitor this closely. More realistically, if the dollar weakens sharply, the hedges will be an anchor, leaving the fund's return looking mediocre while unhedged funds soar.

The fund's performance also depends entirely on the underlying stock markets. A downturn in European or Asian equities will hurt HFXI just as it would hurt an unhedged version; the only difference is that HFXI's losses will not be magnified (or mitigated) by currency moves.

The FTSE index leans toward large, established companies and may underweight smaller-cap or more innovative firms that might grow faster. Emerging-market exposure is included but typically at lower weights than some emerging-market-focused funds maintain.

## How to research HFXI

Start with the fund's prospectus and recent fact sheet to confirm the current geographic and sector weightings. Compare the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) — which should reflect the modest cost of maintaining currency hedges — against both hedged and unhedged international ETF alternatives to understand the price trade-off.

Look at the fund's total return (including dividends and the effect of currency hedges) over rolling one-year, three-year, and five-year periods, and compare it to unhedged international ETFs and a global stock index. Track the underlying FTSE index performance to verify the fund is tracking it closely, with minimal tracking error.

Monitor the dollar's trend; if the dollar is weakening, HFXI will look less attractive versus unhedged peers, but that is a feature, not a bug — it is doing exactly what it is designed to do. For investors seeking true international diversification untethered to a dollar bet, HFXI provides a clean vehicle.
