*HIPS is an exchange-traded fund that holds a basket of US stocks selected and weighted by their [dividend yields](/dividend-yield/). It tracks the Solactive US High [Dividend](/dividend/) Yield index, a rules-based strategy that identifies the dividend-paying companies in the broad US market and overweights the ones offering the highest [current yield](/current-yield/) — a straightforward bet that income-seeking investors will find familiar, though with important trade-offs embedded in its construction.*

## The dividend-yield tilt: building the index

HIPS constructs its holdings by starting with a wide universe of US-traded stocks and filtering for those that pay dividends. The fund then weights them according to their yield — stocks offering higher current dividend payments get a larger allocation, creating a portfolio that is systematically tilted toward income over growth. This approach is mechanical and transparent: the index rules are public, and the fund simply buys whatever the index tells it to hold.

The consequence is a portfolio heavily concentrated in value sectors and mature, slower-growing industries. Utilities, energy companies, [real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/) (REITs), and telecoms typically dominate a high-dividend-yield portfolio because they are obligated or incentivized to return cash to shareholders rather than reinvest it. Conversely, high-growth technology and consumer-discretionary companies — which often retain earnings to fund expansion — appear sparingly or not at all. That concentration is both the fund's appeal to income investors and a genuine risk to [diversification](/diversification/).

## What dividend yield actually means

The current dividend yield — the annual dividend paid divided by the share price — is a backward-looking number that captures only the most recent quarter or year of payouts. A yield that looks attractive one quarter can shrink sharply if the stock's price rises, or become a trap if the dividend is cut. HIPS' index methodology buys high yields as they appear, which can lead the fund to chase yields right before they disappoint, and to avoid cheap, low-yielding stocks that might become the next decade's outperformers. This is a structural bias toward mature, stable businesses, which sounds safe but frequently underperforms in periods when the economy accelerates and value stocks outrun.

Dividend payments themselves are not free money — they reduce the cash on a company's [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/), and the tax owed on dividends (at the federal, state, and sometimes local level) is owed by the shareholder, not the fund. For taxable accounts, high-dividend funds are notoriously tax-inefficient because the bulk of the return arrives as taxable income rather than tax-deferred capital gains. A shareholder in a high marginal tax bracket can lose a substantial portion of HIPS' returns to taxes, making the after-tax performance of high-dividend funds much weaker than the headline yield suggests.

## Concentration and rebalancing rhythm

Because the index overweights the highest-yielding stocks, HIPS' holdings are not evenly distributed across the market. A few mega-cap stocks in utilities, REITs, and energy may anchor a significant portion of the fund's assets, and that concentration grew wider when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rose sharply — existing dividend stocks became more attractive relative to [bonds](/bond/), pushing yields down and prices up, which paradoxically made some of the fund's core holdings less attractive by the index's own logic at the next quarterly rebalance.

The quarterly rebalance (when HIPS reshuffles its holdings to match the newly calculated index) can also create tax consequences and trading costs, particularly in volatile markets where yield rankings shift rapidly. An investor considering HIPS should check the fund's [turnover ratio](/turnover-ratio/) and tax-efficiency metrics in its fact sheet to understand how much annual trading is driving costs.

## Who HIPS is built for

HIPS appeals most to retirees or others seeking steady current income from equity holdings, or to investors who believe value stocks and dividend payers are underappreciated. The fund's broad US exposure and low [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) make it cheaper than actively managed dividend funds, and the rules-based approach eliminates the risk of a manager's poor stock-picking decisions — you get exactly the dividend-yield tilt the index delivers.

That said, income-focused investors should be aware of what they are not getting: diversification into growth, exposure to companies that innovate and expand rather than return capital, or any protection against the risk that dividend stocks fall out of favor (as they have for long stretches in recent decades). For a complete portfolio, HIPS works best alongside other [equity ETFs](/equity-etf/) that tilt toward growth, value, or size diversification — not as the sole US equity holding.

## How to research HIPS

Start with the fund's fact sheet and prospectus, which detail the index methodology, the current top ten holdings, and the expense ratio. The Solactive website publishes the full index rules and the rationale for dividend-yield weighting. For performance and tax-efficiency, compare HIPS to competing high-dividend ETFs (several exist, with slightly different methodologies and cost structures) over a multi-year horizon, paying close attention to the turnover ratio and after-tax returns. In the fund's [annual report](/10-k/), look for commentary on how the portfolio concentrated or diversified as market conditions changed, and watch whether dividend growth is keeping pace with the universe of potential holdings.
