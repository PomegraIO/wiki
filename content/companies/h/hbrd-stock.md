*The **Invesco U.S. Hybrid [Bond ETF](/bond-etf/) (HBRD)** is an exchange-traded fund that tracks an index of investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) and preferred shares issued by U.S. companies. Rather than holding only traditional [bonds](/bond/) or only equities, HBRD blends the two security types to capture both the downside protection of fixed-income instruments and the higher income potential that [preferred stocks](/preferred-stock/) offer — making it a distinct middle ground for income-focused investors.*

## The hybrid securities landscape

A hybrid security sits at the boundary between debt and equity. A preferred share is technically a stock — it carries no maturity date and no obligation for the company to ever repay the principal — yet it behaves like a bond. Preferred holders receive a fixed, contractual [dividend](/dividend/) ahead of common shareholders, and in bankruptcy they stand ahead of common equity holders but behind traditional bondholders. Corporate hybrids, similarly, blend fixed-income characteristics (regular coupons, high seniority in a bankruptcy queue) with perpetual or very long-dated structures that resemble equity.

This dual nature creates a market niche. For a company, hybrid securities are cheaper to issue than straight equity — they carry the appearance of debt-like reliability — and more flexible than bonds, which must eventually be repaid or refinanced. For investors, hybrids offer yields that exceed [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/) yet carry less volatility and lower default risk than [common stocks](/common-stock/). HBRD capitalises on this opportunity by holding a broad basket of both preferred shares and [subordinated bonds](/subordinated-bond/) issued by large, creditworthy U.S. corporations.

## What HBRD holds and why

The fund tracks the ICE BofA U.S. Hybrid Securities Index, a benchmark that includes U.S.-issued corporate hybrid securities with investment-grade [credit ratings](/credit-rating/). The typical [holding period](/holding-period/) is long-dated: many preferred shares and hybrid bonds have maturities of 30, 40, or 50 years, or are perpetual. This means HBRD buyers are committing capital for decades, betting on the credit stability of the issuer and the bond market's appetite for the security.

The fund holds securities across sectors — banks, energy companies, utilities, and industrials are common issuers of hybrid debt and preferred stock — so [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) is naturally spread. Individual positions tend to be moderate in size, reflecting the size and diversity of the index. Because hybrid securities are less liquid than vanilla bonds or common stocks, HBRD may not move as quickly to respond to market changes, and wide [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) are possible in volatile markets.

## Income, duration, and interest-rate sensitivity

HBRD produces a steady stream of income from the dividends and coupons paid by its holdings. Because preferred shares and hybrid bonds are contractually obliged to pay regular distributions, the fund's yield is typically higher than an investment-grade corporate-bond ETF would offer, and significantly higher than a dividend-paying stock fund. The exact yield depends on market conditions and the fund's turnover, but it is evergreen and the primary return driver for most HBRD shareholders.

Like any fixed-income fund, HBRD is sensitive to interest-rate movements. When yields on new bonds rise, the prices of existing bonds fall — a seesaw effect that hurts the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/). Because hybrid securities are typically long-dated, this interest-rate sensitivity is pronounced: a sharp rise in rates can quickly erode the value of HBRD units, even if the underlying companies remain creditworthy. Conversely, a falling-rate environment can drive gains as existing securities appreciate.

## Risks specific to hybrid securities

Hybrid securities carry risks that more familiar instruments do not. In a severe credit downturn, preferred shares can be suspended by the issuer — the company stops paying the dividend, legally entitled to do so because preferred is technically equity. This is distinct from a bond default: the company is not in breach of a legal obligation, so preferred holders cannot force bankruptcy proceedings. They simply stop receiving income, sometimes for years, waiting for the business to stabilize.

Regulatory changes also present a specific risk. Hybrids are heavily issued by banks, which are tightly regulated; changes to capital requirements or definitions of what counts as tier-1 capital can make certain hybrid securities less attractive to issuers or holders and can suppress their prices. The 2008 financial crisis and the Dodd-Frank aftermath saw such shifts, and future regulations could create similar dislocations.

There is also a [call risk](/call-risk/): many hybrid securities contain embedded [call options](/call-option/) that allow the issuer to repay the security early. If rates fall and the security appreciates, the issuer may call it away, leaving HBRD shareholders with principal returned at an inopportune time — forced to reinvest the proceeds at lower yields. This is a hidden cost to income-focused investors in low-rate environments.

## Costs and the ETF wrapper

HBRD's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is modest by industry standards, typical of passive index-tracking funds. The ETF wrapper itself — the ability to trade HBRD shares on an exchange throughout the day at market prices, rather than waiting for a daily net-asset-value calculation — confers the standard ETF convenience advantages. However, actual trading costs (bid-ask spreads) can be wider than those on more liquid funds because hybrid securities themselves are less liquid. A large shareholder trying to exit a substantial position may move the market and face slippage.

## How to research HBRD

Start with Invesco's fact sheet and prospectus, which detail the fund's current holdings, sector weights, and top individual positions. The ICE BofA index methodology document explains the eligibility criteria and rebalancing rules. Watch the fund's average yield relative to the broader investment-grade bond market — if HBRD's yield advantage compresses, it is a signal that the market is pricing hybrid securities less favourably.

Follow corporate earnings and [credit spreads](/credit-spread/), particularly for the largest issuers in the fund. Bank earnings are crucial, given their outsized role in the [hybrid market](/hybrid-market/). And pay attention to [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) policy and interest-rate expectations: HBRD's price sensitivity to rate moves is significant, and understanding the [yield curve](/yield-curve/) matters more for this fund than fundamental stock-picking does.
