## Small-cap stocks and why size matters

Small-cap stocks are companies with market values typically between $300 million and $2 billion. They are bigger than micro-caps but smaller than mid-caps, and they occupy an interesting middle ground: large enough to be liquid and covered by analysts, small enough to be less efficient and sometimes overlooked by institutions. Small-cap stocks can grow faster than blue-chip giants, because they have more runway for expansion, and they are often more sensitive to economic cycles — they tend to outperform when growth accelerates and underperform during downturns.

HSCZ focuses on small-cap companies in developed markets outside the United States: the United Kingdom, Germany, Japan, Australia, and other mature economies. These are genuine small companies in wealthy countries, not emerging-market microcaps. They face similar competitive pressures as US small-caps but operate in different economies with different growth rates, [interest rates](/interest-rate/), and regulations.

## How MSCI EAFE and hedging work together

EAFE stands for Europe, Australasia, Far East — the developed-market regions outside North America. MSCI EAFE is the standard index for tracking developed-market performance ex-US. The small-cap version of that index selects the smallest publicly traded companies in those regions that meet MSCI's liquidity and financial criteria. HSCZ tracks that index by holding roughly 400 to 500 of those stocks, weighted by market value, so the largest small-cap European bank might be a bigger position than a smaller Australian retailer.

The [currency hedging](/currency-hedging/) is the second layer. Every holding in HSCZ is denominated in a foreign currency — euros in Germany, pounds in the UK, yen in Japan. If the dollar strengthens against those currencies, unhedged foreign stock prices fall in dollar terms, even if the companies themselves do fine. HSCZ removes that translation problem by hedging currency exposure. The fund contracts (usually [forward contracts](/forward-contract/) or options) to lock in exchange rates, so a US dollar investor's return comes from the companies' actual stock performance, not from currency movements.

The hedge is not free — it costs money to maintain, and during periods when the dollar weakens, it prevents investors from capturing the currency upside. But the point of HSCZ is to isolate small-cap equity exposure from currency risk, so that tradeoff is intentional.

## Small-cap international exposure and diversification

HSCZ is not the same as owning US small-cap stocks. International small-caps operate in different regulatory environments, face different customer bases, and respond to different economic cycles. A German manufacturer is exposed to eurozone growth and supply chains; a Japanese retailer is shaped by Japan's particular demographics and credit environment. These differences offer [diversification](/diversification/) — when the US is in [recession](/recession/) but Europe is growing, international small-caps may outperform, and vice versa.

The universe is also smaller. Fewer small-cap companies exist outside the US relative to the number of international mega-caps, so HSCZ's 400+ holdings represent a material percentage of the investable small-cap universe in those regions. That concentration (relative to broader international funds) also means HSCZ's performance can diverge sharply from larger-cap international peers.

## Tracking error and fund expenses

HSCZ aims to track its index closely, but it is not a passive holding — the fund has an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), the hedging costs money, and there is always some tracking error (a small difference between the fund's actual return and the index's return). The hedging cost is meaningful in markets where interest-rate differentials favour the dollar; it is smaller in markets where rates are higher elsewhere. Investors should know the current expense ratio and understand that it applies every year, so even in [flat markets](/flat-market/), HSCZ will slightly underperform the unhedged MSCI EAFE Small-Cap index.

## Currency hedging: the choice

HSCZ is one of several international small-cap options. An unhedged version exists (ticker SCZ), which tracks the same index without currency hedging. The choice between the two depends on your currency view and your portfolio needs. If you believe foreign currencies will strengthen versus the dollar over your [holding period](/holding-period/), unhedged is more appealing because you capture both stock appreciation and currency gains. If you are indifferent to currency or believe the dollar will strengthen, hedged HSCZ isolates you from that headwind. A diversified portfolio might own both, or own hedged HSCZ alongside dollar-denominated [bonds](/bond/) to get the net currency exposure the investor wants.

## How to research HSCZ

Start with the fund fact sheet and prospectus, which detail the current holdings, the tracking-error history, and the exact hedging methodology. Compare HSCZ's year-to-date, one-year, and three-year returns against the unhedged SCZ and against broader international developed-market funds to see how much the hedge has cost or benefited you. Review which countries and sectors are largest in the fund — if Europe dominates and you are concerned about European growth, that's material. Look at the economic calendar for the eurozone and Japan to understand near-term tailwinds and headwinds for the underlying companies.

Consider your total portfolio geography. If you already own US large-caps and US small-caps, adding international small-caps diversifies you into different geographies and economic cycles. If you own international large-caps too, HSCZ fills the small-cap gap. The hedging component is a choice, not a necessity — make sure it aligns with your views on currency and your overall portfolio construction.
