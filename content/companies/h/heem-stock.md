> *"The currency is often the biggest risk in emerging markets — bigger than the stocks themselves."*

The iShares Currency Hedged MSCI Emerging Markets ETF (HEEM) holds a diversified portfolio of large and mid-cap companies across developing economies — primarily China, India, Brazil, Taiwan, South Korea, and Mexico — and removes the foreign-exchange noise by hedging out the exposure to the Chinese yuan, the [Indian rupee](/indian-rupee/), the [Brazilian real](/brazilian-real/), and the other emerging-market currencies that swing against the [U.S. dollar](/us-dollar/).

The fund tracks the [MSCI Emerging Markets Index](/msci-emerging-markets-index/), a broad measure of about 800–1,000 stocks across emerging Asia, Latin America, Eastern Europe, and the Middle East. Without hedging, a U.S. investor buying an emerging-market fund faces two layers of volatility: the price movements of the stocks themselves and the movements of each company's home currency against the dollar. A rising emerging-market currency can amplify stock gains (or losses), while a plunging currency can wipe out returns even if stocks themselves have risen. HEEM strips away that second layer through a systematic forward-currency program that locks in dollar returns and resets daily.

The decision to hedge emerging-market currency exposure is genuinely contentious. In some periods — notably the years following the 2008 financial crisis, when emerging-market currencies weakened sharply — hedging proved expensive and dragged on returns. In other periods, when emerging-market currencies depreciated because of commodity crashes or [capital flight](/capital-flight-sovereign/), hedging provided meaningful insurance. The longer-term historical record is mixed: over very long horizons, currency headwinds in emerging markets often persist because many of these economies have higher [inflation](/inflation/) than the United States, which tends to weaken their currencies over time. An investor hedging away that drift trades away a long-term [depreciation](/depreciation/) risk in exchange for short-term predictability.

HEEM's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is roughly 50–60 basis points, slightly higher than an unheged emerging-market ETF because of the cost of running the currency hedge. That cost is real — WisdomTree and other [ETF sponsors](/etf-sponsor/) pay the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) on the [forward contracts](/forward-contract/) daily, and those costs accumulate. In a period where emerging-market currencies strengthen (making hedging expensive), the drag is more acute. In a period where they weaken (making hedging valuable), the fund earns back some of that cost.

The fund's cyclical story is tied closely to emerging-market currencies themselves, which tend to move sharply in risk-on and risk-off periods. In risk-on phases — when investors reach for higher-yielding assets and emerging markets are in favor — emerging-market currencies often strengthen alongside stock prices, so hedging costs investors potential returns. In risk-off phases — when investors flee to the safety of dollars — emerging-market currencies crumble, and a hedged fund holder is insulated from that damage. For U.S.-based investors, HEEM is appealing in times of financial stress or expected volatility when currency [diversification](/diversification/) is a liability; it is less appealing in calm environments where investors are comfortable with foreign-exchange risk.

The core holdings are the same as any emerging-market index: large state-owned or private enterprises in oil, mining, manufacturing, technology, and banking across China, India, Brazil, and dozens of smaller markets. Because emerging markets are far more concentrated in commodities and state-owned firms than developed markets, commodity cycles and geopolitical shifts have outsized influence. A plunge in [crude oil](/crude-oil/) prices or copper prices or [iron ore](/iron-ore/) prices can swing the entire index regardless of stock-specific news. A [trade war](/trade-war/) or sanctions regime can upend entire countries' weightings overnight.

To research HEEM, start by comparing it to the unhedged version — EEM (iShares MSCI Emerging Markets ETF) — over periods of emerging-market currency weakness and strength. Look at the rolling three-year and five-year return difference between the two. Understand your own view on whether emerging-market currencies will strengthen or weaken: if you believe they will appreciate (a bet on emerging-market growth and capital inflows), leave it unhedged; if you expect them to depreciate (inflation, capital flight, commodity weakness), hedging insulates you. Check the fund's daily [currency hedging](/currency-hedging/) cost by looking at its tracking error relative to the underlying index — a persistently higher expense ratio due to hedging costs is a sign that currencies are moving against you.
