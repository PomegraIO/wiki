*The **Hartford Equity Premium [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (HEMI) is an exchange-traded fund that holds a portfolio of US stocks and systematically sells [covered call](/covered-call/) options against them — a strategy of trading potential upside for immediate cash. It is built for investors seeking higher current income, with the understanding that the trade-off is a ceiling on how much the portfolio can appreciate.*

## The mechanics: what a covered call does

A covered call works like this: you own a stock and sell someone the right to buy it from you at a fixed price (the strike) by a set date (the expiration). In exchange, they pay you a premium upfront. If the stock stays below the strike, the option expires worthless, you keep the stock and the premium, and you repeat the process next month. If the stock rockets above the strike, the option gets exercised, you sell your shares at the [strike price](/strike-price/) (not the market price), and the buyer walks away with the upside.

HEMI executes this playbook across its holdings month after month. The result is a portfolio that generates income from two sources: the [dividends](/dividend/) the underlying stocks pay, plus the [option premiums](/option-premium/) collected from selling calls. That combined yield is typically several percentage points higher than a simple dividend-paying stock ETF would produce.

## Why this matters for income seekers

In a rising-rate environment where [bonds](/bond/) and money-market funds offer meaningful yields, selling calls is less compelling. But when Treasury yields are modest and bond funds are returning single digits, an income-focused investor faces a hard choice: accept lower yields or take more risk. HEMI offers a third option: keep equity exposure but harvest option premium to juice the yield. For someone living on portfolio withdrawals, those extra percentage points can mean the difference between a sustainable draw and slowly depleting capital.

The fund is transparent about the game: you get higher income, but you give up the chance to capture a steep rally. If the underlying stocks rise 15% but all the [call options](/call-option/) cap your portfolio at 8% appreciation, you received premium income but forgone capital appreciation. Over full [market cycles](/market-cycle/), that trade-off either looks wise (markets are flat or falling) or regrettable (markets soar).

## The portfolio underneath

HEMI typically holds large-cap and mid-cap US equities — often the same kinds of stocks you would find in a broad market index, though the specific selection depends on Hartford's managers' decision about which holdings support a consistent option-selling strategy. High-quality, liquid stocks with active options markets are preferable because they have tight spreads on options and deep interest from buyers. That naturally skews the portfolio toward names like the largest tech firms, banks, consumer discretionary leaders, and industrial heavyweights.

The fund does not pick stocks for growth or value; it picks them for option depth. That constraint shapes the portfolio in ways that are easy to overlook but matter: the fund cannot easily hold micro-caps or illiquid names even if they look attractive, because there is no active options market to harvest premium from. In effect, the option-selling strategy has already narrowed the investment universe before security selection begins.

## Costs and trading mechanics

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) reflects both the [management fee](/management-fee/) and the cost of executing the options strategy month to month. It is higher than a plain vanilla dividend ETF but typically lower than actively managed [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/). The real cost of the strategy, though, appears in the opportunity cost: a portfolio that would have returned 12% if held naked instead returns 8% because all the call options expired in the money. That 4-percentage-point gap is not explicit on any fee sheet, but it is the core trade-off.

HEMI trades like any ETF — standard settlement, [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) in line with its size and liquidity. The fund itself collects distribution income continuously throughout the month (both from dividends and option premium) and typically distributes monthly to shareholders. Monthly distributions are attractive for income investors who want steady cash flow, though they complicate tax reporting because each distribution can involve both [qualified dividend](/qualified-dividend/) income and short-term capital gains (option premium is generally taxed as short-term gains).

## When this strategy breaks down

The covered call strategy's fatal flaw arrives when a portfolio experiences a sharp drop. If you sold calls when stocks were trading at $100 and the market falls to $70, the calls expire worthless and you keep the premium — which is good, it softens the loss. But the premium collected over the past three months was, say, $3 per share, so your net loss is 27% instead of 30%. The option premium acts as a modest hedge. That sounds appealing, but the real cost of owning through a drawdown is the opportunity it forgoes: if you had not sold calls, you would have the same 30% loss today, but you would still own the stock at $70 and could participate in any bounce-back.

More subtly, systematic call selling creates a bias toward "harvesting" strong performers. If a stock rallies hard, the call gets exercised and you are forced to sell. You then rinse and repeat with a different stock or a new entry point. The net effect, over time, is a kind of forced rebalancing that trims winners and locks in gains. For long-term wealth building, that is often harmful — you want strong performers to compound. For income harvesting, it is the intended outcome: take the money off the table and move on. The trade-off is real and worth examining before committing.

## How to evaluate HEMI

Start by comparing its distribution yield and past quarterly distributions to plain dividend ETFs and to [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/). Ask whether the higher yield is worth the risk of capped upside. Run a scenario: if the market fell 20%, how much loss would the option premium cushion? If it rose 20%, how much would you give up? The prospectus includes the fund's historical distribution rate, so you can see what the strategy has paid out in recent years and whether it is stable or volatile.

Also scrutinise the portfolio composition. A list of the top 20 holdings and the fund's sector weights tells you whether HEMI is tilted toward any particular theme or risk. If it is heavily weighted toward financial stocks and you already own a bank ETF, the overlap means you are implicitly buying that sector twice. The fine print on option strike prices and expirations (usually disclosed in the fact sheet) shows how aggressively the fund is capping upside — a fund that sells calls just slightly out of the money is much more likely to have them exercised and thus pay out capital gains than a fund that sells them far out of the money.

Finally, consider your own tax bracket and investment horizon. If you are in a high tax bracket, monthly distributions of mostly short-term gains are expensive. If you have a 30-year horizon, capping your equity exposure's upside is a significant penalty. HEMI is best suited to lower-taxed accounts (like IRAs) or to investors with a clear income need in the near term who can accept that the trade-off with long-term appreciation.
