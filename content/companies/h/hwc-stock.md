## The lineage: Whitney National Bank and Hancock Bank

The deeper history of Hancock Whitney really begins with two institutions. Whitney National Bank was founded in 1883 in New Orleans, where it became a pillar of Louisiana banking for more than a century. It grew into the largest bank headquartered in Louisiana and carried that identity through generations of prosperity, economic disruption, and the particular upheavals of the Gulf region — the oil boom, the oil bust, and the consequences of Hurricane Katrina.

Hancock Bank, meanwhile, traces its roots to 1899 and Mississippi, where it became the dominant bank in that market and adjacent areas. For decades the two banks were fierce competitors, each dominant in their respective states, each rooted in the regional culture and business networks of their territory. Hancock Bank eventually grew beyond Mississippi into Alabama and Florida.

For most of the twentieth century, these two banks never crossed paths as anything more than regional rivals. But the consolidation wave that defined American banking from the 1990s onward changed the calculus. Both banks needed to grow to compete with national megabanks, and both had geographic strengths that were complementary rather than overlapping. In 2019, the two merged under the name Hancock Whitney, with Hancock Bank's headquarters in Gulfport, Mississippi, becoming the corporate center of the combined entity.

## The combined bank and its geographies

The 2019 merger created a mid-sized regional bank with a franchise spanning Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, and Florida — the Gulf South and the Gulf Coast. The bank operates several hundred branches across these markets and serves everyone from Fortune 500 companies to small family businesses to individual consumers. It is the kind of regional bank that dominates its home markets and can leverage those relationships across a broader Southeast footprint.

Geographically, the franchise is concentrated in areas with specific economic drivers. Louisiana has oil and gas, petrochemical refining, ports, and seafood. Mississippi has energy and gaming. Alabama has manufacturing and aerospace. Florida has real estate, tourism, and wealth concentration. These are not high-growth regions relative to the Sunbelt boom corridors like Texas or North Carolina, but they are stable, mature markets where a dominant bank can extract consistent returns.

## How Hancock Whitney makes money

Like any regional bank, Hancock Whitney generates revenue from two main sources: interest income and fee income.

Interest income comes from the gap between what the bank pays depositors to hold their money and what it charges borrowers to lend it. A depositor might earn 0.5% interest on their savings account; the bank turns around and lends that money to a small business at 6%. The 5.5% spread (minus the cost of operations) is the interest margin. This is the bread and butter of banking. The size of the margin depends on the slope of the [yield curve](/yield-curve/) (the difference between short-term and long-term [interest rates](/interest-rate/)), the credit quality of borrowers, and the competitive intensity of the deposit market.

Fee income comes from services the bank provides: overdraft fees, wire-transfer fees, wealth-management fees (charged as a percentage of assets under management), mortgage-origination fees, commercial-loan fees, and payment-processing fees. In a modern bank, fee income is a growing share of the total, and wealth management in particular is a long-term focus because it is higher-margin and more durable than lending margins.

Hancock Whitney's loan portfolio is weighted toward commercial loans (to small and mid-sized businesses, real estate developers, and commercial property owners) and consumer mortgages. The bank also has a meaningful wealth-management business serving high-net-worth individuals and families, which provides both fee income and deposit relationships.

## The post-merger integration period

The 2019 merger between Whitney National Bank and Hancock Bank was in many ways a [merger of equals](/merger-of-equals/) — two strong regional banks combining to create something larger. But like all [mergers](/merger/), it required integration: consolidating branch networks, merging technology systems, deduplicating back-office functions, and deciding which executives and cultures would define the combined entity. This work took years and came at a cost (merger-related expenses and disruption to customer relationships). By the early 2020s, the worst of the integration work was behind the bank, and it could focus on running the combined entity.

The timing of the merger was relevant. The rate environment at that time was very favorable for banks — interest rates were at historic lows, but banks still had customers and deposit bases from a higher-rate environment, so margins were unusually wide. This meant the merger combined two profitable entities at a moment when the banking industry was benefiting from extraordinary tailwinds. Those conditions did not last.

## The interest-rate reset and margin pressure

By 2023 and 2024, the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) had raised interest rates sharply in response to [inflation](/inflation/), and the whole shape of the banking business changed. When rates are low, a bank's deposit base (the money it owes depositors) costs almost nothing, and lending rates are low, so the margin is tight but predictable. When rates rise, especially when the Fed raises them quickly, the math inverts. Deposits become expensive — customers can now earn 4% or 5% on a savings account, so they move money to higher-yielding products or withdraw it for direct lending. At the same time, the bank's loan portfolio is locked in at older, lower rates.

For a bank like Hancock Whitney that relies on a core deposit base and has a portfolio of mortgages and fixed-rate commercial loans, a rapid rate-hiking cycle is painful. The deposits leave, the loans reprrice slowly, and margins compress. This is the environment the bank faced from 2022 onward, and it hit all regional banks hard.

The bank responded with the standard playbook: raising deposit rates to retain the core base, competing more aggressively for new deposits, reducing costs where possible, and waiting for either the rate cycle to stabilize or for the loan portfolio to reprice at higher rates. These are all real levers, but they work slowly, and in the interim, profitability declines.

## From history to the present

The long view of Hancock Whitney is of a bank that evolved from two powerful regional institutions that were ultimately stronger together than apart, combined at a moment of unusual profitability, and then tested by one of the most disruptive rate environments in modern banking. The merger put the bank in a position to be a durable player in the Gulf South for decades to come — the brands are strong, the customer relationships are deep, the deposit base is sticky. But like all regional banks, Hancock Whitney is hostage to interest rates, to [credit cycles](/credit-cycle/), and to the economic health of its footprint.

## Risks and structural questions

The most obvious risk is interest-rate and credit exposure. If economic conditions deteriorate and borrowers stop paying loans, Hancock Whitney's loan-loss provisions could spike and earnings could contract sharply. The bank is well-capitalized and has strong regulatory oversight, but credit cycles are inevitable, and a bank's earnings in a downturn are materially worse than in expansion.

A second risk is deposit flight. In a world where rates are high, deposits are not loyal — customers will move to higher-yielding options or stay at the bank only if the service and relationship is compelling enough to justify leaving money in a lower-yield account. If Hancock Whitney's deposit base erodes, the bank will be forced to fund its lending through more expensive means (wholesale borrowing, [brokered deposits](/brokered-deposit/)), which further compresses margins.

The third risk is competitive displacement. National banks and out-of-region banks with large capital bases can cherry-pick the best credits in Hancock Whitney's market. Technology and fintech are also gradually eroding the traditional bank's advantages in deposits and lending. Hancock Whitney needs to maintain its franchise strength in its home markets, and that requires continued investment in technology, service, and brand.

## How to research Hancock Whitney as an investment

Start with the annual 10-K (SEC CIK 0000750577), which details the loan portfolio (by type, geography, and credit quality), the deposit base, interest-rate sensitivity, and capital ratios. Quarterly earnings calls are where the most useful color emerges: watch the net-interest margin (the spread between what the bank earns and what it pays out), deposit trends, loan growth, and any commentary on loan-loss provisions or credit deterioration. Pay attention to regulatory filings on capital ratios and any stress-test results published by the Federal Reserve, which show how the bank would perform in a severe economic scenario. The price at which a bank's stock trades reflects the market's expectations about future interest rates, credit conditions, and the bank's ability to maintain its franchise. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell.
