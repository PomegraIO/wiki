The tension: [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) pay well, but crumble when rates spike. HYZD attempts to thread that needle—collect the coupon without the volatility. It holds a high-yield [bond](/bond/) index (several hundred corporate IOUs from lower-rated issuers) but simultaneously holds derivative positions that move opposite to [interest rates](/interest-rate/). When rates rise and bonds fall, the hedge gains. When rates fall and bonds gain, the hedge loses. In theory, the credit income survives intact while duration risk vanishes.

The mechanism relies on index futures or swaps—tools that move in lockstep with rate moves but cost something to maintain. The fund buys the bonds and simultaneously shorts interest-rate exposure (or buys inverse-duration positions). The tracking is mechanical: bond duration divided by derivative sensitivity should equal near-zero net duration. In practice, the hedge is rougher. [Credit spreads](/credit-spread/) and duration are not perfectly correlated. During panic selling, spreads widen faster than duration alone predicts, and the hedge underperforms. The index's duration also drifts as credit quality shifts and bonds approach maturity. The rolling cost is real—WisdomTree charges an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) that includes the ongoing cost of maintaining the derivative positions.

The fund's audience is narrow: income investors who have already decided they want high-yield's return but have concluded they cannot live with its volatility. A retiree drawing 4 to 5 percent annually from portfolio income might use HYZD to lock in the coupon without watching drawdowns of 15 to 20 percent in a rate-hiking cycle. The explicit tradeoff is transparent: muted downside from rising rates in exchange for muted upside if rates fall. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises rates aggressively (as it did 2022–2023), the hedge protects. In periods of stable or falling rates, the hedge is deadweight, and an unhedged high-yield fund outperforms.

The fund rebalances continuously—holdings shift as bonds mature or get downgraded, and the manager rolls the hedge daily to maintain alignment. All of this is hidden from the shareholder but reflected in the expense ratio and in tracking error against a simple high-yield index.

Liquidity is genuine. The ETF trades throughout the day with tight spreads, and because it is a passive index tracker (despite the complexity of the hedge), the expense ratio is reasonable, not exotic. The real cost is the fundamental one: are you paying enough in fees and hedge slippage to justify the volatility dampening? Compare HYZD's volatility and downside in a rising-rate environment directly against an unhedged high-yield fund, then decide if the difference justifies the fee. The prospectus lays out the hedge methodology and tracking data. Inspect the recent performance in both rallies and rate-spike episodes to get a feel for hedge effectiveness in real conditions, not theory.

Suitable for investors near or in retirement who need current income and cannot tolerate a 20 percent portfolio drop. Not suitable for those who believe rates will fall significantly (where the hedge becomes a cost with no benefit) or for portfolios with long time horizons where higher volatility is manageable and mean reversion is expected.
