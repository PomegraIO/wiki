*The **Hartford Strategic [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (ticker HFSI) is an exchange-traded fund built expressly to generate higher income distributions than a conventional stock or [bond](/bond/) index offers. Rather than pursuing capital appreciation, the fund employs a diversified mix of income-producing assets — dividend-paying stocks, [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/), [floating-rate notes](/floating-rate-note/), and sometimes more exotic income sources like preferred shares or covered-call strategies — all configured to send regular cash to shareholders.*

## The income-stacking philosophy

HFSI is built on the premise that investors hunting for yield should not be confined to a single asset class. A portfolio of only [dividend](/dividend/) stocks chases equity risk; bonds alone expose you to interest-rate movement. By layering together multiple income sources — dividend payers, bond coupons, preferreds that sit between stocks and bonds in the capital structure, and sometimes tactical income strategies — the fund seeks to construct a more resilient stream of cash distributions while managing volatility.

The fund's holdings typically include large-cap companies known for consistent, long-lived dividend policies (utilities, [real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/), energy, financial services), investment-grade corporate and government bonds that are less volatile than equities, and sometimes preferred shares, which carry high [coupon rates](/coupon-rate/) and fixed payments but no [voting rights](/voting-rights/) and less upside than [common stock](/common-stock/). The exact mix varies with [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and the fund manager's outlook.

This [diversification](/diversification/) of income sources is the selling point. When equity [dividend yields](/dividend-yield/) fall (as they do when stock prices rise), bond income may be stepping in. When bonds underperform due to rising rates, dividend-heavy equities may compensate. No single downturn in one asset class obliterates the entire income stream.

## How the distributions work

HFSI pays out income regularly — typically monthly — and those distributions come from several places: the dividends collected from stocks, the [coupon payments](/coupon-payment/) collected from bonds, the interest on floating-rate notes, and sometimes realized capital gains from selling holdings at a profit. Some of that income is "[qualified dividend](/qualified-dividend/) income," which receives preferential tax treatment in non-retirement accounts; other parts are ordinary income or short-term capital gains, which are taxed at higher rates. The fund documents which portion is which in its annual tax reporting.

An important reality: the fund does not segregate the sources of income the way a bond fund or dividend-equity fund might. A high distribution rate does not mean the underlying investments are yielding that much in aggregate. The fund may be employing leverage (borrowing to increase holdings), harvesting capital gains by selling appreciated positions and reinvesting, or returning some of the investor's own capital in order to achieve a target payout. The prospectus details whether and how much leverage, if any, is used.

## Income investors and distribution traps

HFSI appeals to retirees and income-focused investors who rely on regular cash from their portfolios. The monthly distributions, seen in a bank account rather than reinvested, create a tangible sense of income. For those who need the cash, that is genuine utility.

But there is a classic income-investing trap: chasing yield can mean taking on hidden risks. A fund paying a very high distribution might achieve that payout through heavy use of leverage (borrowed money amplifies income but also amplifies losses), concentrated positions in high-yielding but volatile sectors, or unsustainable harvesting of capital gains that will not repeat. HFSI avoids the most reckless structures, but any income-focused fund warrants careful reading of the prospectus to understand the actual risks underneath the attractive payout number.

## Risks and cost

The most straightforward risk is that the underlying assets — particularly the stocks and any leverage — can fall in value. If equity markets decline sharply, the bond and preferred components may not absorb the full loss, and the fund's share price will reflect the decline. A retiree relying on the distributions also depends on the fund's assets remaining large enough to sustain the payout; extended underperformance or outflows can force the manager to shrink the distribution.

[Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) affects both the bond and preferred-share components. Rising rates depress the prices of existing fixed-income securities, so a sudden rate spike can translate into a loss if an investor must sell before holding to maturity. Conversely, falling rates lift bond and preferred valuations.

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is moderate — higher than a plain stock or bond [index fund](/index-fund/), lower than actively managed [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) that charge 1% or more. That extra cost reflects the active management required to source multiple income streams and rebalance the multi-asset allocation.

## How to research HFSI

Begin with the fund's prospectus and its most recent fact sheet, which detail the exact [asset allocation](/asset-allocation/) (what percentage in equities, bonds, preferreds, and alternatives) and the current distribution rate. Compare the historical yield — the annual payout divided by the share price — over several years to see whether the distribution is stable or volatile. Check whether the payout is sustainable; if distributions consistently exceed the net income and capital gains earned by the underlying holdings, the fund is eroding its assets, a habit that cannot last indefinitely.

Look at the total return — capital appreciation or [depreciation](/depreciation/) plus reinvested distributions — over one, three, and five-year periods against similar multi-asset [income funds](/income-fund/) and against a simple 60/40 stock-and-bond benchmark. A fund that delivers a higher yield but lower total return is often just front-loading income at the expense of future capital. Finally, review the fund's holdings and the issuer's commentary on how the multi-asset mix is currently tilted given interest rates, economic conditions, and dividend trends.
