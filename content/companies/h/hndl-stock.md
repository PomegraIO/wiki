*The **Strategy Shares [Nasdaq](/nasdaq/) 7HANDL Index ETF** (HNDL) is an exchange-traded fund tracking the Nasdaq 7HANDL Index, a proprietary selection and weighting methodology that identifies large-cap US stocks offering high [dividend yields](/dividend-yield/) alongside sound valuation and financial quality, then reweights the portfolio monthly across seven ranking tiers. It is designed for investors seeking regular [dividend](/dividend/) income from a diversified but concentrated portfolio of mature, cash-generative US companies.*

## The yield pillar: starting with cash return

HNDL's selection process is built on three interconnected screens. The first filters for dividend yield: the fund targets large-cap stocks paying dividends well above the market average, eliminating non-dividend payers and those offering minimal distributions. This initial screen assumes that mature, profitable companies returning cash to shareholders are more likely to do so sustainably than high-growth firms plowing earnings back into expansion. Yield is not the only measure of safety — far from it — but it is the entry gate. A company paying nothing to shareholders is excluded before the next screens apply.

## The valuation pillar: avoiding yield traps

A high yield can be a warning sign as much as an opportunity. The second screen applies valuation discipline using metrics such as price-to-earnings, price-to-book, and enterprise-value-to-cash-flow ratios. The goal is to exclude stocks that appear cheap only in isolation; they are expensive on deeper inspection, or the market has priced in a high probability of trouble. A stock yielding 7% but trading at 30 times forward earnings is a different beast from one yielding 6% at 12 times earnings. The valuation screen aims to identify opportunities where yield and reasonable price align — genuine value, not a value trap dressed up in a high-dividend wrapper.

## The quality pillar: sustainability of dividends

The third input examines financial quality. The algorithm assesses earnings consistency, profitability trends, debt levels, cash flow generation, and balance-sheet strength. A company with erratic earnings or climbing leverage may yield well today but could cut its dividend during the next downturn. The quality pillar aims to isolate companies with the financial durability to maintain or grow dividends in normal and stressful environments. It filters out balance-sheet disasters that happen to yield well because the market suspects trouble ahead.

## Monthly tier reweighting and turnover mechanics

Unlike a static index, HNDL rebalances each month. The methodology ranks all eligible holdings on a composite score reflecting yield, valuation, and quality. The portfolio is then divided into seven tiers, each tier receiving a predetermined weight target. Stocks climbing in the score move to higher-weighted tiers; those declining move to lower-weighted tiers. This monthly refresh prevents the portfolio from aging and adapts holdings to changing fundamentals without requiring ad hoc stock-picking decisions.

The monthly cycle creates inherent turnover — positions are shifted each month as ranks change — but the systematic, rules-based approach limits idiosyncratic trading. An investor holding HNDL should expect modest but consistent rebalancing activity, which generates trading costs and potential short-term [capital gains distributions](/capital-gains-distribution/).

## Holdings composition and sector concentration

With approximately 40 to 50 holdings, HNDL is more concentrated than a total-market or broad dividend index but diversified enough to weather an individual holding's misfortune. The portfolio typically overweights utilities, energy, consumer staples, and telecommunications — sectors where dividend payments are structural and culturally important. Technology, healthcare, and other high-growth sectors are underweighted because they reinvest earnings rather than distribute dividends.

This sector tilt creates a meaningful performance dependency: when dividend-focused sectors outperform (often in low-interest-rate environments or periods of economic slowdown), HNDL outperforms; when growth dominates or rates rise sharply, it lags. The fund is not a neutral bet on the overall market; it is a thematic play on income-paying businesses.

## Interest rates and cyclical sensitivity

HNDL's returns are sensitive to interest-rate environments in two ways. First, as [bond](/bond/) yields rise, the relative appeal of dividend stocks declines — capital shifts to fixed income, and dividend-stock valuations compress. Second, rising rates pressure highly leveraged companies, which may cut dividends to preserve cash or service debt. Conversely, in low-rate or declining-rate environments, investors reach for yield, dividend stocks outperform, and HNDL tends to rally.

An investor should understand that HNDL is not a stable income vehicle independent of market conditions; it is a dividend-focused equity bet exposed to interest-rate and credit-cycle dynamics.

## Costs, distributions, and research approach

HNDL charges an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) covering the monthly rebalancing and index maintenance. Distributions are paid monthly (a mix of dividends collected from holdings and any rebalancing gains), providing regular cash flow. Prospective investors should examine the fund's prospectus to understand the exact scoring methodology, any minimum market-cap threshold for inclusion, and the weighting distribution across the seven tiers.

Comparing HNDL's trailing yield and total returns to simpler [dividend-focused ETFs](/dividend-focused-etf/) clarifies what premium (or discount) the proprietary quantitative methodology delivers. Reading the fund's monthly fact sheet reveals the current composition, sector exposure, and any meaningful changes to the holdings roster. Because the methodology is rules-based and documented, an informed investor can predict approximately how the portfolio will shift if market conditions or valuations change materially.
