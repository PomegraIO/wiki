*The **[State Street](/stt-stock/) Blackstone High [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (HYBL) is an exchange-traded fund that gives retail investors direct exposure to high-yield [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/)—the debt of companies with weaker [credit ratings](/credit-rating/), which compensate bondholders with higher interest payments in return for greater default risk. It bundles hundreds of these [bonds](/bond/) into a single holding, spreading that risk across issuers and industries.*

## What does this fund actually hold?

HYBL's portfolio consists of below-investment-grade corporate bonds—typically rated BB and lower by the major credit agencies. These are borrowings from companies across all sectors: energy, healthcare, technology, retail, industrials, and consumer staples. The fund avoids emerging-market debt, government bonds, and financial institutions, keeping its focus tightly on the non-financial corporate space. Bond maturities range from near-term to ten-plus years, creating a kind of credit barbell where longer-duration debt offers more yield but sits on more duration risk.

[High-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) have a particular appeal to yield-hungry portfolios: they carry coupons (annual interest payments) typically between 6% and 8% in stable credit environments, or much higher when market conditions tighten. That extra income comes because the issuer is viewed as more likely to stumble or default than a blue-chip company. A fund like HYBL aims to make that trade legible and accessible, holding enough names that the failure of any single company dents the portfolio but does not break it.

## Why the expense ratio matters here

HYBL trades with a modest [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), which is essential for an income-focused fund—every basis point of cost directly reduces the yield flowing through to the holder. Because high-yield bonds themselves carry wider [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) (the difference in yield between corporate and safe debt), the fund's gross yield is already rich, but [management fees](/management-fee/) eat into that return. Lower costs mean more of the actual bond coupon stays in the investor's pocket rather than going to State Street.

## The real risk: credit deterioration and economic downturn

High-yield bonds are credit instruments, which means their value hinges on the financial health of the companies that issued them. In periods of economic stress—[recessions](/recession/), sharp rate hikes, specific industry crises—weaker credits fail at higher rates, and surviving bonds lose value as investors reprice the default risk. A portfolio holding hundreds of these bonds will experience material losses in such environments. HYBL's [diversification](/diversification/) helps, but it does not eliminate that [systemic risk](/systemic-risk/): in 2008, high-yield spreads blew out and many funds in this space fell 20–40%.

More subtly, rising [interest rates](/interest-rate/) hurt all bonds equally by depressing their market price (existing bond prices fall when newer bonds yield more), and high-yield bonds feel that pain sharply because they already trade on [credit risk](/credit-risk/). Holders of HYBL who bought during a period of low rates may find themselves underwater if rates rise and credit conditions tighten at the same time.

## Ownership concentration and liquidity

Most of HYBL's holdings are liquid—traded regularly in the secondary bond market—but the fund itself is more liquid than owning individual bonds. Buying the ETF takes seconds; buying fifty individual high-yield bonds requires a dealer and expertise. That liquidity premium is part of why people buy the fund rather than assembling a custom portfolio, but it comes with a caveat: the underlying bond market can freeze during stress. In such moments, the ETF's [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) can widen sharply, even if the underlying bonds still trade.

## How to research HYBL

Start with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, which lay out the objective, the index or strategy it tracks, and the current composition. Look at the weighted average maturity and weighted average credit rating—these two numbers together tell you how much duration and credit risk you are taking. Check the year-to-date and trailing-twelve-month yield, always adjusted for the expense ratio.

Watch the credit spreads—the difference in yield between high-yield bonds and risk-free government debt. When spreads tighten, the market is being greedy, and existing bonds appreciate. When spreads blow out, bonds are being punished, and new money flows elsewhere. HYBL will reflect those moves. Finally, monitor any commentary on corporate defaults in the high-yield universe and the health of the largest issuers in the fund's top holdings. A shift toward stressed names signals deteriorating credit conditions ahead.
