*[**HOME BANCORP, INC.** (HBCP)](https://www.sec.gov/cgi-bin/browse-edgar?action=getcompany&CIK=1436425) is a regional bank headquartered in Louisiana and traded on the [NASDAQ](https://www.sec.gov/cgi-bin/browse-edgar?action=getcompany&CIK=1436425&type=10-K), filing with the SEC under CIK 1436425. The bank earns its principal dollars from the classic retail banking model: borrowing deposits at low or zero cost and lending them at higher rates through residential mortgages, home equity lines of credit, and commercial real estate loans.*

The core economics of HOME BANCORP rests on the net interest margin — the spread between what the bank pays depositors and what it collects from borrowers. In residential lending, the business model resembles that of any regional mortgage originator: the bank issues loans (often conforming mortgages insurable by government-sponsored enterprises), holds some in portfolio, and sells others into the secondary market, capturing origination and servicing fees. The servicing revenue stream is essential to HBCP's model; every month that a borrower makes a payment, the bank collects a small percent of that payment for administrative work, generating recurring, relatively low-margin income that persists even if the mortgage itself has been sold.

Commercial real estate lending operates under a different unit economics. The bank makes larger, customized loans to local businesses and real estate investors, often retaining them in portfolio rather than selling. These loans carry higher yields (and higher default risk) than residential mortgages, but the bank's exposure is geographically concentrated — most loans are made to businesses in Louisiana, where economic performance and regional real estate values directly affect portfolio health. The primary lever for HBCP's profitability is thus threefold: (1) the spread between its cost of deposits and its yield on loans; (2) the volume of deposits it can attract and the stability of those deposits; and (3) credit quality — loan losses erode the margin and reduce capital available for lending.

HOME BANCORP's deposit franchise is its liability side of the business model. The bank funds its lending through customer deposits (checking, savings, money market accounts) and occasional wholesale funding. Deposits are the cheapest source of capital when rates are low, but they are also sticky only to the extent that customers have few better alternatives. In a rising-rate environment, depositors move to higher-yield accounts or competitors, forcing HBCP to raise deposit rates and eroding its net interest margin. The bank's cost of deposits thus rises and falls with the Federal Funds Rate, making profitability cyclical.

A material piece of HBCP's revenue model is mortgage servicing rights (MSRs). When the bank sells mortgages to investors, it often retains the right to collect monthly payments, handle escrow accounts, and manage defaults. This servicing revenue is valuable and recurring, but it also exposes HBCP to prepayment risk: if borrowers refinance (which they do aggressively when rates fall), the bank loses servicing income. Prepayments thus create a headwind to revenue visibility.

The loan-to-deposit ratio is a key metric for understanding HBCP's business model. If the bank holds fewer loans than deposits, it must either make more loans, buy securities, or hold excess capital. If it holds more loans than deposits, it relies on wholesale funding or equity, which are more expensive. The bank's management of this ratio, along with its mix of loan types and deposit stability, determines whether it can grow profitably or must shrink.

Capital management also shapes the business model. As a [bank](https://www.sec.gov/cgi-bin/browse-edgar?action=getcompany&CIK=1436425), HBCP must maintain regulatory capital ratios set by federal banking agencies. These ratios limit how much the bank can lend and grow relative to its equity base. If the bank retains earnings and builds capital, it can grow loans and deposits; if it returns capital to shareholders via [dividends](/dividend/) or [share buybacks](/share-buyback/), it constrains its growth capacity. This tension between growth and [shareholder returns](/return-on-equity/) is central to how HBCP allocates its earnings.

Operating leverage is another dimension of the business model. A regional bank's fixed costs — branch network, technology, compliance staff, regulatory expenses — do not scale linearly with loan growth. A bank with $5 billion in assets may have similar compliance costs as one with $8 billion. HBCP's ability to increase loan volume and asset size without proportional increases in operating costs improves [profitability](/operating-margin/) per dollar of assets. This is why regional banks pursue growth through organic loan origination and, often, acquisitions of smaller competitors.

The fee-based revenue (beyond net interest income) matters to HBCP's model as well. The bank collects fees for account maintenance, wire transfers, overdrafts, ATM usage, and other services. These fees are not interest-dependent and thus provide some hedge against margin compression. However, fee income is modest relative to net interest income at most regional banks, typically 25–30% of total revenue.

Losses on loans (provision for credit losses) are the wild card in HBCP's business model. In a stable or growing economy, loan losses are low and reserves can be released, boosting net income. In a recession or when regional economic conditions deteriorate, losses rise, and the bank must increase reserves, reducing net income. HOME BANCORP's Louisiana focus means its credit performance is tethered to oil and gas cycles, maritime industries, and regional real estate prices — all inherently volatile inputs.
