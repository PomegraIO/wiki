[John Hancock Preferred Income](/hpi-stock/) Fund II is a closed-end management investment company whose primary objective is to generate high current income by investing in [preferred stocks](/preferred-stock/) and other fixed-income securities, together with an allocation to common equities. Unlike an open-ended [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/), which can issue unlimited shares redeemed at [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), a closed-end fund issues a fixed number of shares that trade on an exchange at whatever price buyers and sellers agree to—often at a discount or premium to the fund's underlying asset value. The fund's shares (NYSE: HPF) attract investors who seek monthly distributions and are willing to trade in the [secondary market](/secondary-market/) rather than buy or redeem at exact net asset value.

## The income engine: preferred stocks and hybrid securities

Preferred stocks occupy a middle tier in a company's capital structure, sitting between [bonds](/bond/) and common equity. They typically pay a fixed [dividend](/dividend/) that takes priority over common-stock dividends but comes after all debt obligations. The fund's core holdings blend preferreds issued by financial institutions—particularly banks and insurance companies—with [collateralized debt obligations](/collateralized-debt-obligation/), loan participations, and other securitized instruments. This portfolio construction aims to capture yields well above those available from investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) or government securities, a premium that compensates investors for the risks of holding subordinated securities.

The fund employs leverage—borrowing money to amplify the amount of securities it can purchase—a tool available to [closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/) under regulatory limits set by the [Investment Company Act of 1940](/investment-company-act-of-1940/). By purchasing more assets than its equity capital allows, the fund increases both the size and the duration of its yield stream. When borrowed funds cost less than the yield on purchased securities, leverage enhances returns to shareholders; when conditions reverse, leverage cuts both ways. This makes leverage a permanent feature of the fund's structure and a material risk that should be understood before investing.

## Trading dynamics and the closed-end discount

A critical feature of any closed-end fund is the relationship between its stock price and its net asset value. The net asset value (NAV) is the sum of all assets minus all liabilities, divided by the number of shares outstanding—a figure calculated daily by the [fund administrator](/fund-administrator/). The market price, by contrast, is set by supply and demand on the exchange. When demand for income exceeds supply of the fund's shares, the stock trades above NAV; when investors flee income products or become pessimistic about preferred-stock risk, the fund trades at a discount to NAV, sometimes a substantial one.

This pricing gap is both an opportunity and a hazard. An investor who buys at a discount to NAV receives an implicit built-in gain if the discount narrows, but faces a loss if it widens further. The discount waxes and wanes with market sentiment, credit conditions, and interest-rate expectations. A sustained widening of the discount—particularly if it reflects genuine deterioration in the quality of holdings—signals that the market has repriced the fund's risk and that shareholders may be locking in losses if they bought near par.

## Regulatory constraints and operational reality

The fund operates under the framework of the Investment Company Act of 1940, which governs all mutual funds and closed-end funds traded in the United States. The act imposes limits on leverage, mandates daily disclosure of holdings and NAV, and requires [annual reports](/10-k/) and [proxy statements](/proxy-statement/) that detail [management fees](/management-fee/), performance, and portfolio composition. The fund's investment adviser—historically the John Hancock arm of [Manulife Financial](/mfc-stock/)—makes allocation decisions within stated objectives and is compensated through management fees that are calculated as a percentage of assets under management.

The monthly distribution is a core feature of the fund's appeal and design. The fund's manager aims to deliver steady, predictable payouts to shareholders, a habit that tends to attract retirees and income-seeking investors. However, distributions are not guaranteed, and in periods of portfolio stress or market volatility, the fund may reduce them. Importantly, distributions are not all income: in years when the portfolio's yield falls short of the stated distribution rate, the fund may return a portion of shareholders' original capital as a "return of capital," a practice that is legal and disclosed but that slowly erodes the underlying asset base if sustained.

## Risks embedded in the structure

Preferred stocks, while senior to common equity, still carry [credit risk](/credit-risk/). If an issuing bank or insurer faces severe distress, preferred dividends can be suspended or eliminated entirely. The financial crisis of 2008–2009 illustrated this vividly: preferred-stock funds suffered severe mark-to-market losses and some cut distributions sharply as issuers reduced their payout commitments. A repeat of such a [credit event](/credit-event-sovereign/) would hit the fund's share price and distributions together.

[Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) is equally material. Preferred stocks and most fixed-income securities fall in value when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise, because their fixed coupons become less attractive relative to newly issued securities offering higher yields. A fund bought at a discount may suffer further price declines in a rising-rate environment, amplifying losses for shareholders who need to exit quickly.

## How to research the fund

Prospective investors should begin with the fund's annual report and fact sheet, which disclose the current distribution rate, the composition of holdings, and the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) (management fees plus other costs). The SEC's EDGAR system carries the fund's filings under CIK 0001189740. Compare the fund's current market price to its disclosed NAV to gauge how far it trades from fundamentals. Watch the trend in distributions—a sustained reduction signals either improving portfolio quality or deteriorating fundamentals. Finally, monitor the fund's ability to maintain leverage within regulatory limits and the interest rates it is paying on its borrowings; rising funding costs can compress the spread that leverage is meant to exploit.
