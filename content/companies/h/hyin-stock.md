## The private credit bet

Public corporate-bond markets have become so crowded with [index funds](/index-fund/), passive investors, and systematic traders that yields have compressed. A [mortgage-backed security](/mortgage-backed-security/) or investment-grade [corporate bond](/corporate-bond/) held in a typical fund reflects the consensus price of millions of observers watching screens all day. Private credit — direct loans to middle-market and lower-middle-market companies, made outside public bond markets — operates in a different ecosystem. Fewer buyers, less transparency, no continuous [price discovery](/price-discovery/), and real friction in selling early create a persistent spread between the yield on private debt and the yield on comparable public bonds. HYIN aims to capture that spread.

The fund holds several asset classes within this umbrella. Direct lending to non-public companies, where lenders know the borrower or the industry well and accept illiquidity in return for yield. Structured-credit products — [collateralized loan obligations](/collateralized-loan-obligation/), [securitizations](/securitization/) of underlying loans, and other repackaged debt with internal waterfalls and seniority layers. Managed-account strategies and closed-end-fund positions that themselves hold private credit. The common thread is that none of these assets trade on a public exchange, and all command yields elevated above the public-bond market's equivalent risks would offer.

## Why public investors should care

The appeal is straightforward: higher yield. For a pension fund or retail investor restricted to liquid, daily-trading securities, an ETF like HYIN offers daily liquidity (you can sell your shares tomorrow) while holding underlying assets (private loans) that would otherwise be locked up for years. The fund's sponsors manage the hard work of sourcing deals, underwriting credit, and eventually harvesting returns from private-market exits. In return, the fund holder accepts a higher [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) than a vanilla bond fund, because the work of sourcing and managing non-public debt is expensive.

The risk is the mismatch between the fund's liquidity and the underlying assets' illiquidity. HYIN trades every day on an exchange; you can sell your shares any morning. But if the fund needs to raise cash to meet redemptions and the underlying private loans cannot be sold, the fund must either borrow money (incurring costs) or hold cash (dragging returns). In a market panic, the spread between what the fund can sell the private debt for and what it could sell for in normal times widens, potentially forcing losses on continuing holders if a large redemption occurs. This is a [tail risk](/tail-risk/), not a daily occurrence, but it explains why private-credit ETFs carry higher fees than their public-bond counterparts.

## The income trade-off and credit quality

Private loans can be senior secured (backed by collateral, paid first) or subordinated (paid after senior lenders). [Structured products](/structured-product/) sit somewhere in the middle — their priority depends on the specific waterfall. Credit quality varies widely. Some private loans go to stable, profitable mid-market companies with clean [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/); others finance [leveraged buyouts](/leveraged-buyout/) or more distressed situations. HYIN does not disclose its full portfolio's composition in the way a public-bond fund must in daily filings, so tracking the exact [credit risks](/credit-risk/) requires reading the fund's periodic fact sheets and prospectus.

Returns in private credit are sensitive to the availability of capital. When credit markets are tight and lenders are selective, borrowers who can access private loans are strong, and defaults are rare. When capital floods in and lenders compete aggressively on terms, lower-quality credits get funded, and defaults may rise later. The cycle is real, and HYIN is vulnerable to it.

## How to research private-credit funds

Start with the fund's prospectus, which lays out the types of assets it can hold and the underwriting criteria. Ask specifically about average portfolio [credit ratings](/credit-rating/), typical position sizes, expected maturity profile, and the percentage of assets that are truly illiquid versus those held in more liquid structures. Compare HYIN's yield to the yield on liquid [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) and ask whether the gap justifies the illiquidity and the higher expense ratio.

Watch for changes in the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) relative to its trading price. In normal times, an ETF's share price stays close to the value of its underlying holdings (the NAV). Large and persistent gaps suggest the market is uncertain about the underlying assets' true value or that redemptions are being restricted. Read the prospectus on how the fund values its illiquid holdings (typically monthly, not daily), as this can mask sudden price changes that only surface with a lag. And understand the fund's borrowing policies: if it can borrow to smooth redemptions, that flexibility comes with interest expense that lowers returns.
