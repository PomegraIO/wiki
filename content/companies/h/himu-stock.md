*HIMU is a **[municipal bond](/municipal-bond/) ETF** operated by BlackRock's iShares division. It holds U.S. [tax-exempt bonds](/tax-exempt-bond/) issued by states, cities, and local authorities—debt used to finance schools, roads, water systems, and other public infrastructure. Municipal [bond](/bond/) interest is exempt from federal income tax and, in some cases, state and local taxes, making it attractive to high-earning investors.*

## The muni bond market and its appeal

Municipal bonds are debt issued by state and local governments. When a city borrows to build a new public library, or a state finances a highway project, it may issue munis. The distinguishing feature is tax treatment: unlike Treasury and [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), muni interest is exempt from federal income tax. For investors in the highest tax brackets—those paying 35–37% federal tax rates—the after-tax yield on a muni is often higher than a taxable bond with the same stated coupon.

The math is straightforward. A municipal bond paying 5% interest (tax-free) is worth more to a high-bracket investor than a corporate bond paying 6% (fully taxable), because the investor keeps all 5% of the muni coupon but only around 4% of the corporate coupon after taxes. For lower-income investors, this math reverses—a taxable bond is better. HIMU is designed for the former group.

## High yield in the muni context

HIMU concentrates on "high yield" municipal bonds—a category that signals higher risk than investment-grade (the safest) munis. These are bonds issued by issuers with stretched budgets, uncertain revenue streams, or lower [credit ratings](/credit-rating/). Examples include bonds from struggling cities, underfunded [pension obligations](/pension-obligation/), or facilities with uncertain demand.

Higher yield means higher risk of default. Muni defaults are rare (far rarer than corporate defaults) because they involve the taxing power of a government, which is durable. But they happen. Detroit's municipal debt was restructured in 2014 after years of decline. Puerto Rico defaulted on its debt in 2016. Even sound issuers sometimes miss payments during fiscal crises. HIMU's higher yields reflect this risk.

## Active management in the muni space

Unlike a passive muni ETF that might hold every bond in a broad index, HIMU employs a team of credit analysts to research individual municipal issuers. The managers assess credit quality, revenue stability, and political factors. They aim to pick bonds trading below [fair value](/fair-value/) and avoid the weakest credits—munis about to experience payment trouble.

This active approach is more common in the muni market than in, say, the [stock market](/stock-market/). The muni market is less transparent and less efficiently priced than equities; there are many small local issuers with limited research coverage, creating genuine opportunities for skilled managers to add value by avoiding bad credits and finding overlooked value.

HIMU's manager is [BlackRock](/blk-stock/), a giant asset management firm. The depth of their muni research team and credit database is a material advantage. Smaller, less-resourced managers might hold the same weaker credits because they lack the analytical depth to identify risks.

## Sector composition and concentration

HIMU's portfolio typically includes bonds from states and cities, special-purpose entities (hospital districts, water authorities, utilities), and [revenue bonds](/revenue-bond/) backed by specific income streams rather than general tax revenues. The fund often overweights sectors with higher yields and growth, such as bonds financing hospital systems, higher-education facilities, and certain transportation infrastructure.

This sector concentration creates risk. If hospital bond credit quality deteriorates (due to reimbursement pressures or competition), or if a major university faces enrollment decline, HIMU can see widespread losses in its holdings. [Diversification](/diversification/) across many issuers helps, but sector-wide trends can override individual credit quality.

## Interest-rate and credit cycles

HIMU's price fluctuates with two drivers: [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and municipal credit conditions. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises rates, newly issued bonds offer higher yields, making existing lower-yielding bonds less attractive and pushing their prices down. HIMU's share price falls. Conversely, rate cuts lift bond prices.

Credit conditions also matter. In a strong economy, muni [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) tighten (investors demand less extra yield for holding munis), and prices rise. In a [recession](/recession/), muni credit spreads widen as investors worry about tax revenues and defaults. HIMU can see sharp declines even if interest rates fall, if credit conditions deteriorate.

## Tax efficiency and account placement

HIMU's main advantage is tax efficiency for taxable accounts. Muni interest is tax-free, so investors in high brackets capture most of the distribution. But capital gains—if the fund sells bonds at a profit, or if share prices rise and are redeemed—are not tax-free and should be taxed normally.

For a high-earner in a high-tax state (California, New York, etc.), holding HIMU in a taxable account can make sense. For someone in a low tax bracket or with low income, HIMU is suboptimal because the tax-free yield advantage vanishes. And for tax-deferred accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s), HIMU is inefficient—you pay the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) to get tax-free income that is already sheltered from taxes by the account structure.

## Risks specific to HIMU

Default risk is the headline concern. Munis do default, and HIMU's high-yield focus concentrates on the bonds most likely to struggle. A severe recession could trigger multiple defaults. Recent economic stress (the pandemic, state/local fiscal pressures) has kept muni credit quality on investors' minds.

[Liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/) is underestimated. Individual muni bonds are often illiquid; if you need to sell a bond quickly, the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) can be wide. An ETF like HIMU pools munis into a liquid wrapper, but if the broader muni market freezes (as it can in crises), trading spreads can widen sharply and share prices might diverge from [net asset value](/net-asset-value/).

[Concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) from sector tilts can amplify losses. If the fund is overweighting hospital bonds and hospital credit quality deteriorates, losses compound.

Finally, there is the reinvestment-rate risk: if interest rates fall and the fund receives coupons and maturities, it must reinvest at lower rates, reducing future income.

## Fit for the portfolio

HIMU is a specialized holding for a specialized investor: a high-earner (high federal tax bracket) in a high-tax state, with a long time horizon, and comfort with municipal [credit risk](/credit-risk/). For retirees with substantial income and smaller reinvestment needs, it can be a permanent core holding. For younger accumulators, it is often suboptimal—a traditional [bond ETF](/bond-etf/) or stock holdings will generate better long-term returns, and the tax efficiency of munis is less valuable without high income.

Sizing matters. HIMU should not be the entire fixed-income sleeve of a portfolio; concentration in one active manager's picks and one segment of the muni market (high-yield) introduces unnecessary risk. Pairing it with a traditional bond fund or some diversified munis makes sense.
