*HEFA is an exchange-traded fund that holds large and mid-cap stocks from developed markets — Europe, Australia, and the Far East — while using currency forwards to hedge away fluctuations in the dollar. The result is a fund that lets a US investor own a slice of the world's second-largest [stock market](/stock-market/) without having to bet on where the euro, pound, or yen will move.*

## What HEFA holds and what it is for

HEFA buys into the MSCI EAFE index, a stock-market benchmark that covers roughly 1,000 large and mid-cap companies across developed nations outside North America. That includes the major economies of Europe — Britain, Germany, France, Switzerland — as well as Australia, Japan, Singapore, and Hong Kong. The fund is cap-weighted, so it mirrors the index's concentration in a handful of the largest European and Japanese firms.

What makes HEFA different from a plain EAFE ETF is the currency hedge. When a US investor buys a British stock listed in pounds, she faces two separate returns: the stock's gain or loss in pounds, and the dollar's strength or weakness against the pound. A stock that rises 5 percent in local currency but the dollar strengthens 3 percent gives a net dollar return of about 2 percent. The hedge sidesteps that problem. HEFA uses currency [forward contracts](/forward-contract/) — agreements to sell foreign currencies at a fixed rate in the future — to lock in a known dollar return from the stocks, isolating the investor's bet to equity performance alone.

The hedge is rebalanced monthly, so the fund is not a perfect static hedge over time; as the dollar's value drifts, the fund rolls the forwards to realign. That monthly reset incurs small costs, reflected in the fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), which is slightly higher than an unhedged EAFE ETF. The trade is worth it for investors who believe foreign markets will deliver returns but want no exposure to currency swings — pension funds, liability-driven investors, and US savers who already have ample dollar exposure elsewhere in their portfolios often prefer this approach.

## The MSCI EAFE index and market coverage

The MSCI EAFE benchmark is one of the most widely tracked gauges of developed-market performance. Its coverage is broad but not exhaustive: the "E" stands for Europe, but it includes Australia ("AE" of EAFE) and the Far East ("FE"). It excludes Canada and the US, which have their own sub-indices within MSCI's world framework. The index includes roughly 40 percent European equities (weighted by market value), with the remainder split between Asia-Pacific (Japan, Singapore, Hong Kong, Australia) and smaller developed markets. Because it is a large-cap and mid-cap index, it skews toward household names: luxury goods, banking, pharmaceuticals, energy majors, and carmakers dominate the weightings.

HEFA tracks the net-dividend version of the index, meaning [dividends](/dividend/) paid by the underlying stocks are reinvested and factored into the return. That is the standard approach for total-return investors; the alternative (gross-dividend) version is rare in practice.

## How currency hedging works and what it costs

The hedge is mechanical: each month, HEFA enters into forward contracts to sell the local currencies of its holdings — euros, pounds, yen, and so on — and receive dollars at rates locked in today. As time passes and the forwards mature, the fund rolls them into new contracts at current market rates. This rolling creates a cost whenever the [forward rate](/forward-rate/) differs from the [spot rate](/spot-rate/) that arrived later, and the net effect over time depends on interest-rate differentials between the US and other countries. If US short-term rates are higher than those in Europe or Japan (which has been true in recent years), the forward rates tend to be lower, meaning the hedge becomes expensive — the fund pays away some of the foreign return to secure the dollar lock. If US rates are lower, the hedge is cheaper or even subsidized by the forward premium.

The real purpose of the hedge is volatility reduction and return predictability, not cost minimization. A US investor holding unhedged foreign stocks faces about 30 to 50 percent additional volatility from currency swings on top of the underlying equity volatility. The hedge filters out that currency noise, leaving the fund to track the stock market's moves alone. For investors who specifically want global [diversification](/diversification/) but have no view on foreign-exchange trends, or who believe foreign currencies will weaken relative to the dollar, the cost of hedging is a worthwhile insurance premium.

## Who holds HEFA and when it makes sense

HEFA appeals to institutional investors with long-dated foreign liabilities (pension funds, endowments) and to retail investors with strong home-country bias or a full allocation to unhedged foreign assets already elsewhere in their portfolios. It is also common among [target-date funds](/target-date-fund/) and balanced strategies that want developed-market exposure without [currency risk](/currency-risk/). Because the fund is moderate in trading volume — not a top-tier liquid vehicle like the broad US stock ETFs — large purchases or sales can incur meaningful [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/), so it is better suited to buy-and-hold strategies than active trading.

The choice between hedged and unhedged international exposure comes down to conviction on currency trends. Over very long periods, currency movements tend to follow purchasing-power parity — currencies of lower-inflation countries strengthen — but the path is volatile and unpredictable in the short and medium term. Most passive investors who cannot forecast currency moves choose hedged strategies to simplify their bet to "I want developed-market stocks, period," rather than accidentally taking a currency view they didn't intend.

## How to evaluate HEFA as part of a portfolio

Start with the MSCI EAFE index methodology — freely available on the MSCI website — to see exactly which companies, sectors, and countries the benchmark weights. HEFA tracks that index with very low tracking error, so the key is understanding whether EAFE exposure fits your investment goals, not whether HEFA executes it. Check the fund's prospectus and factsheet for the current expense ratio and any recent changes in the hedging methodology. Review the fund's year-to-date performance against both the unhedged MSCI EAFE (which many [brokers](/broker/) offer via different [BlackRock](/blk-stock/) ETFs) to see what [currency hedging](/currency-hedging/) cost in a rising-dollar environment versus a falling-dollar environment — that comparison over rolling multi-year periods shows the trade-off in action. Compare bid-ask spreads at your broker before trading, especially for large positions, because HEFA's moderate volume means the spread can be wider than top-tier alternatives.
