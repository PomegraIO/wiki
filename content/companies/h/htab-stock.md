The Hartford Schroders Tax-Aware [Bond ETF](/bond-etf/) is a [bond](/bond/) fund engineered to do something most funds ignore: count the tax bill. A typical bond fund reports returns before taxes; a tax-aware fund accounts for the fact that distributions are taxable events, and arranges its portfolio to minimize the damage. HTAB, issued by Hartford Funds and managed with Schroders, targets the investor in a high tax bracket — someone who holds a bond position in a taxable account, not a retirement account, and cares as much about the after-tax result as the pre-tax yield.

The distinction matters because bonds generate income, and income is taxed. A fund that distributes interest at year-end passes a tax bill to its holders. A tax-managed bond fund tries to reduce that bill through several levers: selecting bonds whose income is sheltered (like municipals or Treasury securities where possible), timing the realization of gains and losses to offset each other, and managing the pace and character of distributions so they carry a lighter tax load than a conventionally managed fund would produce.

HTAB takes a diversified approach to its holdings — a mix of investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), government bonds, and a tax-efficient slice of the [municipal bond](/municipal-bond/) market, the traditional tax-advantaged fixed-income instrument. The portfolio is not a simple index tracker; it requires active management to execute the tax-minimization strategy. The trade-off is that the fund carries [management fees](/management-fee/), but for a taxable investor holding a meaningful bond position, those fees can be justified if the tax efficiency generated over time exceeds the cost.

The fund's objective is not to produce the highest absolute return but the highest after-tax return, a cleaner metric for a taxable holder than the pre-tax yield alone. A bond returning 4 percent that distributes 4 percent in taxable income is less attractive than a bond returning 3.5 percent that distributes only 1 percent in current income, if the holder faces a marginal tax rate above roughly 30 percent. HTAB constructs its portfolio with that insight at the core.

The appeal is most acute for high-income individuals: corporate executives, professionals, and business owners in the highest federal and state tax brackets. For them, tax drag on bond holdings can be significant over years, and a fund that explicitly minimizes it addresses a real cost of holding bonds in taxable accounts. Investors in lower brackets or those using bonds in retirement accounts (where taxation is deferred or irrelevant) receive less benefit and may prefer lower-cost index bond funds.

Like all exchange-traded funds, HTAB trades on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) at prices set by the market, though the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) is updated daily. The liquidity of the fund — how easily and at what cost a holder can buy or sell shares — depends on the market's appetite for the fund itself, which for a Hartford product of this type is generally adequate but not spectacular compared to the largest bond funds.

Researching HTAB requires looking at two layers: the fund's strategy documents (the prospectus and fact sheet) and the underlying index or framework it tracks. The prospectus details the tax-minimization approach and the portfolio's construction rules. For anyone considering it, the critical questions are whether the fund's tax efficiency premium justifies its fees, and whether the fund's duration and credit quality match the investor's fixed-income objectives. Like any fund holding bonds, [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) and [credit risk](/credit-risk/) apply: if rates rise, bond prices fall; if credit conditions tighten, corporate bond holdings may suffer. A tax-efficient structure does not protect against those market moves.
