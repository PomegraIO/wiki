*[**HORIZON BANCORP INC /IN/** (HBNC)](https://www.sec.gov/cgi-bin/browse-edgar?action=getcompany&CIK=706129), trading on the NASDAQ and filing with the SEC under CIK 706129, is a community bank serving the Midwest with a concentration in Indiana. The bank's business model centers on gathering retail deposits from individuals and small businesses, then deploying those deposits into residential real estate loans, commercial real estate loans, and agricultural credit.*

The earnings engine of HBNC is the net interest margin — the percentage-point spread between the average rate the bank pays on deposits and the average rate it earns on loans. If HBNC pays 0.5% on deposits and earns 5% on loans, the margin is 4.5 percentage points. Multiplied by the bank's asset base, this margin generates net interest income, which is the largest component of HBNC's revenue. The margin is dynamic: it rises when the Federal Funds Rate is high (because the bank earns high rates on loans while deposit rates remain sticky), and it contracts when the Fed cuts rates (because loan yields fall faster than deposit costs).

HBNC's deposit franchise is geographically concentrated. The bank operates branches across Indiana and neighboring states, and the vast majority of its deposits come from individuals, families, and small businesses in those markets. Deposits include checking accounts (low-rate, high-turnover), savings accounts and money market accounts (rate-sensitive, can flee to competitors), and individual retirement accounts (stickier, less sensitive to rate movements). The bank's ability to grow and retain deposits depends on local brand recognition, branch convenience, and competitive deposit rates. In rural Indiana communities where HBNC has long-standing relationships, the bank can retain deposits at lower rates than national competitors; in more competitive urban areas, deposit rates must match or exceed those of larger regional and online banks.

Residential real estate lending is HBNC's bread and butter. The bank originates mortgages to local homebuyers, primarily conforming mortgages eligible for sale to government-sponsored enterprises (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, Ginnie Mae). HBNC often sells these mortgages in the [secondary market](/secondary-market/), capturing origination fees and yield spread (the gain on sale), but retains servicing rights. The bank thus earns origination revenue up-front and recurring servicing revenue over the loan's life. Residential lending is lower-risk than commercial lending — homes are tangible, relatively liquid collateral, and residential default rates are historically low (except during housing crises). However, mortgage origination is competitive; loan officers shop rates across multiple banks, and refinancing volumes are highly sensitive to interest rate movements.

Commercial real estate lending is the second major earning asset for HBNC. The bank makes loans to small and mid-sized businesses that need real estate financing — acquiring a warehouse, refinancing an office building, or expanding a manufacturing facility. These loans typically carry higher yields than residential mortgages (because business borrowers have higher credit risk and face more volatile cash flows) but also higher default risk. HBNC must underwrite each commercial loan carefully, assessing the borrower's creditworthiness, business stability, and the collateral value. Commercial loans are often retained in HBNC's portfolio, creating long-term earning assets but also concentrating credit risk.

Agricultural lending distinguishes HBNC's portfolio in the Midwest. The bank makes loans to farmers for land acquisition, equipment purchases, and working capital. Agricultural lending requires expertise in farm finances, crop economics, and commodity prices. A farmer's ability to repay depends on crop yields, commodity prices, and input costs — all volatile. HBNC mitigates this risk by holding liens on land and equipment and by staying closely involved in the borrower's operation, often requiring annual financial statements and crop insurance. Agricultural lending also creates seasonal dynamics: farmers draw down lines of credit during planting and growing season, then repay after harvest. HBNC must have sufficient deposit funding and working capital to support this seasonal swing.

Loan losses (credit losses) are the critical variable in HBNC's profitability. In good economic times, loan losses are low, and the bank can release portions of its loan-loss reserve, boosting net income. In recessions or when regional economic conditions deteriorate, losses rise. HBNC must increase its provision for credit losses, which reduces earnings. The Midwest economy is relatively stable and diversified (manufacturing, agriculture, services), so HBNC's credit losses are not as volatile as those of banks in oil-dependent or single-industry regions, but they still fluctuate with economic cycles.

HBNC's fee income (account maintenance, wire transfers, overdraft fees, safe deposit boxes) is modest relative to net interest income, typically 20–30% of total revenue. Fee income is less rate-sensitive than net interest income and thus provides some diversification. However, deposit fee income has come under pressure as customers shift to online banking (reducing overdrafts) and demand no-fee accounts.

Operating efficiency is a key profitability metric for HBNC. The efficiency ratio (non-interest expenses divided by total revenue) measures how much the bank must spend to generate each dollar of revenue. A high efficiency ratio indicates that the bank has high costs relative to its earning assets — branch count is too high, technology is outdated, or staffing is bloated. HBNC must manage its branch network and operating costs carefully; consolidating redundant branches and migrating customers to online banking lowers the efficiency ratio and improves returns on equity.

Regulatory capital requirements constrain HBNC's ability to grow. As a bank, HBNC must maintain Tier 1 capital and Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) ratios above federal minimums. These ratios prevent the bank from taking on excessive leverage relative to its equity base. If HBNC earns strong profits and retains earnings, capital grows, enabling more lending. If the bank pays out all earnings as [dividends](/dividend/) or repurchases [shares](/common-stock/), capital grows more slowly, constraining lending capacity. HBNC's management must balance shareholder distributions with the need to fund growth.

Acquisitions are a growth lever for HBNC. The bank can acquire smaller competitors, consolidating their deposits and loan portfolios into HBNC's balance sheet. Acquisitions are expensive (the acquirer must pay a premium to shareholders of the acquired bank) and disruptive (systems, brands, and cultures must be integrated). However, successful acquisitions can diversify HBNC's revenue, reduce operating costs per dollar of assets, and expand its geographic reach.

Interest rate risk exposure is material for HBNC. If the bank holds mortgages with fixed rates and deposit costs rise (because the Fed raises rates), the margin contracts and profitability falls. HBNC can mitigate this by matching the maturity of its assets and liabilities (holding short-term assets and liabilities) or by using derivatives to hedge interest rate risk. However, perfect hedging is costly.
