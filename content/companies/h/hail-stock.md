*The **SPDR S&P Kensho Smart Mobility ETF** (HAIL) is an exchange-traded fund that owns companies positioned at the intersection of transportation, autonomy, and electrification — firms that make self-driving systems, build electric powertrains, supply batteries, or manage the real-world logic that will underpin driverless fleets.*

## What the fund owns

HAIL tracks the S&P Kensho Smart Mobility Index, which screens global companies for exposure to the enabling technologies of autonomous and electric transportation. The index combines automated text-analysis algorithms (Kensho Technologies, acquired by [S&P Global](/spgi-stock/), supplies the scanning) with human review to identify firms whose business is meaningfully tied to self-driving vehicles, battery chemistry and manufacturing, vehicle-to-vehicle communication, wireless charging, ride-sharing platforms, fleet management software, or related infrastructure.

The resulting portfolio is not narrow or single-stock bet. It holds dozens of companies across geographies and [market caps](/market-capitalization/) — from household-name automakers making the transition to electric and autonomous ([Tesla](/tsla-stock/), traditional car manufacturers adding EV lines) to specialized suppliers (companies making lidar sensors, battery management systems, semiconductor components for vehicle control), software firms (mapping, localization, decision-making stacks for autonomous systems), infrastructure players (charging-network operators), and even some materials firms (lithium producers, rare-earth extractors needed for batteries and motors).

The fund is cap-weighted, meaning the largest eligible companies have the biggest influence on returns. It rebalances semi-annually or when triggered by index rules, with turnover lower than an active manager's but higher than a buy-and-hold strategy.

## Why someone would use this fund

A reader choosing HAIL is making a bet on the thesis that autonomous and electric vehicles will represent a substantial portion of global transportation over the next 10–20 years, and that the companies enabling that shift — not just vehicle-makers but the entire supply chain — will be profitable. Rather than trying to pick winners among lidar companies or battery chemists, HAIL offers exposure to a curated basket of players across the stack.

The thematic framing also appeals to investors who believe they have a view on a big structural trend but lack the expertise to build a custom portfolio. A single ETF wrapper simplifies that bet and spreads single-company risk across many firms. For investors in SSGA's ecosystem (those already holding SPDR products), it is a straightforward way to add mobility-sector exposure alongside other holdings.

## The concentration risk

[Thematic ETFs](/thematic-etf/) like HAIL have an embedded [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) that differs from a broad market index. Because the screening focuses on a specific industry thread — mobility technology — it is smaller and more correlated than, say, the S&P 500 or a total-market fund. Holdings cluster in a subset of sectors: automotive, semiconductor supplies, battery manufacturing, and software for autonomous systems. A single regulatory change affecting self-driving approval, or a major recall in the electric-vehicle space, or a slowdown in battery-material supply could ripple through most holdings at once.

Additionally, some of the companies in the index have limited track records in their core mobility business. Many traditional automakers are decades old but only recently committed capital to EV and autonomous fleets. Some smaller suppliers are newer, with uncertain path to profitability. Holding both the established and the speculative under one ticker means the fund's fortunes depend on both the viability of the underlying trend and the company-specific execution that follows.

## Costs and mechanics

As a passive [index fund](/index-fund/), HAIL carries an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) considerably lower than an actively managed thematic fund would. The exact expense ratio should be checked in the [fund prospectus](/fund-prospectus/), but SPDR ETFs of this type typically charge in the range of 0.45–0.75% annually — reasonable for active management but higher than a total-stock-market index fund. That cost is deducted from the fund's assets each year, shrinking the effective return to the investor.

HAIL trades on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) like any stock, with [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) depending on market conditions and trading volume. Most days it is liquid enough for retail and institutional investors to buy or sell blocks without excessive slippage, though traders should check the volume and spread before entering large positions.

The fund is dividend-paying when underlying holdings pay [dividends](/dividend/), though the yield is not high given the growth orientation of many holdings. Capital-gains distributions occur when the index rebalances or when underlying companies have significant price moves.

## How to research HAIL

Start with the prospectus and fact sheet on SSGA's website, which lay out the index methodology, the current holdings (usually top 10–20 positions), and the exact expense ratio. The prospectus explains the inclusion criteria and rebalancing schedule.

Next, examine the underlying index itself: what companies are in the top holdings? Are they household names (Tesla, major automakers) or largely unknown suppliers? Tracing through the top 10 holdings and reading recent news on each gives a feel for which part of the mobility story the fund is betting on most heavily. Some periods it may be overweight automakers; other periods, battery or chip suppliers.

Because smart mobility is a moving regulatory and technological target, watching industry news is essential: announcements about autonomous-vehicle approval timelines, major battery breakthroughs, electric-vehicle sales trends, and supply-chain health all affect the entire portfolio. Conversations on Tesla earnings calls and statements from traditional automakers' investor days often illuminate what the broader industry thinks is happening next.

Finally, HAIL is explicitly a thematic bet, not a diversified core holding. It belongs in a portfolio as a satellite position — a smaller, higher-conviction bet alongside broader index holdings — not as a core allocation unless the investor is convinced that mobility-technology exposure is central to their financial goals.
