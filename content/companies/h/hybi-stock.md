NEOS Enhanced Income Credit Select ETF (ticker HYBI) is an actively managed fixed-income fund that holds a portfolio of high-yield corporate bonds — debt issued by companies with below-investment-grade credit ratings, which offer higher yields to compensate investors for the elevated risk of default. The fund is managed by NEOS Advisors, a group focused on credit-selection strategies, and trades on NASDAQ as a daily-redeemable ETF that bundles active bond management with exchange-traded liquidity.

## The genesis of active credit selection

For decades, the high-yield bond market was the domain of passive indexing. Fund sponsors would build a vehicle holding every significant high-yield bond, weighted by market value, and investors would collect the spread — the difference between what junk-rated issuers paid and what Treasury bonds paid. The logic was simple: hold the index, accept that some bonds would default but the yield spread on the others would more than compensate, and let market mechanics do the rest. Cost was low; diversification was automatic; stock-picking did not apply to bonds, or so the conventional wisdom held.

NEOS emerged with a contrarian thesis: what if credit analysis — the same discipline that equity managers apply to stock selection — could improve returns in the high-yield market? Which bond issuers were showing early signs of financial deterioration? Which companies faced cyclical headwinds that would become acute in a downturn? Which bonds offered yields that did not truly compensate for hidden risk, and which traded at wider spreads than credit fundamentals justified? By answering these questions before committing capital, an active manager could theoretically avoid the worst performers, reduce default rates, and deliver higher risk-adjusted returns than the mechanical index approach.

## How HYBI applies credit selection

HYBI pursues active credit research and rigorous portfolio construction. The fund's team examines the financial statements, leverage ratios, business outlooks, and competitive positions of bond issuers, then makes explicit decisions about which bonds to own and in what quantities. A holding the managers are highly confident in might represent 3–4 percent of the portfolio; a smaller conviction receives 0.5–1 percent weight. This active weighting diverges sharply from passive indices, which mechanically weight by the amount of debt outstanding, treating a weak credit and a strong credit identically if their debt levels happen to match.

The "enhanced income" label reflects the aspiration: that careful credit selection — avoiding weak credits and identifying undervalued opportunities — can generate higher risk-adjusted returns than holding the passive index, net of fees. In practice, this depends entirely on whether the managers' credit decisions prove right.

## The competitive field

HYBI competes against a range of alternatives. Passive high-yield ETFs offer lower costs and transparent, mechanical weighting; an investor gets the market return at minimal expense. Other actively managed high-yield funds from larger sponsors like Vanguard and BlackRock compete on their own credit-selection track records. Newer total-return bond strategies combine credit selection with broader interest-rate positioning, betting not just on which bonds will do well but on how yields will move.

HYBI's singular pitch is that credit selection, disciplined and expert, is the leverage to outperformance — that a dedicated credit team will avoid the worst names and identify the best values before the market catches on. But this thesis only holds if the credit team actually outperforms, net of the higher fees active management requires.

## Structural risks and market evolution

The core risk is simple: credit selection, like equity stock-picking, may not work. If all high-yield bonds rise together in economic expansions, the fund's selectivity adds nothing and it merely collects returns with an expense-ratio drag. In sharp credit crises, bonds the managers selected may fare no better than the average, and concentrated positions may amplify losses. High-yield bonds are also highly sensitive to interest-rate movements; if yields rise sharply across the board, credit skill is overwhelmed by the market effect.

The high-yield market itself has evolved. Passive flows have grown, index providers have proliferated, and active managers face steadily compressed margins for outperformance. HYBI must now justify not just itself against other active funds but against the basic argument that a lower-cost passive high-yield ETF, tracking the broad market, is the sensible choice.

## Assessing HYBI

Begin by comparing HYBI's portfolio holdings directly against a passive high-yield index. Which bonds does HYBI hold that the index holds? Which does it overweight and which does it avoid? Review the fund's long-term performance relative to passive high-yield benchmarks over at least 5–10 years, and account fully for the expense-ratio difference; if HYBI's gross returns do not exceed the passive index by at least the fee gap, active management has not added value.

Examine the average credit rating of the portfolio, the duration (interest-rate sensitivity), and the sector weightings — this reveals where the manager is concentrating risk. Read quarterly fact sheets and commentary for the manager's outlook on credit conditions and the specific names driving positioning. This reveals whether the active thesis remains sound or whether the fund has drifted toward index-tracking passivity.
