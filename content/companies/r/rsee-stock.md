[Systematic investing](/systematic-investing/) means following a set of mathematical rules to pick stocks rather than relying on human judgment or discretion. RSEE runs a quantitative screen across the U.S. equity market—perhaps thousands of companies—and selects those that meet specific criteria: signs of business quality (strong return on capital, stable earnings), valuation cheapness (low price relative to earnings or book value), and momentum (stocks that have outperformed lately). The exact weightings and thresholds are part of the fund's strategy documentation.

The advantage of a rules-based approach is consistency and repeatability. A human analyst might fall in love with a story and overweight it; a system applies the same logic to every stock. There is no room for [recency bias](/recency-bias/) or a manager's pet sector. This does not mean systematic strategies always outperform—they can underperform for years if the factors they favor fall out of style—but it does mean their behavior is predictable and their methodology is transparent. You can see exactly what stocks they own and why.

Most systematic strategies combine multiple factors because any single factor—value, say, or momentum—goes through periods of working brilliantly and periods of working terribly. By blending quality, value, and momentum in a single screen, a fund hedges that no single factor will be disastrous if it reverts or fades. During periods when value is cheap and despised by the market, the value portion of the screen lights up. When momentum is dominant, the momentum portion carries the day. The multi-factor approach aims for steadier returns than any single-factor strategy would deliver, though it also caps outperformance versus whichever single factor is hot in a given year.

RSEE is actively managed, which means a human team oversees the portfolio and can make adjustments outside the strict systematic rules if needed—for example, handling a [merger](/merger/) or managing [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/). Pure factor funds are entirely rules-driven; active systematic funds retain some discretion. This human overlay can add value in edge cases but also introduces the possibility of human error.

The practical reality of systematic equity funds is that they tend to concentrate in certain sectors or styles. A screen emphasizing quality and value will have many holdings in banks, insurance, industrials, and energy—sectors trading cheaply—and fewer in expensive growth sectors like technology. Investors get not just a diversified equity portfolio but a tilted one. That tilt can be a feature (if you believe it is undervalued) or a bug (if it conflicts with your market view). Performance depends heavily on whether the chosen factors are in or out of favor.

Research RSEE by reviewing the prospectus for the exact screening criteria, the holdings list to see the sector and market-cap composition, and the performance history against comparable indices and against similar multi-factor funds. Compare the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) to passive index alternatives to understand the cost of the systematic approach. Look at how the fund performed in different market regimes—value booms, momentum crashes, quality squeezes—to see if the factor blend is one you can live with through a full cycle.
