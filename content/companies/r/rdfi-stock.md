The **Rareview Dynamic Fixed [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (RDFI) is an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) that does not track an index. Instead, a team of analysts uses quantitative models to decide which parts of the [bond](/bond/) market offer the best risk-adjusted returns at any given moment, then allocates the fund's assets accordingly. The fund might overweight [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) when they look cheap, shift into high-yield corporate debt when spreads appear attractive, increase duration during an economic slowdown, or reduce it when [recession](/recession/) is a distant threat. The whole portfolio is constantly reassessed.

Active management in fixed income has a mixed reputation. In equities, beating the market is genuinely hard and most active managers do not do it. In bonds, the story is different. The bond market is vast, less liquid than equities, full of mispricings, and dominated by institutional investors who have different objectives (insurance companies need to match liability schedules, banks manage funding costs, pensions target specific returns). A skilled analyst can sometimes find pockets where the market is wrong or where the risk premium does not compensate for the danger. RDFI is premised on that idea.

## What the fund can hold

Rareview Dynamic manages its holdings across a wide swath of the fixed-income landscape. Treasuries are always fair game — a foundation for the portfolio, they offer liquidity and credit safety. Investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) (BBB-rated and up) give higher yield in exchange for some [credit risk](/credit-risk/). [High-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) (BB-rated and lower) carry more credit risk but offer fatter coupons. The fund can also hold agency mortgage-backed securities (mortgages guaranteed by [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/) or [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/)), [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/), floating-rate instruments, and other fixed-income sectors. Allocation between these is not fixed. The fund might be 30% Treasuries and 50% corporate bonds one quarter, then shift to 60% Treasuries and 20% high-yield the next, based on how the models assess relative value.

The fund operates with a stated duration band — a range for how interest-rate-sensitive the portfolio should be. If long-term rates look poised to fall, the fund will push duration high (buy longer-dated bonds that gain more when rates drop). If rates look poised to rise, it will lower duration (favor shorter-dated bonds that lose less). This is a deliberate active call that distinguishes RDFI from a static-allocation bond fund.

## The premise: market mispricings and relative value

The wager underlying RDFI is that bond markets are not perfectly efficient. When the US Treasury curve is abnormally steep, the fund might see an opportunity to buy short-term Treasuries and sell long-term ones, betting the curve will flatten. When high-yield spreads blow out to 600 basis points above Treasuries (a sign of panic), the fund might see [junk bonds](/junk-bond/) as a bargain and overweight them. When the Fed is tightening and the market expects three rate hikes, the fund might bet there will be only two and position duration accordingly. These are educated guesses, not certainties, and they can be wrong. A recession can appear out of nowhere and send high-yield bonds down in a way no model predicted.

The models Rareview uses likely include measures like [current yield](/current-yield/), duration, [credit spreads](/credit-spread/), volatility, correlations, and forward expectations. They scan across sectors looking for asymmetries — places where the risk premium seems too high or too low. That constant scanning and rebalancing is what distinguishes an active manager from a passive, static-allocation fund.

## The cost structure and the active-management question

Because RDFI is actively managed, it carries a higher [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) than a simple broad-market [bond ETF](/bond-etf/). Passive bond funds might charge 0.05–0.20% per year. RDFI likely charges in the 0.50–0.80% range, depending on how much research and trading the strategy requires. Over a ten-year horizon, that 50–60 basis points of annual cost compounds to a significant drag. The fund only makes economic sense if the active managers' skill generates returns above that cost threshold — that is, if their market calls beat a passive alternative by more than 50–60 basis points per year on a risk-adjusted basis.

The track record becomes central to the evaluation. If RDFI has consistently outperformed a simple aggregate bond fund (like BND, which holds a static mix of Treasuries and investment-grade corporates) net of fees, then the active approach has added value. If it has underperformed or matched the passive index, then investors are simply paying for the activity without getting paid back.

## Risks and limitations

Active management in bonds can fail in several ways. First, the models can be wrong. A model built on fifty years of history does not necessarily anticipate unprecedented events — a pandemic, a geopolitical shock, a new regime in [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/). Second, the managers have conviction biases. If the team is consistently overweighting high-yield because they believe [credit cycles](/credit-cycle/) are tame, and then credit stress hits hard, the fund suffers more than a diversified passive strategy. Third, while bond markets have mispricings, exploiting them requires patience and capital — the spread the fund is betting on might take months to correct, and in the meantime the position is underwater and tempting to exit.

Finally, there is the tactical-allocation risk: guessing the right bond-market moves is hard. Overweighting duration before a rate rise is expensive. Underweighting Treasuries before a flight-to-safety rally costs returns. RDFI's performance will be driven as much by whether the macro calls are right as by the quality of security selection.

## How a reader would evaluate it

Start with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, which disclose the current allocation, the duration, the weighted-average yield, and the stated investment process. Then look at the track record: does RDFI outperform a passive broad-bond-market index, net of fees, over rolling three-, five-, and ten-year periods? Does it deliver lower volatility or smaller drawdowns during credit stress? Examine the portfolio composition — is it dramatically different from the benchmark, or just modestly tilted? Review commentary from the management team describing what they are seeing in the market and why they are overweighting one sector or another; good managers give you a coherent narrative, not guesses. Finally, understand the fees and whether the historical outperformance is enough to justify them.
