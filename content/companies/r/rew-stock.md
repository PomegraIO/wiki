*The **ProShares UltraShort Technology** (REW) is a leveraged [inverse ETF](/inverse-etf/) designed to move approximately negative two times the daily return of the Nasdaq-100 technology index — using derivatives to amplify a short bet against large-cap tech stocks, with the explicit caveat that it resets daily and is not meant to track the index's performance over periods longer than a single day.*

REW is not a stock fund and does not hold shares of technology companies. Instead, it is a derivatives strategy wrapped in an ETF wrapper. ProShares, the sponsor, uses a combination of index futures, options, and total-return swaps to construct a portfolio whose daily return is engineered to move in the opposite direction of the Nasdaq-100, with a two-times multiplier. When the tech index drops 1 percent in a day, REW is designed to move up roughly 2 percent. When the tech index rises 1 percent, REW is designed to fall roughly 2 percent.

The mechanics of that amplification are what matter. The leverage is built using derivatives, not margin or borrowing. REW holds a cash position and a carefully constructed derivatives portfolio that moves in sync with the target (-2x daily) movement. It rebalances at the end of each trading day to ensure that the next trading day's [leverage ratio](/leverage-ratio-forex/) is reset — hence the term "daily reset." This distinction is critical, because it creates a phenomenon called volatility decay. If an index rises 2 percent one day and falls 2 percent the next, it ends up flat. A -2x inverse with daily reset would have fallen 4 percent on day one (when the index rose 2 percent) and would have risen 4 percent on day two (when the index fell 2 percent), ending up slightly higher than flat. Over longer periods, volatility decay erodes the inverse fund's returns relative to a simple inverse position or a traditional short sale, because the daily resets cause the fund to be "short at the worst times and long at the best times" on average.

## Who uses REW and why

REW is a short-term tactical tool, not an investment. Sophisticated traders and [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) use inverse-leveraged ETFs to hedge technology exposure on a day-to-day or week-to-week basis — to profit from or protect against sharp declines in the Nasdaq-100. Retail investors sometimes buy these products in the mistaken belief that they are a hedge to technology holdings over months or years, which they are not; the volatility decay would erase the hedge over longer periods.

The fund trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) with typical ETF liquidity, meaning it is straightforward to buy and sell. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is modest, but the leverage and derivatives positions are what drive the fund's returns, not the cost of managing the underlying index.

## The core risks

The first and most obvious risk is leverage. A 3 percent decline in the Nasdaq-100 results in a 6 percent gain in REW; a 3 percent rise results in a 6 percent loss. This amplification means the fund is volatile and suitable only for investors with high risk tolerance and a short time horizon.

The second risk is volatility decay. If an investor holds REW for more than a single day or week, the daily resets begin to work against the position. The more volatile the underlying index, the worse the decay. In a market that rises 20 percent over three months with steady, gentle gains, REW will probably track -2x that movement reasonably well. In a market that rises and falls multiple times within that same three-month period, even if the net move is still up 20 percent, REW will likely have decayed to a loss of more than -40 percent. This is not a flaw in the fund; it is a mathematical property of leveraged daily-reset funds. Anyone holding REW for longer than a few days must understand this risk.

The third risk is structural. Leveraged inverse ETFs require daily rebalancing, which involves trading futures and derivatives. In extreme market conditions — sharp gaps, [circuit breakers](/circuit-breaker/), market halts — the fund may not be able to rebalance smoothly, and tracking error relative to the target (-2x) return can become substantial. During the March 2020 market crash, leveraged and inverse ETFs experienced periods of significant tracking error.

## How to research REW

If you are considering REW, you should not be making that decision lightly. The fund's prospectus is mandatory reading; it details the derivatives strategies, the daily-reset mechanics, and the specific risks of leverage and volatility decay. The fact sheet shows current performance, expense ratio, and holdings. You should compare REW's returns to what a simple -2x inverse position would have returned, and you should model what the fund would do in scenarios of high volatility to understand the decay risk concretely.

Because REW is meant for short-term tactical use, monitor it daily if you hold it. Leaving a -2x leveraged short position unattended for months is a recipe for unexpected results.
