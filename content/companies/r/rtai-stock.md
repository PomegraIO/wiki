The Rareview Tax Advantaged [Income ETF](/income-etf/) pursues an uncommon strategy in the crowded field of income-focused exchange-traded funds: it tries to deliver meaningful current cash returns while keeping the tax bill lower than traditional [dividend](/dividend/) funds would create. The fund does this by coupling a portfolio of established dividend-paying stocks with a systematic covered-call options program — selling [call options](/call-option/) against those holdings to collect premium income, which typically reduces upside but stabilizes and enhances the cash distributed to shareholders.

The fund holds a portfolio of U.S. large-cap and mid-cap dividend payers—typically names like utilities, REITs, energy companies, and established consumer staples where shareholders expect regular cash distributions. Rather than simply holding and collecting those dividends, the fund systematically sells call options—contracts that obligate it to sell those shares at a pre-set price if the stock rises above that level before expiration. This options income supplements the dividend and is distributed to shareholders.

The math is simple and familiar to anyone who has studied income strategies. When you sell a call option, the buyer pays you a premium (income), but you give up the upside above a certain price—the [strike price](/strike-price/). If the stock stays flat or falls, you keep the premium and the dividend. If it rallies sharply, you are likely called away from the shares at the strike price, capping your gain. Over a full year of rolling these contracts, you trade potential long-term appreciation for a stream of [option premiums](/option-premium/) that, combined with the underlying dividends, can produce a yield well above what the stocks alone would offer.

That yield boost comes with a cost, and it is not subtle. Covered-call funds naturally underperform their underlying stocks in rising markets, because the sold calls cap upside participation. In a strong [bull market](/bull-market/), RTAI will lag—a deliberate and predictable drag that is the fee for the higher current income. Conversely, in flat or declining markets, the fund's steady cash distributions and reduced drawdown (because you are not exposed to the full equity decline) can make it appealing. The fund is explicitly targeted at retirees or other investors who need or want high current income and are willing to sacrifice appreciation to get it.

The tax-advantage angle enters through two mechanisms. First, option premiums are often treated as short-term capital gains at ordinary income-tax rates, but the fund's quarterly or monthly distributions can be structured to prioritize [qualified dividend](/qualified-dividend/) income where possible, which carries preferential tax rates for individuals. The fund's managers monitor the composition of distributions to maximize the amount treated as qualified dividends rather than ordinary income. Second, by covering some of the portfolio's volatility through sold calls, the strategy tends to reduce the turnover and realize fewer capital gains than a pure dividend fund might, further lowering the tax drag.

The fund is not for everyone. Conservative investors seeking long-term capital appreciation will find the capped upside frustrating; they are better served by a traditional dividend-growth fund. Aggressive growth investors will be bored by both the yield focus and the diluted price appreciation. RTAI is built for the middle band: retirees, bond-fund replacements, and income-focused investors who can live with moderate returns as long as the cash distributions are substantial and—relative to the risk taken—tax-efficient.

Researching RTAI means understanding both its underlying holdings and its options overlay. The fund's prospectus spells out the covered-call strategy and the expected yield target (typically described as a range, e.g., "5–7% depending on market conditions"), and the fact sheet will show the current holdings and the average strike prices of the options program. The fund's performance history compared to simple dividend-stock indices or comparable [income funds](/income-fund/) will reveal whether the covered-call drag is worth the cash yield in real-world conditions. For investors, the key question is whether they genuinely need the income (or prefer it) and are comfortable accepting capped upside as the cost.

A covered-call income fund lives and dies by execution: whether the option strikes are set with wisdom, whether the portfolio is diversified enough to weather a downturn in any one sector, and whether the tax structure lives up to its promise. Read the prospectus closely, compare the after-tax returns to alternative income funds, and decide whether the fund's yield and structure fit your cash needs. If you need appreciation, this is not it. If you need reliable distributions and can live with modest long-term returns, RTAI may deserve a serious look.
