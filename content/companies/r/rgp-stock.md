Resources Connection is a staffing and consulting firm that places experienced professionals with corporate clients on a contract or project basis. The company operates in a familiar model: it recruits and vets consultants in finance, accounting, human resources, IT, and other professional domains, then contracts their labour to enterprise clients for fees that are substantially higher than what the consultant receives, with RGP retaining the spread as gross margin. The business is simple, and simplicity is both its advantage and its constraint.

Being a mid-sized services firm in a [fragmented market](/fragmented-market/) means Resources Connection occupies the middle ground where many labour-arbitrage businesses live — large enough to have a brand and client relationships, but small enough that it cannot command the scale advantages of the very large staffing firms or compete on the specialization and premium positioning of top-tier boutique consulting. The company's scale buys it stability, in that it can absorb consultant attrition and client project cycles better than a tiny competitor could, but it denies the company the leverage that larger firms have over both consultants (who have more options at bigger firms) and clients (who might get volume discounts from behemoths like [Accenture](/acn-stock/) or IBM).

The fundamental unit economics of professional services staffing are straightforward: hire a consultant away from the market at some cost, contract them out at a higher rate, and pocket the spread. Margins depend on how efficiently the company can place consultants (high utilization rates across the portfolio) and how much market power it has over pricing. Consultants are a commodity in one direction — if you cannot place someone quickly, the cost of carrying them mounts and the economics go underwater — but specialized consultants (those with deep expertise in a particular domain, a particular software system, or a particular industry) are less commodity-like and command higher rates.

Resources Connection's positioning is toward the larger, more structured client base: finance departments of public companies, corporate reorganizations, technology implementations, and similar high-complexity, project-based work. This is higher-value staffing than general temporary labour, but it is also more cyclical. When companies cut budgets or postpone large projects, contract consulting is often one of the first levers management pulls. The company's revenue is therefore sensitive to [business cycle](/business-cycle/) and hiring freezes — the 2020 pandemic, the 2022–2023 tech layoffs, and the 2008 financial crisis all rippled through RGP's results.

Margins in staffing improve with scale, but only to a point. A much larger firm can negotiate better rates from clients and can move consultants between projects more efficiently. A much smaller firm can sometimes command higher premiums for specialization. RGP's scale is neither of those — it is subject to competitive pressure from both directions. The company's advantage is a solid brand among finance and HR teams at mid-to-large corporations, deep client relationships built over decades, and operational systems for recruiting, vetting, and managing consultants. But those advantages are not defensible in the way that network effects or proprietary data create defensibility elsewhere.

The company's cash flow profile is seasonal: demand for contract consulting tends to spike in the first and last quarters as companies pursue fiscal-year initiatives, and troughs in the middle of the year. Utilization rates — the percentage of bench consultants who are actively placed and billing — drive profitability more than absolute headcount. In a downturn, RGP must shrink the consultant population quickly to avoid carrying unprofitable labour; in recovery, it must hire and place rapidly to capture demand without missing the moment.

For anyone examining RGP as an investment, the key metrics are utilization rates and the average bill rate (the revenue per consultant per day or per project). The company's 10-K and quarterly filings (SEC CIK 0001084765) disclose revenue by practice area (finance and accounting, human resources, technology and operations consulting, etc.) and provide insight into which segments are growing and which are shrinking. Watch the trend in headcount growth relative to revenue growth — if headcount is outpacing revenue, the company is building bench and betting on placement; if revenue is outpacing headcount, margins may be compression near-term or the company may be at risk of capacity constraints. Debt and cash flow are important because capital-light staffing firms should generate steady cash; if RGP is burning cash or carrying large debt, it signals operational stress or a deliberate investment in growth that has yet to pay off.
