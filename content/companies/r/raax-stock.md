Real Assets is a [thematic ETF](/thematic-etf/) that corrals companies and funds whose primary business is extracting, producing, or managing physical assets that have [intrinsic value](/intrinsic-value/) independent of financial markets. VanEck's interpretation includes three main buckets: (1) precious-metals miners and producers who pull gold, silver, and other metals from the earth; (2) oil and gas midstream companies—the pipelines, terminals, and infrastructure that transport and store energy after it is produced; (3) [real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/) that own land, buildings, and properties; and (4) timber companies and farmland REITs that harvest or lease land. Together, these are supposed to offer a hedge against [inflation](/inflation/) and financial-system stress—the logic being that if the currency debases or stocks and [bonds](/bond/) fall apart, tangible assets retain value.

The composition shifts with market valuations, but precious-metals exposure typically carries the most weight, followed by real estate. Exposure to pure commodity producers (oil-drilling companies, for instance) is more limited because the fund focuses on companies that own the assets themselves, not necessarily those that just pump them out and sell. That distinction matters: the equity of a pure exploration-and-production company can move wildly with oil prices and becomes a crude-oil play in disguise. The equity of a pipeline company that holds long-term contracts, by contrast, is more stable and dividend-focused, making it closer to the infrastructure-like return profile the fund's designers intended.

The real-assets thesis relies on a specific economic narrative. In normal times, when inflation is mild and financial assets are rewarded, real-assets stocks can underperform. Precious-metals miners tend to move with gold, which does not pay a [dividend](/dividend/) and often trades inversely to [real interest rates](/real-interest-rate/); when rates are high and rising, that is a poor trade. Oil-and-gas midstream companies do better in strong energy markets, which often coincide with booming economic growth—not a time when investors need a hedge. Real estate REITs struggle when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) climb because rising [discount rates](/discount-rate/) push down the present value of their cash flows. So there are long stretches where RAAX looks left behind, and investors question why they bothered buying "real assets" at all.

The fund works best for investors who genuinely believe inflation will be persistent and meaningful, or for those using RAAX as a modest satellite position to add [diversification](/diversification/) that moves differently from stocks and bonds. In a crisis where financial markets seize and [currency risk](/currency-risk/) becomes real, a basket of actual asset-owning companies can be a reasonable life raft. But that is a low-probability, high-impact scenario. In the mean case—steady growth, low to moderate inflation, stable interest rates—RAAX is likely to lag the market. The historical record bears this out: real-assets funds and commodities-linked funds have long periods of deep underperformance punctuated by brief, sharp rallies when inflation fears spike.

Holding RAAX also introduces complexity. Precious-metals stocks are volatile and sensitive to geopolitics, central-bank policy, and shifts in real interest rates. Oil-and-gas midstream is capital-intensive infrastructure, sensitive to interest rates and energy-market health. REITs are interest-rate-sensitive fixed-income-like products. Timber companies have their own weather and commodity-price risks. Combining all of these into a single fund means RAAX has a bit of character from each, but is not a pure play on any one theme. An investor trying to hedge inflation might be better served buying an [inflation-protected securities](/inflation-protected-securities/) ETF or explicit [commodity ETFs](/commodity-etf/) instead, where the relationship to inflation is more direct.

Another real constraint: many of RAAX's holdings are mid-cap or even small-cap companies that lack the liquidity of mega-cap stocks. The ETF itself has decent trading volume, but some of the underlying companies are thinly traded, which means the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) can diverge from its market price during stress or dislocations. In most conditions the spread between the ETF's trading price and its NAV is narrow, but in a market crash—precisely when an investor might want to sell the hedge—liquidity can evaporate.

The fund's quarterly [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) comes from the underlying holdings: metals-mining companies often pay dividends during profitable years, midstream infrastructure typically distributes cash, and REITs are required by law to pay out most of their income. That yield can be meaningful, but it is not stable. Metals miners cut dividends sharply when metals prices fall. REITs cut when interest rates spike and capital costs rise. So the income is cyclical, not a reliable cash-flow substitute.

For research, start with VanEck's factsheet, which lists the current holdings and weighting by asset class—how much gold miners, how much midstream, how much REITs. The MVIS Real Assets Index prospectus details the selection rules and the quarterly rebalancing mechanics. Monitor the fund's performance against simpler alternatives: a gold ETF, an infrastructure ETF, and a real-estate ETF separately would often be more cost-effective than bundling them. Watch how RAAX performs during periods of rising and falling [inflation expectations](/inflation-expectations/) to see whether it is actually delivering the advertised hedge benefit. And stay aware that real-assets exposure is most valuable to investors with a long time horizon and the conviction that inflation or financial instability will be genuinely problematic; for others, a smaller allocation—or none—is perfectly reasonable.
