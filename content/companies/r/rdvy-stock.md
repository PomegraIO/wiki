*RDVY is built on a deceptively simple thesis: companies that consistently raise their [dividends](/dividend/) year after year are not just paying income — they are signaling confidence in their cash generation, their competitive strength, and their future earnings. By owning a basket of such companies, an investor gains exposure to stability paired with rising cash flow.*

The First Trust Rising Dividend Achievers index, which RDVY tracks, identifies U.S.-listed companies that have increased their dividend payout for a required minimum number of consecutive years. The specific threshold has shifted with index revisions, but the principle is constant: only firms that have actually raised payouts make the cut. This is not a theoretical metric or a forecast; it is a demonstrated pattern of behavior. A company that has raised its dividend five, ten, or twenty years running has put its money where its mouth is, backing the commitment with actual cash.

The resulting portfolio spans across sectors — financial services, utilities, industrials, healthcare, consumer staples, and energy firms all appear in meaningful weights. Unlike an [income fund](/income-fund/) tilted entirely toward utilities or utilities and REITs, RDVY's [diversification](/diversification/) across sectors insulates it from concentrated bets on [interest rates](/interest-rate/) or infrastructure [regulation. A](/regulation-a/) dividend cut by energy producers or a sudden rate shock hitting financials or utilities hurts, but does not destroy the whole fund. The breadth of the portfolio means no single sector's headwinds can cripple total returns.

The companies in RDVY tend to be mature, profitable, and stable — the kinds of businesses that generate enough reliable cash flow to pay shareholders reliably and raise those payments over time. Young biotech firms, unprofitable tech companies, and other speculative ventures do not appear here; instead, the fund holds the operational backbone of the economy. This selectivity is powerful for an income-focused investor but comes with an obvious cost: the companies reinvesting profits aggressively for high growth are excluded or underweighted. The portfolio trades growth for stability and income.

For the investor seeking rising cash flows rather than rising stock prices, this trade is often the right one. A starting yield of 3 to 4 percent, paired with annual dividend increases, compounds into something substantial over decades. Your 3 percent yield today, amplified by a 5 percent annual dividend increase, becomes 4 percent next year, then 5 percent the year after, purely from management raising payouts. That compounding, when applied to a static portfolio value, can be as powerful as price appreciation — and it arrives in the form of actual cash the investor can use or reinvest.

The drawback is equally real. A portfolio of dividend raisers typically lags a broad market index during strong [bull markets](/bull-market/), because those rallies are usually driven by high-growth, low-dividend companies plowing earnings back into expansion. RDVY's price appreciation has often trailed a simple total-market ETF over multi-year stretches when growth stocks dominated. The income compensates for this underperformance in nominal terms — the investor is receiving dividends during the wait — but the feature is inescapable: choosing to own dividend raisers means foregoing the maximum possible price upside in a growth-driven market.

Sector composition shifts naturally with the economic cycle. When interest rates rise, banks and financial companies become more valuable dividend sources because they can raise rates on deposits and loans; they tend to grow their weight in the portfolio. When rates fall or the economy cools, utilities and consumer staples gain relative appeal as safer, more recession-resistant income sources. RDVY gradually rebalances itself into whatever sectors are currently raising dividends most aggressively — an organic, market-driven allocation that requires no manager judgment.

The fund carries a modest [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), typical for broad equity-based strategies, and trades with tight [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) reflecting large daily trading volumes. Dividend payouts flow to shareholders monthly or quarterly, depending on the underlying companies' calendars, with the fund distributing the full dividend stream it receives.

An investor evaluating RDVY should start with the [fund prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) and fact sheet to understand the exact inclusion criteria and current sector weights. Reviewing the holdings over time reveals which types of companies have most consistently raised dividends and how that diversification has held up during market cycles. Comparing the trailing yield to a broad market index and to other dividend-focused strategies answers a critical question: is the discipline of owning only dividend raisers delivering more income than simpler alternatives? And tracking the fund's price performance versus a total-return index reveals whether the sacrifice in price appreciation over the years has been worth the cash flow received.
