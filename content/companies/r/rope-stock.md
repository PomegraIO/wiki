*The **Coastal Compass 100 ETF** (ticker ROPE) is a moderately concentrated equity fund that holds 100 large-cap U.S. stocks chosen by Coastal Investment Management using a disciplined, research-driven selection process. The fund sits between the breadth of a traditional [index fund](/index-fund/) and the extreme concentration of a single-stock portfolio, offering investors a middle path: more focused than the market, but not so concentrated as to rest on a handful of bets.*

## What companies does ROPE hold?

ROPE's portfolio is composed of 100 of the largest publicly traded U.S. companies, selected from the universe of the largest 500 or so by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/). The holdings span all major sectors — technology, healthcare, finance, industrials, consumer goods, and energy — and the fund attempts to maintain meaningful exposure to each area. Within each sector, Coastal's research team identifies the companies with the strongest competitive positions, the most durable business models, and the most attractive valuations. The result is a portfolio that is more selective than a broad index but still sufficiently diversified that no single position dominates.

## How is ROPE different from an index fund?

The most important difference is active selection. While a typical [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) fund holds all 500 large-cap stocks in proportion to their market capitalization, ROPE holds only 100, chosen by Coastal's analysts. This means that ROPE will diverge from the index in both composition and performance. In years when Coastal's picks outperform the broader market, ROPE beats the index. In years when they underperform, ROPE lags. A broad index fund offers the assurance of tracking the market average; ROPE offers the possibility of beating it, alongside the risk of falling short.

The concentration also affects volatility. With only 100 positions instead of 500, each position has a larger impact on the fund's returns. This is not leverage — ROPE does not use borrowed money or derivatives — but it is [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/). An adverse development at one of the top 10 holdings can move the fund's price more than it would in a broader index.

## What methodology does Coastal use to select stocks?

Coastal Investment's selection process is grounded in fundamental analysis. The firm's research team analyzes financial statements, industry dynamics, competitive positioning, management quality, and valuation to identify stocks that meet their criteria. The goal is not to time the market or to follow trends, but to identify companies that are trading at reasonable valuations and have sustainable competitive advantages. The fund rebalances periodically, typically moving positions when valuations shift or when the underlying investment thesis changes.

## Who should own ROPE, and who should avoid it?

ROPE is appropriate for an investor who believes that active stock selection can add value after fees and who is comfortable with moderate concentration. It suits someone who wants a more focused portfolio than a total-market index but does not want to bet heavily on a small number of individual companies. The investor should have a long time horizon — at least five years, ideally longer — because the fund's performance relative to the index will fluctuate over shorter periods.

ROPE is not suitable for an investor who believes that markets are efficient, that active management does not add persistent value after fees, or who is uncomfortable with the possibility that the fund may underperform the index in any given year or stretch of years. It is also not appropriate for an investor who requires maximum [diversification](/diversification/); the 100-position structure is concentrated relative to a total-market fund.

## How does ROPE charge investors?

Like all ETFs, ROPE charges an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) — the annual fee that compensates Coastal for management and operations. This ratio is higher than a passive index fund's fee but typically lower than an actively managed [mutual fund's](/mutual-fund/) because ETFs operate with lower distribution and administrative costs. The exact percentage is disclosed in the fund's prospectus and fact sheet. Investors also pay the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) when they buy or sell ROPE shares on the exchange, though this spread is generally tight because the [market maker](/market-maker-trading/) can create and redeem ETF shares in kind.

## What are the main risks?

ROPE's largest risk is selection risk: if Coastal's picks underperform the broad market, the fund will lag the index by approximately the underperformance margin minus the fee difference. This can happen for extended periods. For example, if a market segment that Coastal is underweighting significantly outperforms, or if its picks are hit by industry-specific headwinds, the fund can lag for years before the analysis reasserts itself.

A second risk is concentration. Because the fund holds only 100 stocks, an unexpected development at a large position can move the fund's price more dramatically than it would affect a 500-stock index. While diversification within a portfolio is strong, ROPE is more vulnerable to individual company shocks than a broader fund.

Finally, ROPE, like all equity funds, is subject to [market risk](/market-risk/). In a [bear market](/bear-market/), when stocks broadly are selling off, ROPE will fall alongside the index. The fund offers no hedge against systematic equity-market decline.

## How would you research ROPE before investing?

Begin with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, which detail the selection process, the expense ratio, the top holdings, and the sector breakdown. Review the fund's historical performance relative to a relevant index — perhaps the S&P 500 — over multiple time periods: one year, three years, five years, and since inception. Underperformance over a single year is noise; underperformance over five years is a signal. Examine Coastal's investment philosophy and track record on the firm's website, looking for consistency and transparency in their approach. Finally, consider whether you believe the fee is justified by the potential for outperformance, and whether the 100-position structure aligns with your own comfort level for concentration and risk.
