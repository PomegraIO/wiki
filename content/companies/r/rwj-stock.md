*The **Invesco S&P SmallCap 600 Revenue ETF** ([NASDAQ](/nasdaq/): RWJ) is a passive [index fund](/index-fund/) that holds the constituents of the S&P SmallCap 600 but weights them by net revenue instead of by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/), making each stock's influence on returns proportional to the size of its sales rather than its stock-market value.*

## What does RWJ actually track?

The S&P SmallCap 600 is a standard market-cap-weighted index of 600 U.S. companies with market capitalizations roughly between $400 million and $3 billion. The net revenue version inverts the typical weighting: instead of a mega-cap company weighing more than a micro-cap company because its stock is worth more, all companies are sized by their trailing twelve-month net revenue. A company doing $50 billion in revenue will have 50 times the portfolio weight of a company doing $1 billion in revenue, regardless of what investors currently think either company is worth.

## Why would an investor choose revenue weighting over market cap?

The theory is that revenue represents something more fundamental than stock price — it is money actually coming in the door, not investor sentiment about future profitability. Market-cap weighting can create biases: expensive companies with high growth expectations gain weight even if they have not yet proven they can turn sales into profits, while cheap, profitable companies with modest growth lose weight. A revenue-weighted approach tilts toward companies that are materially larger in the real economy relative to their stock-market valuations, which some investors view as a value tilt. Over long periods, this can reduce [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) and tilt toward companies with established business models and sustainable cash generation.

## What are the risks?

The primary risk is that revenue weighting is a backward-looking measure in a forward-looking market. A company losing market share or facing disruption might still have high revenues today, so it will be overweighted in RWJ even as its future is deteriorating. Market-cap weighting automatically downgrades losers as investors reprrice them; revenue weighting does not. Additionally, not all revenue is created equal — a dollar of revenue at 3% net margin (retail) is worth far less than a dollar of revenue at 25% net margin (software). RWJ could accumulate low-margin businesses while missing high-margin winners. Finally, the small-cap universe itself is more volatile, less liquid, and harder to predict than large-cap stocks, so this fund has a volatility floor that revenue weighting alone cannot overcome.

## How is RWJ different from other small-cap funds?

Most small-cap index ETFs track the market-cap-weighted S&P SmallCap 600 or Russell 2000 and charge very low [expense ratios](/expense-ratio/) (0.05–0.1%). RWJ has a modest fee that reflects the cost of quarterly rebalancing to revenue weights, which are more mechanical to implement than market-cap weights. The fund's returns will diverge from the market-cap-weighted SmallCap 600 in any period where revenues and stock prices are out of sync — high-growth small caps will underperform in RWJ, while mature, profitable small caps will outperform.

## How to evaluate RWJ

Start with the fund's prospectus and annual fact sheet, which list all current holdings, their revenue weights, and the fund's trailing returns net of fees. Compare RWJ's performance to the standard market-cap-weighted S&P SmallCap 600 index over the past 5, 10, and 15 years to see whether revenue weighting has added or subtracted value. Check the fund's [turnover ratio](/turnover-ratio/) — revenue weighting requires quarterly or semi-annual rebalancing, creating tax consequences and trading costs. Finally, ask whether the tilt toward mature, established small-cap companies aligns with your investment goals and time horizon.
