*The **[Risk Parity](/risk-parity/) ETF (RPAR)**, issued by Invesco, flips the conventional wisdom about portfolio construction by refusing to treat [bonds](/bond/) and stocks as a hierarchy. Instead of the traditional 60/40 split — 60% equities because they dominate by market value, 40% bonds as a cushion — risk parity asks: what allocation actually contributes equal risk from each bucket? The answer, for most of recent market history, has been something more like 35% stocks and 65% bonds, with leverage on the bond side to amplify its contribution. The fund is built for investors who distrust the assumption that bigger numbers must mean bigger influence on a portfolio's fate.*

## What risk parity actually does

The premise behind risk parity is deceptively simple: in a traditional portfolio, equities drive almost all the volatility and price swings. A 60/40 portfolio gets roughly 95% of its day-to-day gyrations from the stock 60%, leaving the bonds to provide ballast without any real say in where the money is going. Risk parity redistributes that influence. It weights each asset class — equities, [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/), commodities, and sometimes inflation-linked securities — so that each one contributes roughly the same amount of risk to the overall portfolio. Because bonds are less volatile than stocks, that means holding far more bonds by dollar amount and using moderate leverage on the fixed-income side to bring its risk contribution up to par.

RPAR targets that balance by holding a diversified mix across U.S. and international equities, U.S. Treasury and investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), [Treasury inflation-protected securities](/tips/), and commodities exposure. The fund rebalances periodically to maintain the risk-equal weighting. The leverage comes through the fund's structure — it uses derivatives and borrowing to amplify the bond and commodity portions without requiring the investor to take out their own margin loan. From a practical standpoint, RPAR offers a single-fund expression of the risk-parity philosophy: one ticker that enforces a discipline that would otherwise require buying four or five separate positions and rebalancing by hand.

## Why the approach appeals — and its limits

Risk parity emerged during a long period when bonds delivered strong returns and acted as a genuine shock absorber. The 2008 financial crisis gave it credibility: while stocks cratered, bonds rallied, and a risk-parity investor who held more bonds would have come through the downturn better than a traditional stock-heavy allocator. That logic still holds in [deflation](/deflation/), when bonds typically rise as rates fall and safe-haven demand surges. In [inflation](/inflation/) and rising-rate environments — like the 2022 bond [bear market](/bear-market/) — the strategy offers no such refuge. Both stocks and bonds fell, and the extra leverage on the bond side simply meant larger losses, not smaller ones.

The approach also assumes that bonds and stocks will behave differently in future crises the way they did in 2008. That is a reasonable historical bet, but it is not guaranteed. Risk parity works best when correlations are low and bonds provide genuine insurance. In environments where everything sells off together, the [rebalancing discipline](/rebalancing-discipline/) becomes either an opportunity or an anchor, depending on timing.

## Structure and costs

RPAR is a fully transparent, exchange-traded fund domiciled in the United States. It trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) under the Invesco banner and carries an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) in the range typical for actively managed or rules-based strategic-allocation funds — meaningfully higher than a simple [index fund](/index-fund/), reflecting the rebalancing, leverage management, and the embedded cost of maintaining the risk-equal weighting. The fund is liquid and can be bought and sold like any [equity ETF](/equity-etf/); its daily volume is modest but sufficient for institutional and retail orders to clear.

The leverage used in the fund is structural, not leveraged on a daily basis, so RPAR does not suffer from the volatility decay that afflicts inverse and 3x [leveraged ETFs](/leveraged-etf/). That said, it does introduce a cost in the form of funding charges on the borrowed money used to amplify bond exposure — a drag that compounds over time, especially in rising-rate environments when borrowing costs rise.

## Real risks

The first risk is philosophical: the fund assumes bonds and stocks will behave like independent actors in future crises, when historical correlations may not hold. The second is mechanical: in a world of persistently high and volatile [interest rates](/interest-rate/), the leverage on the bond side magnifies losses. A 10-year bond bear market is much more painful when you own 65% of the bond market by dollar value than when you own a conventional 40%.

A third, less obvious risk is that the risk-parity approach assumes you can rebalance into weakness without regret. If stocks fall and you sell bonds to rebalance into equities, that discipline delivers only if equities then recover. If they continue falling, you have locked in early sales at bad prices and own more of the falling asset. Risk parity is a rebalancing discipline, not a market-timing system, and discipline only pays off over full cycles.

## How to research RPAR

Start with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, which lay out the exact asset-class targets and the rebalancing methodology. Compare the fund's rolling performance — one, three, five, and ten years — against a static 60/40 benchmark and a pure equity index to see where risk parity adds or subtracts. Pay special attention to performance in rising-rate and deflationary environments separately. The fund's website publishes the current allocation, so you can verify that the risk-weighting is actually held in practice. For context on the broader risk-parity philosophy and its history, look to papers and commentary from the strategy's originators and critics — the debate is substantive and both sides make sound points.
