ProShares Short Russell 2000 (RWM) is an exchange-traded fund that bets against the Russell 2000, an index of roughly 2,000 small and mid-sized US companies. When the Russell 2000 falls, RWM rises. When the Russell 2000 rises, RWM falls. It is designed for investors or advisors who want to hedge or tactically profit from a near-term decline in small-cap stocks without needing to borrow and short individual securities.

## How it works — and its critical limit

RWM achieves its inverse exposure by holding [derivative contracts](/derivative-contract/) — primarily equity index swaps and futures — that pay out when the underlying index declines. The fund aims for a negative one-to-one correlation with the Russell 2000 on a daily basis, meaning a 1% drop in the index should produce roughly a 1% gain in the fund. This mechanics is straightforward and legal, but it has a severe built-in flaw: the daily reset guarantee only holds for a single day.

Because derivatives have to be rolled and recalibrated every day, a prolonged series of small moves can create compounding slippage. If the Russell 2000 zigzags — up 2% one day, down 3% the next, up 1% the next — RWM will not perfectly track negative returns over the multi-day period. Instead, the series of daily resets accumulates small losses, a phenomenon called volatility decay or beta slippage. The longer an investor holds RWM, and the more volatile the small-cap index is, the more pronounced the decay becomes. This is why RWM is explicitly designed for short-term tactical trades, not [buy-and-hold investing](/buy-and-hold/).

| Holding period | Decay risk | Primary use |
|---|---|---|
| 1–5 days | Minimal | Tactical hedge against imminent weakness |
| 1–3 months | Moderate | Seasonal rotations, earnings seasons |
| 6+ months | Very high | Not recommended; regular shorting preferable |

## Structure and costs

RWM trades on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) like any other ETF. The fund holds virtually no stocks; instead, it holds the derivative instruments that deliver the inverse return. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is modest relative to the complexity involved — typically around 0.95% annually — but it compounds over time, especially if held in a volatile market where the derivatives lose value and must be replaced at unfavourable prices.

The fund does not hold a cash reserve significant enough to pay out large redemptions; instead, it rebalances dynamically using its derivative positions. This means that periods of extreme small-cap volatility can cause the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) to swing sharply, and wide [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) may emerge during market dislocations when liquidity dries up. For this reason, RWM is best used in normal market conditions, not during crashes or crises when small-cap stocks are in free fall and derivatives pricing becomes unreliable.

## When and why investors use it

Advisors may use RWM as a temporary hedge in a portfolio of small-cap stocks if they believe a near-term correction is likely but do not want to sell holdings and realize capital gains. Because the fund moves opposite to the Russell 2000, it offsets small-cap losses during downturns. A portfolio that is 90% small-cap stocks and 10% RWM will lose much less in a 10% small-cap decline than one fully invested in small caps.

Individual traders sometimes use RWM to profit from expected weakness in small caps, treating it like a leveraged bearish bet. Because it resets daily, any profits from a correct short-term forecast are not eroded by daily decay — if the Russell 2000 falls 2% in a week and a trader exits RWM, they capture roughly that 2% gain with minimal slippage. But if that trader holds RWM while the market drifts sideways for months, they will gradually lose money to the fund's own expenses and the compounding effects of daily rebalancing.

## The real risk: confusion with sustained short exposure

The fund is often misunderstood. New investors sometimes buy RWM expecting a sustained bet against small-cap stocks over a year or more, only to discover that volatility and expense ratios have eroded their gains. A trader who bought RWM in January expecting small caps to crash and then watched them rise in a volatile, choppy pattern would find that their loss is substantially worse than the simple opposite of the Russell 2000's return, because each day's reset compounds the losses.

For someone seeking genuine long-term short exposure to the Russell 2000, [short selling](/short-selling/) individual stocks or using [put options](/put-option/) is preferable. RWM is a tool for the next few days or weeks, not the next year.

## How to evaluate and use RWM

Check the fund's fact sheet and prospectus to understand the exact derivative strategy and the holdings in its portfolio of futures and swaps. The daily factsheet shows the fund's net asset value and price, which may diverge slightly due to market conditions. Track the fund's actual returns versus the inverse of the Russell 2000's returns over short periods (one week, one month) to verify that daily tracking works as expected; this is a sanity check that the fund is functioning as intended. For periods longer than three months, calculate the volatility decay ratio to estimate how much slippage has accumulated.

RWM is not a hold-it-and-forget-it security; it is a tactical position that requires monitoring and exit discipline. Use it only if you have a clear, time-bound thesis about small-cap weakness and can commit to reviewing the position regularly.
