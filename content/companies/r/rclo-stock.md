> "A CLO is a securities vehicle that buys a portfolio of leveraged loans, slices them into tranches by credit quality, and sells them to investors hungry for yield — turning illiquid middle-market debt into tradable bonds for those willing to hold credit risk."

The Reckoner BBB-B CLO ETF offers exposure to [collateralized loan obligations](/collateralized-loan-obligation/), specialized fixed-income vehicles that pool hundreds of middle-market corporate loans and repackage them into tradable securities with different risk tiers. RCLO holds the mid-to-lower [tranches](/tranche/) (BBB-B rated), capturing yields significantly above [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) and investment-grade corporate debt, in exchange for accepting that some underlying loans will default and that [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) can widen.

CLOs are a corner of fixed income that most retail investors never encounter directly, yet they are central to the financing of middle-market companies and private-equity portfolio companies across North America. RCLO is a way for a retail investor to own a slice of that ecosystem without building a dedicated derivatives trading desk.

## What a CLO is and why they exist

A CLO begins with a manager who buys a portfolio of 100–150 leveraged loans — typically loans to companies too big for banks to refuse (revenues $50 million to $1 billion) but not big enough for traditional [bond](/bond/) markets. These are loans to PE-backed companies, roll-ups, and transitioning mid-market firms. The manager holds the entire portfolio and then securitizes it: she issues multiple tranches of securities (AAA, AA, A, BBB, BB, B) that have first claim on the cash flows of the loan portfolio, with lower tranches absorbing losses first.

An investor who buys the AAA tranche has the strongest claim and earns a modest coupon (close to [corporate bond](/corporate-bond/) yields). An investor in the B tranche (where RCLO sits) earns a much fatter coupon — perhaps 8–10% — but stands to lose principal if enough underlying loans default. The structure is mathematically elegant: the losses that would wipe out a B investor are paid by defaults and recoveries that the AAA holder never sees.

Why CLOs exist: banks and institutional loan funds have balance-sheet constraints and would prefer to sell loans after originating them, freeing capital to make new loans. Pension funds and insurers have vast pools of capital seeking yield above Treasuries but below equity risk. CLOs bridge that gap, allowing the loan originator to sell, the institutional investor to earn yield, and the middle-market borrower to access financing at reasonable rates.

## The return and risk of RCLO's portion of the stack

RCLO holds the BBB-B tranches, which are the "equity-like" parts of a CLO — the layers that absorb losses first and earn the highest yields. The coupon is attractive: 8–10% annually (or higher, depending on the loan-loss environment). The risk is equally real: if the underlying loan portfolio degrades — defaults rise, companies struggle, spreads widen — RCLO holders mark losses. A 10% increase in default expectations in the loan market can translate to a 5–10% decline in the value of a B tranche.

Leverage in the underlying loans amplifies this risk. Most CLO portfolios consist of loans to companies with [debt-to-EBITDA ratios](/debt-to-ebitda-ratio/) in the 4–6 range, which is aggressive. In a [recession](/recession/), when [EBITDA](/ebitda/) contracts, debt covenants break, and defaults spike. RCLO investors are betting that defaults will stay within historical norms (3–5% annually) and that recoveries (money back after a default) will be sufficient to cover losses. In a severe recession, that bet can look very wrong.

## Credit cycle dependency and market timing

RCLO is deeply cyclical. Early in the [credit cycle](/credit-cycle/), when defaults are low and expectations are benign, the fund performs well and spreads tighten. Late in the cycle, as loan quality deteriorates and recession looms, spreads widen and RCLO declines sharply. RCLO reached peak appeal in 2021–2022 when yields were high and [default rates](/default-rate/) were historically low; it has been less attractive in periods when recession risk mounts and institutional investors flee riskier credit.

The real risk is misjudging the cycle. A shareholder who buys RCLO at the peak of euphoria — when the loan market has written loose covenants, underwritten marginal credits, and pushed yields to decade lows — is setting herself up for mean reversion. Equally, a shareholder who buys RCLO in the depths of a panic (during a credit crunch) may be buying at a true opportunity cost, as spreads are wide and the cycle is turning up.

## The management layer

RCLO is a fund of CLOs, not a CLO itself — meaning the fund holds many different CLO securities from different managers (Blackstone, Ares, Carlyle, and others). This [diversification](/diversification/) is valuable: the risks of a single CLO manager's credit picks are diluted across many managers. However, the fund adds another layer of fees: the underlying CLO managers take fees (typically 0.4–0.7% of assets), and the RCLO fund itself charges an additional [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/). Total drag is qualitatively moderate, but in a slow-return environment or a rising-rate period, fees compound.

## How the loan market moves RCLO

RCLO is sensitive to three drivers. First, the health of the underlying borrowers — employment, credit-card defaults, manufacturing orders, and other [leading indicators](/leading-indicator/) of economic stress flow through the loan market faster than they do equity or bond markets. Second, covenant-lite issuance and underwriting standards. If banks are issuing loans with weak covenants and tight spreads, the CLO stack is exposed to easier defaults when trouble hits. Third, Fed policy and credit spreads. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) tightens rates and credit spreads widen, RCLO underperforms because mark-to-market losses accumulate. When the Fed cuts rates and spreads tighten, RCLO performs strongly.

## Illiquidity and redemption risk

CLO securities themselves are illiquid — they do not trade on exchanges, and [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) are wide. RCLO is liquid (it trades on the exchange), but during stress episodes, the fund can face redemptions (shareholders selling) while the underlying CLO holdings are slow to sell. The fund may need to hold cash or sell the most liquid CLO positions at unfavorable prices to meet redemptions, which hurts staying shareholders.

During the March 2020 stress, loan-market liquidity evaporated, and some CLO ETFs gapped down sharply because redemptions overwhelmed the fund's ability to trade the underlying positions. That extreme event was resolved quickly, but it underscores that owning CLO exposure via an ETF introduces a wedge of fund-level [liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/) atop the [credit risk](/credit-risk/) of the loans.

## Who RCLO fits

RCLO suits a credit investor hunting for yield above traditional corporate bonds, comfortable with 5–10% annual volatility, and willing to hold through a credit cycle. It works in a balanced portfolio as a diversifier from Treasuries and equities, because CLO default rates are not perfectly correlated with stock returns. For a pension fund or insurance company facing liability matching, the 8–10% coupon and the structured cash flows of CLOs are valuable.

RCLO does not suit a conservative investor needing principal preservation, nor does it suit someone unable to tolerate paper losses of 20–30% during credit stress. It also does not suit someone timing the credit cycle trying to catch the exact bottom; CLO returns are path-dependent and early in a tightening cycle, performance can lag even though the opportunity is forming.

## Researching RCLO

Read the prospectus and fact sheet to understand the composition of the underlying CLO portfolio — loan types, industries, company sizes, [leverage ratios](/leverage-ratio-forex/). Check the default and loss rates by [vintage (year](/vintage-year/) of issuance) in recent CLO manager reports; that data reveals how much stress is embedded in the current portfolio. Monitor loan-market spreads (LCDX index) as a leading indicator of stress; when spreads blow out, RCLO is likely to decline. Watch financial conditions and Fed guidance; tightening cycles are historically difficult for CLO performance.
