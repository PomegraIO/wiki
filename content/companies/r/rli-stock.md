## What does RLI actually insure?

RLI Corp is a specialty insurance company that writes coverage for risks standard carriers typically decline or ignore. Its portfolio includes professional liability insurance for architects, engineers, and construction firms; product liability for manufacturers; and coverage for specialized industries like solar installation, contractors' equipment, and emerging tech sectors. RLI does not pursue volume; it pursues precision. A general liability insurer might be happy to write a thousand policies at thin margins and accept that some claims will erode their profits. RLI instead wants to write a hundred policies where it understands the risk so deeply that it can price accurately and decline the ones it knows will lose money. This philosophy — deep expertise in narrow niches rather than broad commodity underwriting — defines the entire company.

## Why would an insured customer choose RLI over a bigger carrier?

Large, diversified insurers maintain vast claim-handling infrastructure, national brand recognition, and the capital to absorb large losses. They can offer a broader array of coverage types bundled together. RLI offers something different: underwriters who live and breathe the specific risks their customers face. An architect designing a residential complex worries about design defects, failure to meet codes, and the liability that follows if the building settles or leaks. RLI's underwriters know these risks, know what questions to ask when evaluating a firm's design process and quality controls, and can price the policy without padding for uncertainty. That expertise means RLI can offer competitive rates to good risks — firms with solid practices and loss history — while maintaining disciplined pricing on marginal ones. A customer with a clean record and strong processes gets a fair price; the less-disciplined competitor cannot. Over time, this creates a book of profitable business.

## How does RLI make money?

Like all insurers, RLI makes money in two ways. The first is underwriting: collecting premiums, paying out claims, and trying to keep premiums above claim payouts plus operating costs. The second is investing. Premiums arrive today but major claims might not be paid for years, so insurers hold customer capital in investments — [bonds](/bond/), stocks, real estate — that generate returns. When underwriting is profitable (premiums exceed claims), the investment returns boost overall returns. When underwriting runs at a loss, the investments must make up the gap.

RLI's success over decades has come from disciplined underwriting that generates positive underwriting income more often than not. The company writes a mix of liability lines — professional liability, product liability, management liability, specialized industry risks — which diversifies the portfolio. A bad year in one niche does not sink the whole company. The company also maintains substantial capital reserves, which allows it to write large policies (offering high limits to customers) without fear that a major claim will strain its ability to pay. This capacity is valuable to customers who need coverage beyond what smaller or undercapitalized carriers can offer.

## What's the competitive picture?

Specialty insurance is less concentrated than standard property-casualty lines. Large competitors like Chubb and Ace operate in specialty niches as a small part of a much larger company. Smaller pure-play specialty carriers exist, along with niche players focused on single industries. RLI stands out for its scale relative to its specialty positioning: it is large and capitalized enough to write substantial policies and operate efficiently, yet still focused enough to maintain deep underwriting expertise. Some business has moved online or to direct channels in recent years, which could pressure smaller carriers, but professional liability — RLI's largest segment — still benefits from underwriter-to-customer relationships where expertise and trust matter more than price.

## What are the main risks?

Specialty insurance is inherently cyclical. In flush periods when capital is abundant, new entrants underwrite business at unsustainable prices, pushing all carriers' margins down. When losses mount and capital becomes scarce, pricing recovers. RLI's ability to maintain discipline during soft markets — to walk away from business rather than chase volume at losing prices — is crucial to its long-term returns. That requires conviction and a willingness to see premium written decline temporarily. Execution risk is real.

The other big risks are long-tail claims. RLI writes professional liability policies that might not be claimed for years after issuance. A design defect in a building might not surface until five years after construction. An architectural firm might go out of business, leaving RLI holding reserves for claims that will take a decade to settle. Changes in how courts award damages, or in regulatory interpretations of liability, can make old estimates obsolete. The company must rely on actuarial models and claims data to estimate its obligation, but these models are imperfect.

## How would an investor research RLI?

Start with the annual 10-K, which breaks the underwriting results by segment and shows the loss ratios (claims paid as a percentage of premium earned) for each business. The loss ratio is the key metric: a ratio below 100 percent means underwriting profit; above means underwriting loss. Also watch the combined ratio, which includes loss ratio plus operating expenses; anything below 100 is profitable underwriting.

The earnings calls offer useful commentary on pricing trends, claims experience, and the pipeline of new business by segment. Watch for evidence that the company is maintaining its discipline during soft market periods when competitors cut prices. Also track the ratio of invested assets to reserves — how much capital is RLI holding in proportion to its obligations? A tight ratio suggests the company is efficiently deployed; a very loose ratio might suggest either over-capitalization or aggressive growth that will strain capital over time.

The investment portfolio deserves attention too. What percentage is in bonds, stocks, or alternatives? In a rising-rate environment, bond portfolios marked to market show losses; in a falling-rate environment they show gains. This can mask or amplify the underlying underwriting performance, so separating the two gives a clearer picture of the business.

Finally, look for any commentary on large losses or adverse claims development. If an old reserve estimate for a particular accident year turns out to have been too low, RLI might report "adverse development" and take a charge. This is normal in insurance, but the frequency and magnitude signal whether the company's actuarial estimates are reliable or whether claims are developing worse than expected.
