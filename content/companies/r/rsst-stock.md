*Return stacking is a technique that lets a fund hold a full position in one asset (say, U.S. stocks) and layer a second position on top by using leverage or derivatives — in this case, pairing equities with managed-futures contracts on commodities, [bonds](/bond/), and currencies. RSST pursues higher expected returns by combining these uncorrelated bets through futures rather than buying some stocks and some futures directly.*

The intuition behind RSST is that U.S. stocks and managed-futures strategies tend to perform in different market regimes. Stocks usually rise in expansions and fall in contractions. [Managed futures](/managed-futures/) — trend-following strategies that bet on commodity prices, bond yields, and currency movements — often do well in volatile or recessionary periods because they are trading momentum rather than holding a single asset class. By stacking these two positions together through leverage, the fund is not trying to time which regime will win, but rather to capture both streams simultaneously at a lower expected volatility than holding them separately.

The mechanics rely heavily on derivatives. Rather than buying and holding shares directly and separately buying [futures contracts](/futures-contract/), a return-stacking fund uses the leverage inherent in futures and swaps to condense two positions into one fund wrapper. This means the fund holds a smaller amount of actual stock (say, 60% of assets) while using the rest to access a meaningful managed-futures allocation through futures contracts. The leverage is moderate — designed to keep total risk roughly in line with a traditional equity fund — but the leverage is there, and that is a fundamental difference from a passive stock-and-bonds split.

The managed-futures piece is key to understanding the [diversification](/diversification/). Managed futures are not speculative bets on which way the market will turn; they are mechanical trend-following strategies. If oil prices have been rising, a managed-futures strategy holds oil futures. If they start falling, it switches to short oil futures. The same logic applies to bonds, currencies, and equity indices. This means the strategy is often long risk assets during expansions (when trends are up) and short them during contractions (when trends are down), which creates a natural hedge to the concurrent equity position.

A simple example: in 2022, U.S. stocks fell sharply while [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rose and volatility spiked. A managed-futures strategy — by shorting equities and going long bonds and volatility as trends shifted — would have gained, offsetting some of the stock losses. RSST would have captured both the equity decline and some of the managed-futures gain, netting a smaller loss than holding stocks alone. Conversely, in a pure equity rally with no macro turbulence, the managed-futures piece might drag returns slightly because it spends time short what is going up.

The fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) reflects both the complexity of executing return stacking and the ongoing cost of the managed-futures sleeve. These are not passive index strategies; they involve active trading and systematic rebalancing. The cost is higher than a simple equity index ETF but lower than a typical [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/). The fund also incurs turnover and trading friction that can show up in tracking error relative to a theoretical perfectly-executed strategy.

Leverage introduces another layer of nuance. The fund uses leverage to maintain a target risk level — roughly the risk of holding equities — while spanning both asset classes. This means in very low-volatility periods, the fund might dial leverage up slightly to keep risk constant, and in high-volatility periods it might dial down. The fund does not attempt to create outsize risk or returns through leverage; it is a tool to make the two uncorrelated positions fit together more coherently. Still, leverage means that unexpected volatility spikes can create slippage, and the fund is more vulnerable to tail-risk events (crashes) than a non-leveraged equity fund would be.

Over longer periods, return stacking theoretically should deliver equity-like returns with lower drawdowns if the managed-futures piece performs as designed. In backtests and some live periods, this has held true. In others, the managed-futures strategy has lagged significantly, meaning the leverage was amplifying a drag rather than enhancing returns. The core risk is regime-dependent: the strategy works beautifully in a diversified regime (stocks up and down, volatility variable, interest rates changing) but can underperform in a strong trending market where equity momentum dominates everything else.

RSST is clearest for investors seeking stock exposure but unwilling to accept a traditional stock-market drawdown, and who believe that diversification into alternative strategies is worth the added cost and complexity. It is not for someone who wants simple, passive equity ownership. The investor needs comfort with leverage and derivatives, and a willingness to hold through periods when the managed-futures drag is most visible — precisely when equities are soaring and the alternative bets feel pointless. Anyone researching the fund should read the prospectus closely to understand the exact [leverage ratio](/leverage-ratio-forex/), the managed-futures index or strategy being replicated, and the rebalancing mechanism. The fund's historical performance in various market regimes — especially drawdown periods and low-volatility rallies — is the best lens for whether this blend matches the investor's temperament and objectives.
