*The **FT Vest Rising [Dividend](/dividend/) Achievers Target [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (ticker: RDVI) holds a portfolio of established U.S. companies that have demonstrated a pattern of increasing their dividend payments to shareholders year after year. The fund's design is simple: collect current dividend income while tilting the portfolio toward stocks likely to raise dividends in future years, thereby increasing the income stream over time without requiring the investor to constantly rebalance or replace holdings.*

## The rising-dividend thesis

A company that raises its dividend year after year signals confidence in its future cash generation — a management team is not going to promise growing distributions unless it expects the cash flow to support them. Because raising a dividend is a public commitment, and cutting it is a visible embarrassment, companies that engage in this practice tend to be financially disciplined and well-managed. They sit in relatively stable industries (utilities, packaged consumer goods, pharmaceuticals, financial services) where durable competitive advantages and predictable earnings support the practice.

RDVI selects from these names based on a systematic set of criteria: length of dividend-increase history (typically companies with at least a decade of consecutive dividend raises are preferred), the stability of earnings and cash flow, and the current level of the dividend relative to earnings (the [payout ratio](/payout-ratio/)). The fund does not hold exclusively Dividend Aristocrats (the S&P's official list of 500+ stocks with 25+ years of increases) but casts a wider net to capture younger companies on the same trajectory.

The portfolio turnover in a dividend-achiever strategy is typically low. Once a company demonstrates consistent dividend growth and meets the selection criteria, it tends to remain in the fund for years or decades, because the underlying business story does not change sharply. This low turnover reduces transaction costs and tax inefficiency for the fund, benefits that flow through to returns.

## Income and total return dynamics across cycles

In economic expansion, dividend-growth stocks perform well on both counts: their underlying businesses prosper, supporting rising earnings and higher dividends, and the stocks themselves appreciate. RDVI would deliver both capital gains and rising income. The sector tilts (toward utilities, consumer staples, healthcare, and industrials) mean the fund is less exposed to the most cyclical areas of the market, so it tends to underperform in the most exuberant [bull markets](/bull-market/) — growth stocks and technology names often run faster.

In contraction and [recession](/recession/), the equation shifts. Companies that raise dividends tend to be less volatile than the broad market, and their [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) (the annual payout as a percentage of stock price) often rises as stock prices fall — creating a larger cushion of income. When earnings come under pressure, the payout ratio (dividends as a percentage of earnings) can climb, and some companies do cut or freeze dividend growth; this risk is real. But over long periods, the discipline embedded in a dividend-increase practice usually insulates these portfolios from the worst declines. A shareholder holding RDVI in a downturn receives not just stability but a rising income stream, which can offset the paper loss if held long enough.

## The fee structure and expense profile

RDVI's annual [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically in the 0.40% to 0.70% range — competitive for an actively screened dividend strategy but higher than a passive dividend index ETF (which might cost 0.15% to 0.30%). The active screening and rebalancing to maintain the list of rising dividend payers, and the semi-annual or quarterly rebalancing, justify the marginal cost. The fund is highly liquid and trades at tight spreads on major exchanges.

Distributions from RDVI come primarily from dividends paid by the underlying companies; capital gains (if the fund or its holdings appreciate) are typically minimal in a steady-state income strategy. The dividend income is taxable at the federal and state level unless held in a tax-deferred account. The tax treatment of dividends is a significant consideration for investors in high tax brackets.

## Composition and risk profile

A typical RDVI portfolio holds 50 to 150 large and mid-cap companies, concentrated in sectors where dividend history is deep and reliable: consumer staples, utilities, pharmaceuticals, financial services, real estate. The fund is fully exposed to broad U.S. equity [market risk](/market-risk/) — stock prices can fall, and economic downturns can hit even the most stable dividend payers. It has no hedges, no [bond](/bond/) component, and no leverage. The sector concentration (heavy on defensive, lower-growth areas of the market) means it will lag broad-market indices when growth and technology are the market leaders, but will hold up better when those sectors fall.

## How to evaluate and research RDVI

Start with the fund's prospectus and current holdings list, both available from the issuer. The holdings reveal the fund's actual portfolio mix and payout ratios. Look at the fund's quarterly distribution history to see the trend of dividends paid per share over time. A key metric is the dividend yield — the trailing twelve-month dividend divided by the [net asset value](/net-asset-value/). Comparing that yield to a broad dividend index (such as the S&P Dividend Aristocrats) or to the S&P 500's yield shows whether RDVI is delivering more or less income than its peers.

Analyzing five- or ten-year total returns (capital appreciation plus reinvested dividends) versus the broader U.S. equity market indices provides context on whether the dividend-growth strategy is performing as intended: stable growth with higher income, at the cost of missing the peaks of technology booms. The fund's sector and market-cap profile (which should tilt toward large and mid-cap, and away from small-cap growth) is visible in its top-ten holdings and factsheet.
