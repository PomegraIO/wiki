VNUE started as a platform company for the music industry. Its core idea was simple enough: take live music events, record them immediately, and sell that content to fans right away. Decades later, the company owns a collection of platforms that try to solve different parts of how musicians earn money and how fans discover them. VNUE is now known as Redacted Industries, and its stated focus has shifted, but its earlier platforms still operate in live music streaming and royalty tracking.

## What the platforms do

VNUE built three main products aimed at different parts of the music supply chain.

**Set.fm** is a platform for recording and distributing live music instantly. The idea is that when a band plays a show, the recording gets captured and made available for purchase immediately afterward. Fans go home, go to Set.fm, find the concert they just attended, and can buy or stream the recording right then. For artists, this means a new revenue stream from every show. For fans, it means they can own or stream a recording of the exact performance they saw, rather than waiting for a studio album or studio version that may never come out. It shifts the economics of live performance slightly — instead of touring purely to build fanbase and sell merchandise, touring generates direct revenue from instant recordings.

**StageIt** is a ticketed livestreaming platform. Musicians can broadcast a live performance through StageIt, and fans anywhere in the world can buy a ticket to watch it in real time. The model is straightforward: the artist or promoter sets a ticket price, fans buy access, and revenue is split. This became more relevant during pandemic lockdowns when touring stopped but musicians still needed to earn. StageIt positions itself as one of the oldest and most established ticketed livestream platforms in the space.

**Soundstr** is the third piece. It is a cloud-based tool that uses AI and other technology to identify music playing in businesses — bars, restaurants, coffee shops, gyms, radio stations. When Soundstr identifies a song, the company can track who played it and ensure the right royalties flow to the rights holder. This is a data and licensing problem. A coffee shop plays thousands of songs a month; a restaurant plays even more. The performing-rights organisations that collect royalties from these venues need to know what is playing, where, and how often. Soundstr automates that identification.

## The problem VNUE is trying to solve

All three platforms touch a real problem in the music industry: artists struggle to capture value from their work, and the data around where music is consumed is fragmented and imperfect.

For recording artists and touring musicians, recorded music streams generate pennies. A service like Spotify might pay out a fraction of a cent per stream. For a musician to earn meaningful money from recordings, they need either massive streaming volume or direct relationships with fans who will buy. Set.fm tries to enable the direct relationship by letting fans buy recordings of shows they attended. StageIt does the same for livestreamed performances.

For music publishers and rights holders, tracking where music is played is a logistics nightmare. Millions of small businesses play music daily. The performing-rights organisations have been trying for decades to track usage and collect royalties. The system works, but it is imperfect and slow. Soundstr attempts to inject real-time data and automation into that process.

## How the company has evolved

VNUE operated as a music technology company focused on these platforms for years. Ownership changed hands, the company pursued and abandoned various strategic directions, and the platforms have seen varying degrees of adoption and revenue generation. Set.fm and StageIt have attracted users and some press attention; Soundstr positions itself as a serious tool for licensing automation.

In recent years, Redacted Optics assumed control of VNUE and signalled a strategic pivot toward advanced firearm optics, thermal imaging, and augmented glass solutions for defence, law enforcement, and commercial users. This shift reflects either a change in investor interest or a decision that the music technology platforms had not reached the scale or profitability needed to justify the company's resources. The name Redacted Industries and the new stated focus suggest that the company may exit or de-emphasise the music business entirely.

## For music creators and publishers

The value proposition of the VNUE platforms remains sound: direct fan monetisation, instant recordings, and better data on where music is played. Whether these specific platforms will ever be the main channel through which musicians and publishers earn money is uncertain. The music industry is highly concentrated — a few streaming services dominate, and the performing-rights organisations control most royalty collection. New platforms have to compete with established infrastructure that, though imperfect, is already in place.

## How to research VNUE / Redacted as an investment

The company's SEC filings (CIK 0001376804) detail the status of the music platforms and any strategic shifts. Watch for updates on user growth and revenue from Set.fm, StageIt, and Soundstr. If the company truly is pivoting away from music and into defence technology, the filings should disclose acquisitions, partnerships, or major contracts in that new space. The earnings calls or shareholder updates will clarify whether the company still views the music platforms as core to its business or whether it is exiting or looking to divest them.

For investors in early-stage technology platforms, unit economics matter more than total revenue: is the cost to acquire a user lower than the lifetime value of that user? Are retention rates improving or declining? Do the platforms have defensible advantages versus competitors? For VNUE, the answers to those questions are less visible from public filings than they would be from speaking directly to the company or to musicians and venues that use the platforms.
