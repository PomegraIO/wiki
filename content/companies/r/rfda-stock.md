RFDA is built on a two-tier screening methodology that separates it from plain [dividend](/dividend/) [index funds](/index-fund/). The first screen identifies US stocks with growing dividend histories and the financial strength to sustain them — a fundamental approach to finding durable dividend payers. The second screen applies technical momentum criteria, examining factors like price trend, volatility patterns, and recent performance to select from the qualified universe. The idea is simple: take a set of fundamentally sound dividend stocks, then tilt the portfolio toward those showing positive momentum or favorable technical setups.

This dual approach attempts to marry dividend income with price appreciation. A traditional dividend index fund holds all dividend-paying stocks that meet a threshold; RFDA holds a curated subset selected for both fundamental stability and technical attractiveness. In theory, this can lead to outperformance relative to a simple dividend index because the momentum tilt captures more of the gains from stocks that are already moving higher and have supportive technical patterns.

## The screening process

The fundamental tier filters for companies with histories of dividend growth, earnings stability, and balance-sheet health — the raw material of sustainable dividend payers. This eliminates high-yield traps: stocks yielding 8 or 10 percent that are paying out unsustainable distributions. The result is a narrower population than a simple high-dividend-yield screen would produce, focused on quality rather than pure yield.

From this qualified universe, the momentum overlay applies technical or price-based criteria. These might include positive price momentum (stocks that have outperformed the broader market in recent months), low volatility relative to the stock's category, or specific chart patterns that technical analysts associate with continued strength. The specific criteria are detailed in the fund's prospectus. The practical effect is that some dividend stocks are favored while others are underweighted or excluded, based on their technical characteristics.

The resulting portfolio is rebalanced periodically, typically on a quarterly basis. This prevents the portfolio from drifting too far away from the screened universe and allows the fund to adjust to changing momentum conditions.

## Returns: dividend income plus price appreciation

RFDA distributes dividends quarterly, capturing the income from the underlying stocks. Because the momentum overlay tends to favor stocks in uptrends, RFDA may also capture more of the capital appreciation than a static dividend index in certain markets. In [bull markets](/bull-market/) where the screened dividend stocks are also advancing, the combination of yield and capital appreciation can lead to solid total returns. In flat or down markets, the dividend provides a floor, but capital losses may still occur.

The [total expense ratio](/total-expense-ratio/) includes both the costs of the systematic screening and the quarterly rebalancing. This is higher than a simple passive dividend index but lower than most actively managed dividend funds, because the process is rules-based and automated rather than reliant on human stock-picker judgment.

## What can go wrong

The first risk is that momentum and fundamentals can diverge. A dividend stock with excellent fundamentals might fall temporarily out of favor, triggering underweighting or exclusion by the momentum screen. Conversely, a dividend stock riding a temporary wave of enthusiasm might be overweighted even though its fundamentals are mediocre. The fund's screening process cannot eliminate this timing risk; it can only bet that momentum tends to persist.

The second risk is that dividend stocks as a category can underperform the broader market for years. During extended periods of growth-stock dominance or when interest-rate cycles favor non-dividend-paying equities, a dividend-focused fund can trail a total-market index substantially. The momentum overlay helps but does not eliminate this sector-timing risk.

The third risk is concentration. Because the fund applies two layers of screening, the resulting portfolio may be more concentrated than a dividend index. If a particular dividend stock or sector becomes heavily represented in the qualified momentum set, RFDA's returns depend on that concentrated bet. The fund's prospectus discloses the largest holdings and sector breakdown; investors should check these to understand concentration.

The fourth risk is systematic screening failure. If the momentum criteria that the fund relies upon stop working — perhaps because market dynamics change or because the strategy becomes too crowded — the momentum overlay might actually detract from returns rather than enhance them. Rules-based systems can stop working, and there is no guarantee that past performance of the screening criteria will continue.

## Who RFDA suits

RFDA is designed for investors seeking current dividend income who are also willing to accept equity-market volatility and are interested in some capital appreciation along with the yield. It sits between a pure income strategy (like a covered-call fund focused solely on extracting premium) and a pure growth strategy. Retirees with a longer time horizon who can tolerate some drawdowns in exchange for the potential of both income and growth might find RFDA appealing. Investors with shorter horizons or low risk tolerance would be better served by simpler, more diversified approaches.

## How to evaluate RFDA

Begin with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, which detail the exact screening criteria and the current portfolio. Check the [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) against the dividend yield of a broad dividend index to understand whether RFDA is selecting higher-yield or lower-yield stocks on average. Review the fund's performance in different market environments: How has it done in years when dividend stocks outperformed? How has it done when dividend stocks lagged? This comparison reveals whether the momentum overlay has been a help or a drag in various conditions.

Compare RFDA's total return to a simple dividend index fund over a long period and to a total-market index. The sum of these comparisons shows whether the dual-screening approach has generated excess returns commensurate with its higher [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) and the additional complexity. Monitor the fund's largest holdings and sector allocation to ensure concentration is within comfortable bounds. Finally, review the fund's distribution history and any changes to the screening methodology, which the sponsor may adjust over time.
