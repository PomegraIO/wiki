> A closed-end fund is locked capital hunting for yield, managed by professionals who arbitrage the gap between net asset value and market price.

RiverNorth Capital & [Income Fund](/income-fund/) (RSF) is a closed-end fund — a pool of investor capital managed by RiverNorth Capital Advisors that buys equities, bonds, preferred shares, convertibles, and other securities with the goal of generating high income for its shareholders. Unlike an open-end mutual fund, which allows investors to buy and sell shares directly from the fund at [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) (the portfolio's assets divided by shares outstanding), a closed-end fund's shares trade on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) at prices set by the market. That distinction matters: the fund's net asset value and the market price of its shares can diverge significantly, creating either a premium (shares trading above NAV) or a discount (shares trading below NAV) that investors must account for.

The supply chain is investor capital flowing in (either at initial offering or through [secondary market](/secondary-market/) purchases), deployed by the fund manager into a diversified portfolio of securities, and then cash income (from dividends, interest, and capital gains distributions) flowing back to shareholders, typically monthly or quarterly. RiverNorth's job is to identify undervalued securities, manage [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/), and optimize the income-to-risk ratio of the portfolio. The upstream input is the pool of available securities across equity and debt markets; the downstream benefit to the shareholder is regular distributions, potential capital appreciation (if NAV rises), and exposure to professional management.

RiverNorth typically holds a mix of common equities (chosen for [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) or potential value appreciation), preferred shares (which pay fixed distributions), bonds and other fixed-income securities (chosen for yield), and sometimes convertible securities or illiquid assets that offer higher returns for accepting additional liquidity or complexity risk. The fund is allowed to use leverage — borrowing money to invest more capital than shareholders have contributed — which amplifies both returns and losses. This leverage is both a selling point (higher distributions are possible) and a risk (in market downturns, leverage can quickly erode NAV and force the fund to de-lever, crystallizing losses).

The fund's distributions are a critical draw for shareholders, but they must be understood carefully. A distribution to shareholders comes from three potential sources: interest and dividends earned on the portfolio, net capital gains (profit from selling securities at higher prices than purchase), or return of capital (shareholders receiving their own capital back). Return-of-capital distributions are economically equivalent to a tax-deferred withdrawal — they reduce NAV but may be marketed as "distribution" in ways that appear more generous than they are. RiverNorth discloses the composition of its distributions in [annual reports](/10-k/), but the details are easy to misread. A high distribution rate (the annualized payout divided by share price) can signal either attractive yield or an unsustainable payout that gradually erodes NAV.

RiverNorth's manager earns a fee (typically charged as a percentage of assets under management), which is paid from the fund's portfolio before distributions are made. Higher fees reduce distributions to shareholders dollar-for-dollar. RiverNorth's fees are visible on its annual report and prospectus but are a point of friction — they are a drag on returns compared to passive [index funds](/index-fund/), which charge much less. The justification is active management: the fund manager claims to select securities better than an index would, earn higher returns, or manage risk more effectively. Whether the fund's excess returns exceed its fees is the central empirical question and the basis on which active funds are evaluated.

The closed-end structure creates an interesting dynamic. If the market prices the fund at a discount to NAV, the fund is cheap relative to its underlying holdings — a patient investor who buys at a discount and waits for the discount to narrow can capture gains even if the underlying portfolio is flat. Conversely, a premium is expensive; buying shares at a premium means the market is betting the manager will outperform, or it reflects strong investor demand that pushes price above fundamental value. Fund premiums and discounts fluctuate based on investor sentiment, supply and demand for shares, and changes in market conditions. A fund that was trading at a 10 percent premium can drop to a 5 percent discount, and that spread affects total returns independently of what happens to the underlying portfolio.

Risk in a closed-end fund like RiverNorth spans several dimensions. Market risk: if the securities in the portfolio fall in value, NAV falls, and shareholders lose money. Leverage risk: if the fund has borrowed to invest, and markets fall sharply, the borrowed funds must be repaid even if the portfolio has declined, which forces selling at bad prices or de-leveraging in a panic. [Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/): if the fund holds bonds and rates rise, bond prices fall and NAV deteriorates; if the fund uses floating-rate leverage, rising rates increase borrowing costs and reduce distributions. Concentration risk: if the portfolio is overweight a particular sector or security that declines, losses are amplified. [Liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/): some [closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/) invest in less-liquid securities (such as illiquid alternatives or preferred shares with thin trading) that are harder to sell quickly without accepting large discounts. Management risk: if the fund manager underperforms or the team departs, the fund's value proposition weakens.

For investors evaluating RiverNorth, the starting point is the SEC filing (CIK 0001644771), which reports net asset value, market price, the premium or discount, the portfolio holdings, fees, and leverage levels. Compare the fund's total return (including distributions) to relevant benchmarks and to peer funds with similar strategies over multiple time periods (one year, three years, five years, and longer). Examine whether the fund has achieved its stated distribution targets and whether distributions are sustainable or eroding NAV over time. Watch the discount or premium to NAV — buying at a wide discount offers a margin of safety, while buying at a premium is inherently more speculative. Review the portfolio composition and the fund manager's track record across market cycles, especially in downturns when leverage and illiquid holdings can become dangerous. [Interest rates](/interest-rate/) affect both the fund's yields on its holdings and the cost of its leverage, so monitor the Fed's policy path. Closed-end funds are tools for income-seeking investors, not capital preservation; they are appropriate only for investors who understand leverage, are comfortable with distribution volatility, and can tolerate NAV fluctuations. The fund's value depends on both the quality of its manager and on market sentiment, making it a more complex investment than owning the underlying securities directly.
