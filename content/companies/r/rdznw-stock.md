## What is Roadzen and what problem does it solve?

Roadzen is a full-stack insurance technology company building what it calls the operating system for insurance and mobility. The company's core mission is to transform how insurers make money: instead of pure risk selection (picking customers to insure), Roadzen helps insurers prevent accidents from happening, automate the claims process when they do, and price policies based on real-world driving behavior rather than static attributes like age and zip code. It sells to major insurers, carmakers, commercial fleets, and third-party administrators—over 112 enterprise customers and 3,800 smaller businesses across the United States, United Kingdom, India, and Europe.

The unit economics angle: a dollar of insurance revenue historically came from charging customers a premium that was supposed to exceed the cost of claims paid out, plus administrative overhead and profit. The gap between premium and cost determines the insurer's margin. Roadzen enters the equation by reducing the cost side—fewer accidents mean fewer claims. It also improves the pricing side by offering granular, real-time underwriting data so insurers can charge higher premiums to riskier drivers and lower premiums to safer ones, reducing adverse selection.

## How does Roadzen actually make money?

Roadzen operates two overlapping revenue streams. The first is technology licensing: it sells its Insurance as a Service platform (IaaS) to insurers and carriers, who embed Roadzen's AI models and telematics capabilities into their own underwriting, distribution, and claims workflows. Insurers pay subscription fees for access to the platform, measured in seats, policies underwritten, or claims processed.

The second revenue stream is insurance distribution itself. Roadzen is a Managing General Agent (MGA)—it can write insurance on behalf of licensed carriers. When Roadzen uses its platform to underwrite and distribute an auto policy through a carrier partner, it earns a commission as a percentage of the premium. A customer might think they are buying insurance from a major carrier, but Roadzen packaged and underwrote the deal. Those commissions are the MGA revenue. Importantly, Roadzen converts underwriting capacity (the carrier's willingness to write a certain volume of policies at certain prices) directly into recurring revenue, because the MGA relationship creates an ongoing distribution channel.

The mix of licensing and MGA revenue gives Roadzen two ways to scale. A large carrier buying the platform pays upfront software fees with potential for high gross margins (80%+). A smaller carrier or a fleet using Roadzen's MGA capabilities shares the policy premium with Roadzen, yielding lower per-unit margins but higher transaction volume. As of mid-2026, Roadzen secured a [letter of intent](/letter-of-intent-ma/) for $30 million in insurance capacity from a major U.S. auto insurer, with over $50 million in additional producer demand signaled, indicating the MGA opportunity was expanding.

## What gives Roadzen competitive advantage?

Roadzen's moat is its dataset and the models trained on it. The company has accumulated over 3.5 billion kilometers of real-world driving data. From that, it built DrivebuddyAI, a driver monitoring system that observes in-cabin video and telematics to detect distraction, fatigue, risky behavior, and predict accident likelihood. DrivebuddyAI is claimed to reduce accident rates by over 70% in validated studies and is the only in-cabin monitoring system certified under both the EU's GSR 2144 standard and India's AIS-184 standard—a regulatory milestone no competitor has yet matched.

The competitive advantage flows from two sources: (1) the dataset and the predictive models trained on it become more accurate the larger they grow, creating network effects; and (2) regulatory approval in multiple jurisdictions creates switching costs—carriers already using an approved system have less reason to rip it out and replace it than to expand usage of what they know works.

Roadzen also benefits from distribution breadth. By servicing insurers, carmakers (selling DrivebuddyAI as an embedded safety feature), and fleet operators simultaneously, it can amortize the cost of platform development across multiple customer types. A single insurer might use the claims automation piece; a carmaker might use the in-vehicle driver monitoring; a fleet might use the telematics data and analytics. This bundling reduces customer [acquisition](/acquisition/) cost and increases lifetime value.

## What are the risks and pressures?

The insurance industry is highly regulated, and regulators take a conservative view of algorithmic underwriting and pricing. Roadzen's models make decisions about who should pay more and who less for insurance, which raises fairness and discrimination concerns. If regulators conclude that Roadzen's models encode bias against protected classes (age, gender, geography), the company could face product restrictions, fines, or forced retraining. This regulatory risk is particularly acute as the company expands into regulated markets like Europe and India.

Roadzen also faces competitive pressure from two directions. Large insurance carriers have begun investing in their own AI underwriting capabilities; they might use Roadzen initially, then build in-house versions and disintermediate the vendor. Secondly, specialized competitors are emerging in specific niches—computer vision for claims (to automate damage assessment), telematics alone (not bundled with underwriting), or agent-based customer engagement. Roadzen's full-stack bet assumes insurers value integration, but if they value best-of-breed specialists more, Roadzen's bundling advantage erodes.

The MGA revenue model also carries a risk: if a carrier partner decides the policies Roadzen is writing for them are unprofitable, they can terminate the relationship with minimal notice, cutting off a significant revenue stream. The company is working to diversify its carrier partners to reduce [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/), but the model remains carrier-dependent.

## How should an investor research Roadzen?

Start with Roadzen's annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001868640) and quarterly 10-Q reports. The key data points are the mix of IaaS revenue versus MGA commissions, the gross margin for each segment, the count of enterprise customers, and the trajectory of the installed base (policies underwritten, kilometers of driving data). Watch for commentary on regulatory challenges and new certifications (like the EU and India driver monitoring approvals, which validate the technology and open markets).

Earnings calls should focus on customer wins and losses, carrier capacity agreements (LOIs and actual contracts), gross margin trends as the company scales, and any changes to the regulatory environment affecting algorithmic underwriting. Compare Roadzen's accident reduction claims to published studies from other companies; validate that the numbers are independently verified, not just claimed.

The business fundamentals depend on whether insurers and carmakers genuinely value the risk reduction and cost savings Roadzen claims to deliver. If the company's technology meaningfully improves underwriting profitability, it will grow. If its models are merely nice-to-have or if regulators constrain their use, growth will slow.
