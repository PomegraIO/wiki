*The **Reckoner BBB-B CLO Annual ETF** (RCLY) is an exchange-traded fund that invests in [collateralized loan obligations](/collateralized-loan-obligation/), which are securitized portfolios of leveraged corporate loans bundled and tranched by credit quality. The fund targets the middle rungs of CLO capital structures — the BBB and B-rated [tranches](/tranche/) that sit between the safer senior notes and the riskier equity pieces — seeking income from interest payments on underlying loans.*

CLOs are a core part of the [structured finance](/structured-finance/) world, yet most retail investors encounter them only indirectly, embedded in [bond](/bond/) funds or pension holdings. RCLY makes them a direct, transparent holding. To understand the fund, it helps to start with what sits underneath: the leveraged loans it ultimately backs.

## The leveraged-loan market and CLO anatomy

Collateralized loan obligations exist because banks have long made large loans to companies at floating [interest rates](/interest-rate/) — loans secured by borrower assets but junior to trade creditors. A bank originates a $500 million loan to a mid-market company, then sells it to a structured-finance sponsor. That sponsor gathers hundreds of such loans into a pool worth several billion dollars, analyzes the [diversification](/diversification/) and default risk, then carves the pool into tranches. The most senior tranche might contain $2 billion in principal, rated AAA, and paid first when loans default. Below that sit mezzanine tranches (BBB, BB, B, CCC) that absorb losses in order, and at the bottom sits an unrated equity piece that takes whatever is left. The interest income from the underlying loans gets distributed up the stack: senior bondholders get the coupon first, then mezzanine investors capture the spread above the senior rate, and equity holders collect whatever remains after all senior claims are paid. This layering is the core insight of [securitization](/securitization/) — by slicing risk and return separately, you can sell securities to investors with different risk appetites and fund loans that might otherwise go unmade.

RCLY focuses on the mid-tier tranches, specifically the BBB and B ratings. These sit where the economics of CLOs are most visible to investors: they carry meaningful coupons (typically 5–7% or higher, depending on the loan market and spreads), they absorb real default losses if the underlying loan pool sours, and they trade with enough width that a fund strategy based on them is actually viable.

## How the fund invests and trades

RCLY is structured as a straightforward exchange-traded fund, not an ETN or leveraged product. It holds a portfolio of individual CLO tranches — securities issued by different CLO issuers, each tranche backed by its own diversified loan pool. The fund rebalances periodically to maintain exposure across a range of CLO vintages and issuers, spreading [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/). Because CLOs are fixed-income instruments that pay quarterly or semi-annual coupons, RCLY distributes income to shareholders regularly, though the distribution yield and frequency can shift as underlying tranches are called (early paid off) or default.

The fund itself is liquid and trades on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) at prices that fluctuate based on [credit spreads](/credit-spread/), interest rates, and the market's assessment of leveraged-loan portfolio health. When the leveraged-loan market is healthy and spreads are tight, CLO tranches appreciate. When credit concerns rise or the [central bank](/central-bank/) raises rates, CLO prices often fall — they are credit instruments, not duration plays, so they react most sharply to default risk rather than rate moves, though higher rates do affect the coupons on the underlying floating-rate loans.

## The trade-off: yield for credit risk

RCLY's appeal lies in its income generation. A retail investor who buys the fund receives exposure to coupons that often exceed those on investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) or intermediateterm Treasuries. That higher yield comes with explicit tradeoffs. First, CLO tranches are less liquid than publicly traded bonds — the underlying tranches are institutional holdings, and while RCLY itself is ETF-liquid, the fund's ability to buy and sell the tranches it holds can narrow or widen depending on market conditions. Second, BBB and B tranches carry genuine [credit risk](/credit-risk/): they absorb losses if more than a handful of the underlying loans default. A [recession](/recession/) that pushes [default rates](/default-rate/) on leveraged loans from 1–2% up to 5–7% can inflict real losses on mezzanine tranches, especially those at the B end of the rating spectrum. Third, CLOs are complex securities, and the true risk profile depends on factors an investor cannot always observe directly — the composition of each underlying loan pool, the quality of the loans' underwriting, the covenant structures, and the experience of the CLO manager in steering the portfolio.

## When CLOs perform and when they falter

CLOs were born in the boom of the 1990s, nearly died in the financial crisis of 2008 (when default rates on leveraged loans spiked and many CLO tranches took losses), and have thrived ever since in the low-rate, easy-credit environments of 2012–2022. During those years, loan defaults were rare, spreads on CLO tranches compressed, and investors who bought and held BBB and B tranches captured strong total returns from coupons plus price appreciation. The trade worked.

What shifts that narrative is a [credit cycle](/credit-cycle/). Leveraged loans are made in good times, often with light covenants and aggressive leverage multiples. When the economy slows, loan defaults rise, prepayments fall (companies stop being bought or refinanced), and CLO tranches that were trading at par begin to trade at discounts. The BBB and B pieces are where most of that loss flows. Investors who bought RCLY expecting 6% annual yield might instead see the share price fall 10–15% if the underlying loan pool deteriorates.

## What to watch: riding the credit cycle

A reader considering RCLY should understand the state of the leveraged-loan market and CLO spreads. During periods of credit stress (rising unemployment, falling corporate earnings, central-bank tightening), CLO tranches are less attractive — prices fall and future returns depend on catching the rebound. During periods of credit strength (low unemployment, stable earnings, accommodative policy), CLOs compress, prices rise, and the fund's appeal rests more on the coupon than on capital appreciation. Historical performance is no guide to future returns; the fund's results turn almost entirely on whether the leveraged-loan market avoids recession and defaults stay low.

SEC filings for RCLY include regular fact sheets that disclose the composition of the portfolio, the weighted-average coupon, and the [effective duration](/effective-duration/) or duration-like metrics. The prospectus details the fee structure and the risks management sees as paramount. A serious investor would also track the leveraged-loan default rate (published by Moody's and S&P) and the trends in new issuance, covenant standards, and loan-to-earnings ratios — the fundamental health indicators of the market the fund depends on.
