The Emerging Markets Equity Select ETF (RNEM) is built on a recognition that emerging markets are not a single asset class but a spectrum, and the difference between a company likely to deliver returns and one likely to destroy them can be stark. The fund packages stocks from developing economies across Asia, Latin America, and Eastern Europe into a single exchange-traded structure, but it does not buy everything — it applies a selection filter designed to tilt the portfolio toward companies with stronger [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/), higher profitability, and better liquidity than the emerging-market universe at large.

For a US-based investor, emerging-market exposure is appealing at first glance: these economies grow faster than the developed world, their labour forces are younger, and their consumer bases are expanding. In theory, faster economic growth should eventually translate to equity returns. The catch is that emerging markets ship volatility along with that growth. Currency movements can dwarf stock-price movements; a Brazilian real that loses half its value against the dollar makes every real-denominated gain evaporate. Political risk is higher — coups, sudden policy reversals, capital controls can arrive without warning. Corporate governance and accounting standards are often weaker, creating space for outright fraud or mismanagement to survive longer before being caught.

RNEM's selection approach tries to mitigate these dangers by being choosy. Rather than buying the 1,000 largest emerging-market companies by market capitalisation, the fund screens for metrics like [return on equity](/return-on-equity/), [debt-to-equity ratio](/debt-to-equity-ratio/), and recent [earnings quality](/earnings-quality/). The result is a narrower list of names — typically a few hundred rather than thousands — that are less likely to implode in a downturn. This is a meaningful difference: a broad emerging-market index would load heavily into state-owned banks with murky accounting and mature commodity companies in volatile countries. RNEM excludes more of those names in favour of companies with clearer financial profiles.

The fund structure itself is straightforward. RNEM is a standard ETF, not leveraged or inverse, and it trades throughout the day like a stock rather than settling at a single daily price like a [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/). That intraday trading liquidity is convenient, but investors should remember that it reflects the underlying liquidity of the securities inside. During stress periods when emerging-market sell pressure hits, spreads on RNEM can widen, and large blocks may execute at worse-than-midpoint prices. The fund is only as liquid as its deepest holdings allow.

The two risks that make emerging markets structurally different from developed ones persist no matter how good the selection filter is. Currency weakness in an emerging-market economy can happen to healthy companies as much as weak ones — if India faces a balance-of-payments crisis and the rupee collapses, even the most profitable Indian manufacturer loses half its value for a dollar investor. Similarly, systemic political or regulatory shocks can ripple through the entire market. China's tightening of the tech sector in 2020–2021 hit even the best-governed internet companies. RNEM's filters address individual-company risk, not systemic [market risk](/market-risk/).

Concentration is a secondary concern. Emerging markets by weight are heavily skewed toward China and India, and even within those countries toward a handful of mega-cap names in financials, consumer goods, and energy. The quality screen cannot escape that geography; it just tilts within it toward the stronger players. An investor holding RNEM is not as diversified away from Chinese policy as they might imagine.

For someone building a long-term global portfolio, RNEM offers one approach to emerging-market exposure: disciplined selection over broad passivity. If you believe the quality screen identifies companies less likely to crater, and if you can tolerate [currency volatility](/currency-volatility/) and geopolitical risk, the fund provides a reasonable vehicle. If you prefer lower costs and maximum simplicity, an unfiltered emerging-market [index fund](/index-fund/) would be cheaper, though wider in scope. Either way, the [fund prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) and the underlying index methodology should be your starting points — they reveal exactly what criteria trigger inclusion and what the true cost of trading that selection strategy really is.
