Consumer staples are companies whose products people buy regardless of economic conditions. Grocery stores and food manufacturers, beer and soft-drink makers, household cleaners, toothpaste, paper products, razor blades, and personal hygiene items are the backbone of the category. These are non-discretionary purchases: [recessions](/recession/) do not eliminate the need to eat or bathe. This predictability of demand has made staples the preferred refuge for conservative investors during market downturns — a reputation earned over decades. When equity markets fall, staples often fall less, or sometimes not at all, because investors seeking safety redirect capital toward proven, recurring revenue streams.

RSPS captures this entire sector in equal proportions. In the typical market-cap-weighted version of a staples index, the largest companies — [Procter & Gamble](/pg-stock/), Nestlé, Coca-Cola, Pepsi, and a handful of others — would dominate the weighting. Equal weighting dismantles that concentration and elevates smaller regional and specialized staples makers to parity. This has two major consequences: (1) the fund captures more idiosyncratic company-specific risk and opportunity from smaller names that grow faster or stumble harder than the giants, and (2) it automatically creates a [rebalancing discipline](/rebalancing-discipline/) that sells winners as they appreciate and buys laggards as they underperform, which is a contrarian mechanic that can pay off when valuations mean-revert.

## Why staples generate dividends and stable income

Staples businesses have strong, recurring cash flows. A consumer buying a box of cereal or a tube of toothpaste every month provides predictable, stable revenue. Companies in this sector typically convert that revenue into substantial operating cash flow, which they return to shareholders as [dividends](/dividend/). Many staples stocks yield 2% to 4%, meaningfully above the broad [stock market's](/stock-market/) 1% to 2% average. This high dividend is not a choice but an economic reality: staples companies operate with modest growth prospects (mature markets, commoditized products), so reinvesting cash into the business offers low returns. Returning cash as dividends is the economically rational move. RSPS's portfolio will therefore generate a higher-than-market stream of cash distributions, making it attractive to income investors or those who value compounding through reinvested dividends.

The downside is that high dividends are taxed as ordinary income (or [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/)) in taxable accounts, making RSPS less tax-efficient than a growth-oriented stock fund. For tax-deferred retirement accounts, this is immaterial; for taxable portfolios, the dividend drag is worth accounting for.

## The economic moat of brand and distribution

Staples companies survive through brand power and distribution relationships. Procter & Gamble does not dominate personal care because its toothpaste is scientifically superior; it dominates because shoppers reach for the familiar green tube, retailers stock it because it moves, and that habit is brutally hard to displace. Nestlé's portfolio of beloved brands — from coffee to infant formula to pet food — creates switching costs that allow pricing power. Coca-Cola's bottling network, relationships with retailers, and century-old marketing give it an economic moat.

This moat insulates the largest staples companies from disruption. For decades, observers have predicted that private-label or discount brands would steal share from premium names. Disruption has happened at the margins — discounters have grown market share — but the largest staples makers have retained their profitability and pricing power. Equal weighting captures both the giants with deep moats and smaller, more vulnerable staples makers, so the portfolio-level moat is thinner than holding only the Procter & Gambles of the world, but broader exposure also means more opportunities to discover emerging or specialized staples businesses that grow faster.

## Defensive characteristics and market cycles

During equity market downturns, staples typically outperform because investors flee to safety. This defensive quality is real and has been demonstrated in multiple recessions. However, the defensive reputation has become self-fulfilling: during upswings, when risk appetite is high and investors chase growth, staples underperform because they lack the explosive upside of technology or [discretionary spending](/discretionary-spending/) stories. A portfolio concentrated in staples will provide safety in downturns but is likely to lag in roaring [bull markets](/bull-market/). An investor in RSPS is making a conscious trade-off: accept lower returns during bull markets to capture downside protection during [bear markets](/bear-market/).

## The specific challenge of equal weighting in a mature sector

Consumer staples do not grow quickly. Revenue growth for mature staples businesses typically runs 2% to 4% annually — roughly aligned with [inflation](/inflation/) and GDP growth. Earnings growth is comparable or slightly higher because of operating leverage and cost discipline. This slow-growth environment means that a staples fund's returns will hinge heavily on dividends: total return is mostly the yield, plus or minus valuation-multiple expansion or contraction. Equal weighting adds another wrinkle: as the portfolio rebalances, it systematically trims back companies whose products are succeeding and buys more of companies whose products are struggling. This can be wise in a mean-reverting environment, but it can also lock in losses if underperforming companies continue to underperform.

## Practical considerations for using RSPS

RSPS is best suited for investors seeking dividend income and downside protection over growth, or those who believe that equal weighting will outperform market-cap weighting in the staples sector because smaller staples companies will grow faster than the giants. For general equity exposure, the fund is too narrow and lacks the growth potential that makes stock investing attractive over [bonds](/bond/).

Understand the fund's rebalancing schedule and the tax implications. If held in a taxable account, ask whether the dividend stream and capital-gains drag align with your tax planning. If held in a retirement account, the tax drag is irrelevant. Compare RSPS's five-year and ten-year returns against both a market-cap-weighted staples index and a broad equity index; this comparison reveals whether equal weighting is adding value or subtracting it. In recent years, equal-weight strategies have generally underperformed because large companies have compounded faster than small ones, but this dynamism can reverse. Monitor the portfolio's largest holdings in the prospectus; if every position is truly equal, the fund is working as intended; if a few positions dominate, the methodology has drifted.
