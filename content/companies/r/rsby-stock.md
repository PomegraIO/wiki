*Return Stacked [Bonds](/bond/) & Futures Yield ETF (RSBY) merges core bond holdings—selected for their coupon income—with systematic futures positions designed to add return layers, targeting total yield across fixed income and trend-following allocations.*

## What makes this fund different from broader alternatives?

Most bond funds chase yield by reaching for [credit risk](/credit-risk/)—buying lower-rated corporate debt, [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/), or emerging-market issues. RSBY takes a different path. It builds yield by starting with [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/) (lower credit risk) and then adding a second income stream through [managed futures](/managed-futures/). This approach appeals to investors who want the coupon income of bonds but also want exposure to the patterns and volatility that trend-following strategies can capitalize on, particularly during inflationary periods when bond yields are under pressure.

## How does the yield actually get generated?

The bond portion generates the predictable piece: [coupon payments](/coupon-payment/) from the underlying securities. The managed futures overlay adds return—sometimes positive, sometimes neutral, sometimes negative—by trading futures across commodities, currencies, and equity indices. In strong trending markets (a commodity rally, a currency move, a broad equity uptrend), the futures positions can contribute gains that supplement the bond coupons. In choppy or sideways markets, the futures positions may tread water and the fund's return depends almost entirely on the bonds. The total yield—the actual cash the fund can distribute to shareholders—comes from both sources.

## Why would an investor choose this over a standard bond fund?

A conventional bond fund's yield is capped by the [yield curve](/yield-curve/). If investment-grade bonds are paying 4 percent, that is roughly what the fund will yield, minus fees. RSBY does not try to outrun the bond yield by taking higher credit risk; instead, it adds a parallel income stream. The managed futures piece is not supposed to replace bond returns but to supplement them. It is a way to get both the anchor of fixed income and the [diversification](/diversification/) boost of systematic trend following in one compact wrapper.

## How stable is the distribution?

Bond yields are relatively stable quarter to quarter—companies pay their coupons on schedule. Managed futures returns are more lumpy. In trending months or years, the futures positions add to the fund's total return and support distributions. In flat, choppy markets, they may contribute little or occasionally detract. This means RSBY's distribution is likely less predictable than a pure bond fund's would be, but it also means the fund's total return potential is higher if trend-following patterns play out. Investors relying on RSBY for stable monthly income should understand that the distribution may vary.

## How would you research this fund?

Start with the prospectus and the fund's fact sheet, which detail the bond allocation (maturity, [credit ratings](/credit-rating/), duration) and explain the managed futures methodology and [position sizing](/position-sizing/) limits. Look at the fund's distribution history—not just the yield, but how much of it came from interest income versus capital gains or return of principal. Compare the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) to similar actively managed alternatives. Watch the fund's performance in rising-rate environments, where bond holdings typically underperform but managed futures often excel, and in bull-market rallies, where the bond drag may dampen total returns.
