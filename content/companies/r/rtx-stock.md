RTX Corp is one of the largest defense contractors in the United States, headquartered in Arlington, Virginia. The company manufactures engines for military aircraft, missiles, air-defense systems, radar and intelligence equipment, and a wide range of defense electronics and software. RTX shares trade on NYSE under the ticker RTX. The company's roots are in two separate legacy firms — Raytheon (a defense electronics and missile company) and United Technologies (a diversified manufacturer with a major aerospace engines division called Pratt & Whitney) — which merged in 2020 to form Raytheon Technologies, later rebranded as RTX.

## What business is RTX actually in, and why does it matter?

RTX has three main divisions. Pratt & Whitney makes jet engines for military and commercial aircraft. Raytheon Intelligence & Space designs and manufactures radar, missile warning systems, satellite communications, and sensor systems for the military. Raytheon Missiles & Defense builds missiles, rocket systems, air-defense systems, and precision-guided munitions. The company sells to the U.S. Department of Defense, to allied militaries around the world, and to commercial airlines that buy Pratt & Whitney engines. RTX's business is essential to U.S. and allied military capability, and the company has few true peers in scale and technological breadth.

The U.S. military is RTX's largest customer, directly or indirectly. Every F-15 and F-22 fighter jet flying today has a Pratt & Whitney engine. Every Tomahawk cruise missile fired has guidance and control systems that come from RTX technology. Many of the radar systems that track threats, many of the air-defense missiles, much of the electronic warfare equipment — it is built by RTX or contains RTX components. This dominance in military equipment is not accidental. It reflects decades of investment in research, development, and manufacturing expertise, and it gives RTX substantial competitive moats.

## How does RTX make money from the defense business?

The U.S. military operates under a contracting system where defense contractors bid on projects, negotiate prices with the government, and then manufacture and deliver equipment under contract. Contracts can be fixed-price (where the contractor absorbs cost overruns) or cost-plus (where the government reimburses costs plus an agreed-upon profit percentage). Most large military programs are structured as long-term development and production contracts, meaning RTX signs a deal today to deliver equipment over the next five, ten, or more years. This creates multi-year revenue visibility.

A modern military aircraft or missile system is enormously complex, and development costs billions of dollars. Once a program is under way, switching to a different supplier is extremely difficult — the government would have to restart development with someone else, which costs time and money. This gives RTX a kind of lock-in advantage. Once a major system enters production, RTX has a long-term revenue stream almost regardless of changes in politics or budget pressures, because discontinuing the program would cost the government more than continuing it.

RTX's gross margins on defense contracts are typically in the mid-twenty to low-thirty percent range — lower than software companies or some consumer goods, but solid for a manufacturing business. The company operates under government audit and regulation, with strict accounting rules. But the stable, multi-year nature of defense contracts means RTX can plan capital spending and workforce levels with confidence.

## What role does Pratt & Whitney play, and why is it strategically important?

Pratt & Whitney is one of the world's leading jet engine manufacturers. The company makes engines for military aircraft (F-15, F-22, F-35, and various helicopters), and it also makes engines for commercial airlines — [Boeing](/ba-stock/) and Airbus both use Pratt & Whitney engines. The company is one of only a handful of firms globally capable of manufacturing large turbine engines for military applications.

Military engines are technically demanding. They must operate at extreme temperatures and speeds, respond instantly to pilot inputs, and be reliable under combat conditions. Developing a new military engine takes years and costs billions. Once in production, an engine program generates revenue for decades. The F-35 fighter jet will be in production for twenty, thirty, or more years, and the engines powering it will generate revenue for Pratt & Whitney for that entire period.

The commercial side of Pratt & Whitney — engines for Boeing and Airbus aircraft — was historically important to the company and provided [diversification](/diversification/) away from defense. But commercial aviation is cyclical and more competitive than military. In recent years, Pratt & Whitney has also faced serious operational challenges with some of its engines, including gearbox failures and durability issues that have grounded aircraft and forced recalls. These problems have been costly to address and have damaged the company's reputation in the commercial sector. Fixing these issues is a strategic priority, as success in commercial aviation is important to RTX's financial performance and growth prospects.

## What about the intelligence and missiles divisions?

Raytheon Intelligence & Space develops and manufactures radar systems, missile warning systems, electronic warfare systems, and satellite communications equipment. These are essential to military operations worldwide. The company's products help detect threats, enable secure communications, and provide intelligence. Like military aircraft and engines, these systems are subject to long-term government contracts, and the customer base is primarily foreign governments and allied nations.

Raytheon Missiles & Defense manufactures missiles and air-defense systems, including the Patriot air-defense missile system (which has been used in numerous conflicts and is in production globally), the Tomahawk cruise missile, and various guided munitions. Again, these are long-term production contracts with recurring orders as militaries maintain and upgrade their arsenals. The Patriot system, in particular, is used by more than a dozen countries and has generated decades of revenue from initial development through ongoing production and upgrades.

## What are the key risks and uncertainties?

One risk is U.S. federal budget constraints. The Pentagon has a fixed budget, and if Congress cuts defense spending or prioritizes different programs, RTX's revenue could suffer. This is more of a long-term concern than a short-term one, but shifts in national priorities or fiscal crises could reduce defense spending. That said, the U.S. defense budget tends to be sticky — even when overall federal spending is constrained, defense spending often holds up reasonably well politically.

A second risk is international competition and arms-control decisions. If the U.S. tightens restrictions on military equipment exports, if international tensions ease and global military spending declines, or if foreign competitors develop superior systems at lower cost, RTX's business could be affected. This is less likely for engines and missiles, where barriers to entry are high, but is a real possibility for electronics and software systems.

The quality and delivery risks are significant. Defense programs are often on fixed budgets, and cost overruns or schedule delays directly reduce profitability. RTX has had troubled programs in the past that overran budgets, and the company must manage these risks carefully. The recent issues with Pratt & Whitney commercial engines show the consequences of quality problems — they are expensive to fix and damage the company's reputation.

Supply chain complexity is another risk. RTX operates a vast supply network involving thousands of suppliers, and disruptions — whether from geopolitical tensions, natural disasters, or manufacturing problems — can delay deliveries and increase costs. The semiconductors used in RTX's defense systems are also subject to supply constraints, as are certain materials and components.

Finally, there is geopolitical risk. Russia's invasion of Ukraine, tensions with China over Taiwan, and other global instabilities affect the demand for defense equipment and can also disrupt supply chains. A major war or conflict could dramatically increase demand for RTX products, but it would also introduce [operational risks](/operational-risk/) and potentially require rapid scaling of production.

## How should someone research RTX as an investment?

RTX's annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0000101829) provides detailed breakdowns of revenue and profit by division, customer concentration data, and descriptions of major programs and contracts. The risk disclosures describe the factors RTX identifies as threatening the business. Quarterly earnings calls give management's commentary on program health, contract wins and losses, and the geopolitical landscape affecting demand.

Important metrics include the backlog of unfilled customer orders — a large backlog indicates strong future revenue visibility. The gross and [operating margins](/operating-margin/) by division show which businesses are most profitable and how profitability is trending. RTX's capital spending and research and development investments show how much the company is investing in future products. The quality of major programs (schedule performance, cost performance) indicates whether RTX is executing well or struggling.

Investors should monitor commentary about the F-35 fighter jet and other major U.S. military programs, as well as international military spending trends. Commercial aviation recovery is also important for Pratt & Whitney's commercial engine business. And the success of the company's efforts to resolve quality issues in its commercial engine portfolio is critical to RTX's long-term profitability.

RTX is a mature, essential provider of military and aerospace technology. It is not a growth company, but rather a stable cash generator with multi-year contract visibility. As with any individual security, RTX's shares trade on a public exchange, and nothing here is a recommendation — only a guide to understanding the business and its key dynamics.
