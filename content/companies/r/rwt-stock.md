Redwood Trust is one of the largest independent mortgage originators and securitizers in the United States, operating as a [real estate investment trust](/real-estate-investment-trust/). The company buys, originates, and manages residential mortgages, then packages them into mortgage-backed securities for sale to investors. Rather than holding all the mortgages on its [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) as a traditional lender would, Redwood systematically originates and sells them, earning fees and spreads on the process while keeping some mortgages and securities for portfolio income. As a REIT, the company is required to distribute at least 90 percent of its taxable income to shareholders annually, which makes it attractive to income investors.

The mortgage market is bifurcated in the United States. On one side are agency mortgages — loans that carry an implicit or explicit government guarantee, typically through [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/), [Freddie Mac](/freddie-mac/), or Ginnie Mae. These mortgages are easier to sell and securitize because the guarantee removes [credit risk](/credit-risk/). On the other side are non-agency mortgages — conventional loans to borrowers who don't qualify for agency programs, either because of income, property type, or other factors. Non-agency mortgages offer higher yields but carry credit risk; if a borrower defaults, the investor (not the government) bears the loss. Redwood operates on both sides, though the company has historically emphasized non-agency origination and [securitization](/securitization/), where its expertise and underwriting standards command a premium.

The business model turns on three core processes. First, origination: the company sources mortgages from [brokers](/broker/) and retail channels, underwrites them according to its own credit standards, and closes them. Second, securitization: Redwood pools these mortgages into securities and sells them to institutional investors — insurance companies, pension funds, [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) — often retaining a portion of the securities or cash flow for itself. Third, portfolio management: the company holds selected mortgages and securities on its balance sheet for the longer-term cash flow and the spread between the cost of funding and the yield on the mortgages.

Income comes from multiple places: origination fees (typically 0.5 to 1 percent of loan amount), the spread between the price at which mortgages are sold and the cost to originate them, the net interest margin on mortgages and securities held in portfolio, and prepayment penalties or other ancillary fees. Most of these sources are fee-based or spread-based and involve relatively low capital commitment once a mortgage is sold and securitized. The exception is the portfolio — mortgages and securities held for income consume capital and tie up cash flow, but they also provide the steady income stream that supports the REIT distributions.

Redwood's performance is extremely sensitive to [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and the housing market. When mortgage rates fall, borrowers refinance, paying off existing mortgages early, which truncates the expected cash flow to investors. Redwood loses income it was counting on from those mortgages. Conversely, when rates rise, refinancings slow and mortgages stick around longer, which can be good for income. But rising rates also damage the mark-to-market value of existing mortgages and securities on the balance sheet, because a mortgage originated at 3 percent is worth less when new mortgages are being offered at 6 percent. Additionally, rising rates increase default risk in some borrower segments because higher rates make payments less affordable.

Housing demand is another major driver. When home prices rise and borrower confidence is high, [default rates](/default-rate/) are low and origination volume can surge. When housing weakens, borrowers are at higher risk of default and origination volume shrinks. The company's underwriting standards and its ability to manage the credit risk on non-agency mortgages are thus critical. A company with poor underwriting can face unexpected losses when the housing market turns; a company with disciplined standards can weather cycles.

Redwood's balance sheet is levered — the company borrows money to fund mortgages and purchases securities. That leverage magnifies returns when things go well but also magnifies losses when credit losses or mark-to-market declines occur. The company must maintain adequate capital buffers and fund the balance sheet efficiently. Access to funding markets is therefore critical; if repo markets seize or if investors become unwilling to fund the company's assets, Redwood could face a liquidity squeeze.

The regulatory environment matters too. Mortgage origination and servicing are heavily regulated, and changes to lending standards or consumer protections can shift the cost of originating mortgages. The REIT structure itself is under periodic scrutiny; if rules around REIT distributions or capital allocation were to change, it would affect the company's tax efficiency and appeal to shareholders.

Redwood's competitive position rests on its scale, its reputation for sound underwriting (particularly on non-agency loans), its access to capital markets, and its operational efficiency. The company is one of the few independent mortgage companies that can originate, securitize, and hold mortgages at scale. That capability is valuable but not unique; larger banks and other [mortgage REITs](/mortgage-reit/) offer competing services.

For investors, Redwood is fundamentally a leveraged bet on housing and interest rates. The distributions are attractive relative to risk-free rates, but they are not guaranteed and can be cut if losses or market conditions warrant. The company's quarterly earnings, the composition of the mortgage portfolio, prepayment speeds, default rates, and interest-rate-sensitive positioning are the critical data points to watch. The 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0000930236) discloses the portfolio in detail, and quarterly earnings calls often provide commentary on market conditions and management's outlook.

The founder-driven culture that shaped Redwood in its early years as an independent non-agency specialist has evolved, but the company's discipline on credit and its focus on mortgage origination and securitization remain core. However, the business is cyclical and capital-intensive, and shareholders should understand that Redwood's returns will vary sharply depending on housing conditions, interest rates, and the credit health of its mortgage portfolio.
