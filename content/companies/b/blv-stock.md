The Vanguard Long-Term Bond ETF is a flagship fixed-income exchange-traded fund that tracks a broad index of long-dated U.S. bonds — Treasuries with 10+ year maturities and investment-grade corporate debt. It exists to give individual investors simple, low-cost exposure to the long end of the bond curve, where price volatility is highest but yield is typically most rewarding relative to shorter-term alternatives.

## What BLV holds and why the maturity matters

BLV owns thousands of individual bonds across two categories. The Treasury portion comprises U.S. government securities issued across the long end of the yield curve — predominantly bonds with 10, 20, and 30-year maturities. The corporate portion holds bonds from investment-grade issuers (those rated BBB or higher by major rating agencies), representing companies across utilities, financials, industrials, and consumer sectors.

The emphasis on long maturities is the fund's defining characteristic. When interest rates rise, long-term bonds lose more value than shorter-term ones, because the gap between the existing coupon and the new market yield is larger. Conversely, when rates fall, long bonds surge in price. This high sensitivity to interest rates is both the fund's greatest appeal and its greatest risk. A buy-and-hold investor capturing yield through falling rates can see substantial price appreciation; an investor buying into a rising-rate environment can experience meaningful drawdowns.

## Vanguard's approach and ultra-low costs

Vanguard designed BLV as a passive tracker — it simply holds the bonds in the underlying index in roughly the same proportions, with no active manager trying to outguess interest-rate moves or credit spreads. This passive approach has two concrete advantages. First, it keeps operating costs extraordinarily low, well under 10 basis points annually. Second, it eliminates active-management risk: the fund's performance is the index's performance, minus only that tiny expense ratio. Investors know exactly what they own and what they pay.

Vanguard, as the sponsor, operates the fund within the company's broader philosophy of minimizing fees and conflicts of interest. The company structures itself as a mutual firm owned by its funds, not by external shareholders, which shapes incentives toward keeping costs low rather than maximizing asset gathering.

## Who BLV is for and the concentration dynamic

BLV is built for investors seeking broad, diversified fixed-income exposure without the fees and time commitment of active bond management. It is particularly useful as the bond sleeve in a balanced portfolio, or for investors who want to tilt toward longer-term bonds without picking individual securities. Because it holds thousands of bonds, it eliminates single-issuer credit risk in ways that holding a handful of individual bonds does not.

The fund does carry concentration risk by geography and sector. The United States Treasury market dominates the holdings, and the corporate portion skews heavily to sectors with large, investment-grade issuers: financials, utilities, consumer staples. An investor in an environment where US credit is weak or where Treasury yields are set to rise further faces meaningful downside risk.

## Tracking error and liquidity

Because BLV tracks a broad, liquid index, tracking error — the gap between the fund's performance and its benchmark — is minimal. The fund itself trades with tight bid-ask spreads during market hours, making it easy for individual investors to buy and sell without paying a wide premium or discount. Vanguard also provides good disclosure of the fund's current holdings and key statistics.

## How to research and use BLV

Anyone considering BLV should start by understanding the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, which Vanguard publishes and updates regularly. The prospectus names the exact index tracked, the fund's investment strategy, and the detailed risk factors — particularly interest-rate sensitivity and credit risk. The fact sheet includes the fund's current duration (a number measuring interest-rate sensitivity), yield, and weightings by bond type and sector.

Before investing, a reader should consider their interest-rate outlook and portfolio needs. A portfolio with little fixed-income exposure and a long time horizon is a natural home for BLV; an investor expecting rates to rise sharply in the near term might prefer shorter-duration bonds. Historical price charts show how the fund has behaved in prior interest-rate cycles — that real data is far more useful than any projection.
