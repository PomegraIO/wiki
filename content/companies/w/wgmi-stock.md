The CoinShares [Bitcoin](/bitcoin/) Mining ETF ([NASDAQ](/nasdaq/): WGMI) grants investors exposure to the companies and businesses engaged in Bitcoin mining—the computationally intensive process of validating transactions and creating new Bitcoin. Rather than holding Bitcoin directly, WGMI holds shares of mining companies, making it suitable for investors who want exposure to the economics and mechanics of Bitcoin production rather than the cryptocurrency itself.

Bitcoin mining is a capital-heavy, operationally complex business. Miners deploy specialised hardware, manage electricity costs at scale, navigate regulatory environments across different jurisdictions, and compete in a global race to solve mathematical puzzles and earn Bitcoin rewards. WGMI captures the fortunes of the firms doing that work: large-scale industrial miners, smaller independent operations, and companies providing mining infrastructure and services.

The fund's approach allows traditional investment accounts—retirement accounts, taxable brokerage accounts, and institutional portfolios—to gain Bitcoin mining exposure through a conventional equity vehicle. This eliminates the need to directly hold Bitcoin on a [cryptocurrency exchange](/cryptocurrency-exchange/) or wallet, a friction point for many institutional and conservative retail investors. However, it also means the fund's performance is tied to the share prices and profitability of mining companies, not directly to Bitcoin's price, a distinction that matters significantly in volatile markets.

The economics of Bitcoin mining shift with the network difficulty (how hard it is to solve the next block), Bitcoin's price, electricity costs, and hardware efficiency. When Bitcoin's price rises sharply, mining becomes more profitable and mining companies see their earnings improve. Conversely, when Bitcoin falls and electricity costs remain stable, profitability compresses and share prices of mining companies tend to suffer. The relationship is not perfectly linear—a mining company's operating leverage, debt levels, and hedging strategy all matter—but the sensitivity is real. WGMI's holdings, therefore, carry significant price volatility, especially during cryptocurrency boom-and-bust cycles.

The fund typically holds publicly listed miners across different geographies and scales: large, well-established mining operations; smaller, newer entrants; and perhaps companies providing mining hardware, cooling solutions, or other mining infrastructure. Because Bitcoin mining is a competitive, global business with no natural moat, the fund may hold companies from North America, Europe, Asia, and elsewhere. Holdings shift as the mining landscape consolidates, new entrants emerge, or companies acquire competitors.

Mining profitability depends partly on where companies operate. Jurisdictions with cheap, abundant hydroelectric or other renewable power—Iceland, parts of Canada, China historically—have been favoured by miners because electricity is often the largest operating cost. Regulatory and tax environments also matter: some countries have become hostile to mining, while others have actively courted the industry. WGMI's exposure to this geography and regulation is an indirect but material risk.

The fund carries electricity and climate risks as well. Large-scale Bitcoin mining consumes significant power globally, raising environmental concerns and inviting regulatory scrutiny in carbon-conscious jurisdictions. Changes to electricity regulations, carbon taxes, or mining bans can quickly erase the advantage of operating in a given location. Investors in WGMI are therefore exposed not just to mining company profitability but to the evolving regulatory treatment of mining itself.

Holding mining companies also means exposure to their financial structures. Many miners have taken on debt to finance hardware expansion and operations, betting that future Bitcoin mining rewards will service that debt profitably. Rising [interest rates](/interest-rate/) can squeeze margins for leveraged miners, and a sustained Bitcoin downturn could stress [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/) that were stressed by the assumption of higher future profitability.

How to research WGMI requires understanding both the mining companies themselves—via their financial statements and operational updates—and the Bitcoin network conditions they operate in. The prospectus and fact sheet outline the fund's selection methodology, current holdings, [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), and liquidity profile. For deeper context, investors should review SEC filings of the underlying mining companies to understand their cost structures, debt levels, and capital expenditure plans. Tracking Bitcoin's [hash rate](/hash-rate/) (the computing power securing the network) and the evolution of mining difficulty provides colour on whether the competitive landscape is growing harder or easier. Financial news sources covering cryptocurrency and mining equity research also track company-specific and sector-wide developments.
