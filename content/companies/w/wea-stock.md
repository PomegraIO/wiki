WESTERN ASSET PREMIER [BOND](/bond/) FUND (NYSE: WEA) is a closed-end investment company that pools investor capital to purchase a diversified portfolio of investment-grade and higher-yielding fixed-income securities. Unlike open-end [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/), a [closed-end fund](/closed-end-fund/) has a fixed number of shares that trade on an exchange like ordinary stocks. The fund is managed by Western Asset, a subsidiary of [Franklin Resources](/ben-stock/) Inc., and its chief objective is to generate high current income while preserving capital.

## Structure and how the fund works

WEA is a closed-end fund, meaning that when you buy shares, you are buying from other shareholders on the open market — the fund itself does not issue new shares or redeem them at will. This differs from an open-end mutual fund, where the fund grows or shrinks with every deposit or withdrawal. The fixed supply of shares can lead to a quirk: the fund's share price may trade above or below the underlying value of its holdings (called the [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), or NAV). If the fund is popular, shares might trade at a premium; if investors lose confidence, they may trade at a discount. Savvy investors sometimes hunt for discounts — buying shares at a lower price than the underlying assets are worth.

The fund employs leverage, borrowing money to purchase additional securities beyond what its shareholder capital alone could buy. Leverage amplifies returns in a rising bond market but magnifies losses if rates move sharply higher and bond prices fall. This is a conscious tradeoff: the fund can offer higher income to shareholders because it controls more assets than shareholders alone have deposited.

## Portfolio and income generation

WEA invests primarily in [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/) — those rated BBB- or higher by major credit-rating agencies — along with a smaller allocation to higher-yielding securities that fall outside that tier. The portfolio typically includes U.S. [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/), [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/), and other fixed-income instruments. The exact allocation shifts with market conditions and the fund manager's view of relative value.

The fund's primary purpose is to generate current income, and it does so by distributing a regular monthly [dividend](/dividend/) to shareholders. These distributions come from the bond interest the fund collects, plus any capital gains if the fund sells bonds at a profit. In a stable or rising bond market, the dividend is sustainable and predictable. But if [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise sharply, bond prices fall, and the fund's NAV declines — the dividend may then consist partly of a return of the shareholder's original capital rather than pure earnings, which gradually erodes the base.

## The interest-rate sensitivity question

The single largest risk to a bond fund is a sustained rise in interest rates. Bond prices move inversely to rates: when rates go up, existing bonds that pay lower yields become less attractive and their market price falls. A fund holding bonds purchased at 3% yields faces a marked decline in NAV if rates rise to 5%. If the fund then tries to maintain its monthly distribution, it may have to sell bonds at a loss or distribute capital rather than earnings.

Conversely, a falling-rate environment is a tailwind — bond prices appreciate, NAV rises, and the portfolio can sustain or increase distributions without depleting assets. Much of a bond fund's total return comes not from interest but from these capital gains (or losses).

## Leverage and dividend sustainability

WEA's use of leverage deserves scrutiny. The fund borrows at prevailing interest rates to buy additional securities that yield slightly more, pocketing the difference. When credit is cheap and rates are low, this works well. But if borrowing costs rise faster than the fund's yield does, or if credit markets seize up, the leverage becomes a drag. In extreme scenarios, a fund might have to liquidate assets to repay borrowers, forcing sales at inopportune times.

The monthly distribution itself is set at a level the fund's board believes is sustainable given the NAV, portfolio yield, and leverage costs. However, distributions are not guaranteed, and funds do cut them when circumstances warrant — typically when NAV has declined persistently and the dividend would otherwise be unsustainably high as a percentage of assets.

## How to research WEA as an investment

Start with the fund's [annual report](/10-k/) and prospectus, which spell out the investment objective, the [leverage ratio](/leverage-ratio-forex/), the portfolio composition by bond type and issuer, and the source of distributions (interest income, capital gains, or return of capital). The SEC filing for WEA (CIK 0001163792) contains these documents.

Watch the trend in NAV per share quarter to quarter — rising NAV suggests the portfolio is appreciating or the manager is making good security picks. Track the yield on the portfolio (the annual interest income as a percentage of NAV) and compare it to the distribution rate. If distributions are running ahead of yield for an extended period, the fund is burning through capital.

The discount or premium to NAV is also revealing. A persistent discount often reflects investor anxiety about the fund's ability to sustain its dividend; a premium suggests confidence. The wider the discount, the more margin of safety a buyer might have — but the reason for the discount matters.

Interest-rate forecasts and the curve outlook are essential context. Bond funds like WEA tend to perform best when rates are steady or falling; rising-rate environments test the dividend sustainability and expose leverage risks. The manager's commentary in quarterly reports offers color on their tactical positioning and their view of [credit risks](/credit-risk/) in the portfolio.
