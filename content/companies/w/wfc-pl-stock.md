[Wells Fargo](/wells-fargo/) is one of the United States' largest universal banks, offering retail banking, commercial lending, investment services, and wealth management to customers ranging from individual savers to Fortune 500 companies. The company's name and history are deeply embedded in American finance — Wells Fargo began as a stagecoach company carrying gold and mail across the frontier in 1852 — yet the modern institution is the product of a series of [mergers](/merger/) that shaped the U.S. banking landscape over the past four decades. Most consequentially, a 1998 merger between Wells Fargo Bank and Norwest Corporation created the company as it exists today, a firm headquartered in San Francisco that serves millions of customers and manages trillions of dollars in assets and deposits. That history carries weight: Wells Fargo was built by cautious, long-lived operators who believed in boring, stable banking and the compound power of a stable deposit base.

Wells Fargo's core business model is fundamentally simple, despite the complexity of modern financial services. The company takes deposits from consumers and businesses, holds those deposits on its [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/), and lends them out to borrowers at higher [interest rates](/interest-rate/). The difference between what it pays depositors and what it collects from borrowers is the net interest margin — the engine of profitability for any traditional bank. Beyond that basic spread, Wells Fargo earns fees for services: investment advisory fees, asset-management fees from wealth clients, mortgage origination and servicing fees, and a broad menu of small charges for account maintenance, wire transfers, and other transaction services. This is the bread-and-butter business that has generated steady profits for generations of bankers.

The company operates through several reporting segments, each serving distinct customer bases. The Community Banking division serves consumers and small businesses in the United States with checking and savings accounts, home loans, and personal lending. It is the closest Wells Fargo comes to a traditional neighbourhood bank, though the company operates through branches, mobile apps, and online channels rather than through a dense local network. The Commercial and Institutional Banking segment serves mid-sized and large corporations, providing cash management, lending, trade finance, and advisory services. This division is where a company might turn to finance an [acquisition](/acquisition/) or manage its global payments. The Wealth and Investment Management division serves high-net-worth individuals and institutional clients, managing investment portfolios, administering trusts, and providing advice. Within this are brokerage services and the company's private banking arm. Finally, the Corporate segment handles treasury functions, debt issuance, and other corporate-level items.

Like all universal banks, Wells Fargo's profitability depends heavily on its deposit base. Deposits are cheap to fund — the company pays interest, but not much compared to what it can earn on loans and investments. A large, sticky deposit base is the foundation of a bank's ability to lend profitably. Wells Fargo is among the largest deposit-gathering banks in the United States, with tens of millions of retail customers and substantial business deposits. That deposit base is a competitive moat, because it costs less than the alternatives: borrowing in wholesale markets, issuing [bonds](/bond/), or relying on short-term funding. The company invests much of its deposit base in U.S. Treasury securities and mortgage-backed securities, which provide stable, lower-risk returns.

The United States banking system is heavily regulated, and that regulation shapes how Wells Fargo operates. Banks must hold minimum levels of capital and liquidity, are subject to stress tests designed to ensure they could survive a severe [recession](/recession/), and face limits on how much they can lend relative to their capital. These constraints exist to protect depositors and the financial system, but they also limit how aggressively a bank can grow or how much risk it can take. Wells Fargo operates within that regulatory framework, though the company faced substantial regulatory scrutiny and restrictions following a compliance scandal that came to light in the mid-2010s, a period that affected its reputation and growth trajectory for years.

The company faces competition from an array of rivals. Other large universal banks — [JPMorgan Chase](/jpmorgan-chase/), [Bank of America](/bank-of-america/), [Citigroup](/c-stock/) — compete directly across retail banking, commercial lending, and wealth management. Regional and community banks compete in their local markets. Investment banks and asset managers compete for wealth management and fee-generating advisory services. Fintech companies and nonbanks have eroded the banking industry's historical monopolies on lending and payments. A consumer today can get a mortgage from a nonbank lender, can invest with a discount [broker](/broker/) that charges no advisory fees, and can pay friends through a digital app without ever visiting a bank branch. Wells Fargo must compete in that environment not just on price but on convenience, technology, brand, and the breadth of services it can offer under one roof.

The profitability of Wells Fargo, like all banks, is cyclical. In a healthy economy with rising interest rates, banks earn wider margins and fewer borrowers default. In a recession, the opposite occurs: margins compress, defaults rise, and banks must set aside capital to cover potential losses. The company must also navigate interest-rate dynamics: when the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) sets rates and the shape of the [yield curve](/yield-curve/) changes, it immediately affects what banks pay depositors and what they can charge borrowers. The last decade's era of very low interest rates compressed bank margins globally, though higher rates in recent years have helped. Beyond cycles, Wells Fargo must adapt to a slowly shrinking demand for traditional banking services as digital payments and nonbank competitors nibble away at market share.

For investors studying the company, the essential starting point is the annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0000072971), which discloses the composition of the loan portfolio, the deposit base by type, and the regulatory capital ratios the company must maintain. Quarterly earnings calls reveal current trends in deposit costs, loan demand, and credit quality. Watch the net interest margin trajectory closely — that spread indicates whether the company can profitably lend. Monitor loan losses and provisions for credit losses, which show how the credit cycle is evolving. And track the company's progress on technology and digital banking, which will determine whether it can keep pace with competitors in an increasingly digital financial world. Wells Fargo's [dividend](/dividend/) and capital management reveal how much profit the regulators allow the company to return to shareholders versus hold in reserve.
