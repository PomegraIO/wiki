Energous Corporation is a small, research-driven company pursuing one of the oldest dreams in physics and engineering: wireless power transmission. Its WattUp technology uses radio-frequency signals to transfer energy through the air to devices at a distance, without contact or visible light. Founded in 2012 and trading on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) under the symbol WATT, Energous has spent over a decade developing and refining the underlying science, collecting regulatory approvals, and preparing for commercial deployment of a technology that could fundamentally change how people charge devices.

> "Wireless charging over distance—fundamentally reimagining how and where devices get their power."

## The core technology and its history

Most wireless charging that exists today is inductive: two metal coils near each other transfer energy across a gap measured in millimetres. Energous approached the problem differently, using radio-frequency (RF) waves to transmit energy at distances of several meters, similar in principle to how cellular or WiFi signals travel through air. The engineering is substantially harder. RF power transfer is less efficient than contact-based induction, energy scatters and dissipates over distance, and safety is a paramount concern when broadcasting power that could heat human tissue.

Energous licensed foundational intellectual property and began assembling its own patent portfolio around WattUp, the brand name for its RF-based system. WattUp comprises transmitter hardware, receiver integrated circuits for devices, antenna designs, and software controls that manage power delivery and safety. Unlike a simple broadcast, WattUp is designed to adjust transmission in real time, aiming power at receivers and shutting down if human tissue is detected nearby—an essential safeguard against cooking someone's fingers.

The first meaningful regulatory milestone came in 2015 when the U.S. Federal Communications Commission granted approval for the WattUp transmitter to operate. That approval was crucial because it signaled that the FCC's spectrum allocation and safety standards permitted the technology to exist and could lead to broader commercial use. A larger milestone arrived in October 2021 when the FCC granted approval for over-the-air wireless power at unlimited distance, removing one class of prior restrictions.

## The business model: licensing and integration

Energous does not manufacture consumer devices. Instead, it licenses its technology to companies that build the devices. A partner integrates WattUp receiver chips into their product—a hearing aid, a medical sensor, a retail shelf label, an IoT tracking tag—and Energous receives royalties on sales or licensing fees upfront. This asset-light model keeps capital requirements lower than manufacturing would demand, but it means Energous needs partners to bring products to market.

The company is pursuing applications across several segments. Medical devices are a focus: hearing aids, pacemakers, and other implants that currently require frequent battery replacement or surgical intervention would be transformed by wireless charging. Retail and industrial sensors also stand out—electronic shelf labels in supermarkets that today need manual battery replacement could self-charge in a store with WattUp transmitters. The technology could charge IoT tracking devices, reducing maintenance labor. Consumer electronics are a longer-term horizon given the need to work within existing device ecosystems and regulatory frameworks around personal RF exposure.

Revenue is currently modest. As of 2025, Energous reported quarterly revenues in the low single-digit millions range, with Q3 2025 marking the highest quarter since 2015. The company is not yet profitable at scale, and profitability depends on achieving meaningful licensing deals and commercial device deployments.

## The regulatory and scientific risk

Wireless power transfer sits at the intersection of physics, regulation, and public health. The core unresolved risk is not whether the technology works—prototypes demonstrably do—but whether it can scale to commercial deployment while meeting strict standards for human safety and radio-frequency interference.

Safety standards for RF exposure in the United States are set by the FCC; internationally, different bodies (ICNIRP in Europe, others elsewhere) maintain their own limits. These limits determine how much RF power can be broadcast near people. Energous must prove that WattUp transmitters and the way they control and focus power do not violate these standards under real-world conditions. This is not trivial: a miscalibrated system or unexpected environmental interaction could exceed limits.

A second regulatory requirement in some jurisdictions is pre-market approval from health authorities. The FDA in the United States may require approval for medical devices using WattUp before they can be sold. This approval process is time-consuming and requires rigorous safety data, adding years to the commercial timeline for medical applications.

The technology also must coexist with existing wireless systems—cellular, WiFi, Bluetooth, radar, and others. If WattUp transmitters generate interference or interfere with licensed spectrum, regulators will restrict deployment. Energous has managed these concerns so far, but they remain contingent risks as real-world deployment scales.

## Capital and commercialization

Energous has raised capital through equity offerings and strategic investments. The burn rate is substantial relative to the company's [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) because R&D and regulatory work require sustained spending without near-term guaranteed returns. The company's ability to survive depends on reaching partnerships with device manufacturers and achieving commercial deployments before capital runs out, a classic venture-backed risk profile that now applies to a public company.

The path to meaningful revenue requires several pieces falling into place: continued regulatory approval, successful partnerships with device makers, commercial willingness by end-users to adopt the technology, and proof that the cost and complexity of integration justify the benefit of wireless charging. Any one of these could stall or fail, stranding the company's investment.
