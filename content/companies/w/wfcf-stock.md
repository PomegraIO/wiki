Where Food Comes From verifies what animals eat, how they are raised, and where they come from. The company sells certification services to ranchers, farmers, beef processors, and retailers—checking whether cattle are raised without antibiotics, whether feed meets organic standards, whether meat comes from known sources. Think of it as a third-party auditor and label printer for food supply. Retailers like Whole Foods want to sell beef marked as humanely raised or pasture-fed or antibiotic-free. Ranchers and processors want proof they can point to when a retailer asks. Where Food Comes From is the middleman that walks the supply chain, verifies the claim, and stamps the product so everyone knows it is real.

The company has evolved significantly in the past two years. It started with traditional verification services—auditors visiting ranches, checking records, certifying that the claim on the label is true. More recently, it has launched new certification programs, forged partnerships with major retailers, and built technology platforms to track animals from birth through sale. The shift reflects what the company sees as a structural change in agriculture: transparency is becoming table stakes, and the animals-to-consumer path needs to be verifiable end to end. That is where Where Food Comes From is trying to position itself.

## What Where Food Comes From does: the business model

The company has three revenue streams. The largest is verification and certification services—about 80 percent of 2025 revenue. Where Food Comes From sends auditors and inspectors to ranches and feedlots, reviews records, checks feed receipts, and verifies that the product meets a specific certification standard. For instance, a rancher wanting to sell beef as antibiotic-free requests a verification audit. Where Food Comes From checks the animal's medical records, verifies no antibiotics were administered, and issues a certificate. The rancher or processor then uses that certificate to market the beef to retailers. The company charges for the verification service per animal, per batch, or per location audited.

The second stream is product sales. Where Food Comes From sells ear tags, brands, and other identifying equipment that ranchers use to mark and track cattle. The company also recently launched RaiseWell Certified, a new certification standard for natural livestock production. RaiseWell certifies that animals were raised with no antibiotics or added growth hormones, verified animal care, and source-level traceability. The company is building an auction platform around RaiseWell, where buyers and sellers can trade RaiseWell-certified cattle, which is a different business model—a marketplace rather than a service.

The third stream is professional services: consulting, data management, and support for certification programs. This is smaller but recurring and sticky.

The 2025 revenue was $25.7 million, driven by the core verification business but constrained by cattle supply tightness in the United States and sector cyclicality.

## The shift happening now: what is changing

For most of its history, Where Food Comes From was a specialist auditor for a niche market—producers and retailers concerned about certification and traceability. That market existed but was small. The company is now trying to push that market forward, both by creating new standards and by partnering with major retailers to embed verification into their supply chain.

The RaiseWell Certified launch is the clearest signal. Rather than just offering verification for existing standards, Where Food Comes From is defining its own certification from the ground up. RaiseWell focuses on animal care, antibiotic absence, and traceability. In January 2026, Whole Foods Market committed to carrying RaiseWell-certified beef, which is a significant retail anchor for a new certification. That partnership does two things: it gives RaiseWell credibility with ranchers (they know there is a buyer), and it gives Whole Foods a proprietary certifying body for a category consumers care about.

The company has also moved into related markets. A December 2025 partnership with Pangea, Prime Pursuits, and [Walmart](/wmt-stock/) aimed to bring CARE Certified sustainable leather to automotive brands. That is a different supply chain—leather rather than meat—but uses the same fundamental business model: verify a claim, certify the product, and provide traceability.

Third, Where Food Comes From has deepened its technology and data capabilities. The company is building U.S. CattleTrace, a producer-led database for cattle traceability, in partnership with the industry. The idea is to create a data layer that runs beneath the certification process—every animal has a traceable ID in a shared database. That makes verification faster and more reliable. It also creates a switching cost: if you have invested in the platform, you are less likely to switch certifiers.

## The business pressures

Cattle supply in the United States has been tight in recent years, which constrains volume. Fewer cattle means fewer animals to certify and fewer opportunities for the verification business to grow. That pressure is cyclical—cattle inventories fluctuate over years—but for 2025, it has been a headwind.

Second, the certification market is fragmented and somewhat commoditized. There are dozens of certification standards, and some of them overlap. Retailers and ranchers have to choose which standards matter. If RaiseWell gains traction, Where Food Comes From captures that market. If it does not, the company is still executing the traditional verification business, which is smaller and less glamorous.

Third is the farmer and rancher perspective. If farmers believe certification is a regulatory hassle that does not add revenue, they resist it. Where Food Comes From's business depends partly on producers seeing certification as a path to higher prices or premium shelf space. That calculus is real in some segments (organic, pasture-raised) but not universal. Industrial beef producers often have limited use for certifications.

## How to research Where Food Comes From as an investment

Where Food Comes From files 10-K and 10-Q forms with the SEC (CIK 0001360565). The company reports verification volumes, which are the raw count of animals certified. Track this metric: if verification volumes grow while prices remain stable, the company is gaining market share. If volumes shrink even as prices hold, the business is contracting.

Watch also for news on retail partnerships and RaiseWell adoption. Whole Foods is the initial anchor, but the opportunity is if other retailers commit. Each major retailer commitment is a revenue multiplier, because it creates demand from ranchers wanting to reach that shelf space.

Monitor cattle inventory data from the USDA. The broader cattle cycle drives how many animals are available to certify. If cattle inventories are rebuilding, the tailwind returns.

Finally, track the company's technology investments and platform progress. The shift from pure verification services to a technology and platform play requires investment and execution. Early wins—like U.S. CattleTrace gaining producer participation—would signal that the company is making progress on that front. As with any small public company, nothing here is advice; these are the mechanisms by which the business operates and where its growth and risks are concentrated.
