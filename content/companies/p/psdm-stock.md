The PGIM Short Duration Multi-Sector [Bond ETF](/bond-etf/) tracks the investing philosophy that not all [bonds](/bond/) need to be boring, but they do need to be prudent. PSDM is a bond fund issued and managed by Prudential Global Investment Management, the asset-management arm of [Prudential Financial](/pru-stock/), a major insurance and investment company. The fund targets investors who want bond exposure but do not want to own a single asset class—say, just [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) or just corporate debt—and do not want to take on the duration risk that comes with owning long-term bonds.

Duration is the measure of how sensitive a bond is to interest-rate changes. A short-duration bond—one that matures soon or has a short weighted average maturity—moves less in price when [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise or fall. A long-duration bond swings wildly. PSDM targets short duration, which means the bonds in the fund mature, on average, in the relatively near term, protecting shareholders from big price swings if the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises rates unexpectedly.

The fund is multi-sector, meaning it draws bonds from several different corners of the fixed-income market rather than betting everything on one. It might hold a mix of Treasury bonds, investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/), securitized debt, and floating-rate instruments. By spreading across sectors, the fund reduces reliance on any single borrower or credit sector and diversifies the sources of yield.

## How bonds pay and what credit risk means

Bonds are IOUs. A borrower issues a bond, promising to pay the bondholder interest (the coupon) at regular intervals and to repay the principal at maturity. PSDM's holdings include bonds issued by the U.S. government (Treasury bonds, which carry essentially zero [credit risk](/credit-risk/) because the government can print dollars), U.S. corporations with strong [credit ratings](/credit-rating/) (investment-grade corporate bonds, which carry modest default risk but usually pay more than Treasurys), municipalities (municipal bonds, which may carry state or local credit risk but often offer tax advantages), and other borrowers.

Credit risk is the chance that a borrower will fail to pay interest or principal on time. Treasurys carry near-zero credit risk. A bond issued by a large, profitable company like [Apple](/aapl-stock/) or [Microsoft](/msft-stock/) carries low credit risk—the chances of default are tiny. A bond issued by a smaller or shakier company carries higher credit risk and usually pays a higher coupon to compensate investors for bearing it.

PSDM's multi-sector approach means the fund holds a mixture of these risk levels. It does not take on huge credit risk the way a high-yield or junk-bond fund would. It also does not try to be risk-free like a Treasury fund. It finds a middle ground.

## The yield-and-risk tradeoff

Bonds trade at prices that reflect current interest rates. If a bond was issued paying three percent and rates rise to five percent, the bond's price falls because new buyers can get five percent elsewhere. If rates fall to two percent, the bond's price rises. PSDM's short duration means those price moves are modest, even as rates shift. A Treasury fund holding thirty-year bonds would see much bigger price swings.

The flip side of that protection is yield. Short-duration bonds pay less coupon income than long-duration bonds because investors do not need to be compensated as much for near-term maturity. A five-year corporate bond might yield three percent; a thirty-year bond might yield five percent. PSDM investors get the modest price stability but give up some of that extra yield.

The multi-sector holdings broaden the yield picture. While short-duration Treasurys might yield two percent, short-duration corporate bonds might yield three percent, and municipal bonds might yield two-and-a-half percent. By mixing them, PSDM targets a weighted yield that beats all-Treasury bonds but avoids the concentration and credit risk of owning only corporates.

## Who holds PSDM and why

PSDM appeals to conservative investors who want bond exposure but do not want to bet their portfolio on the direction of long-term interest rates. It also suits those who want [diversification](/diversification/) across bond types without building a portfolio manually. Retirees and near-retirees often hold bonds for stability; PSDM offers stability with a bit more yield than a plain Treasury ladder.

The fund also attracts investors who are unsure about the interest-rate environment. If they think rates might rise, they should own short-duration bonds to avoid big losses. If they think rates might stay flat or fall, they could own longer-duration bonds for more price appreciation. When uncertain, short duration is a hedge.

Sophisticated investors sometimes use bond funds like PSDM as ballast in a stock-heavy portfolio, knowing that bonds will cushion losses when stocks fall and offer steady income when stocks are flat.

## Costs and tax implications

PSDM charges an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) that covers Prudential's management, trading costs, and custodial services. The expense ratio is typically modest for an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/)—often in the range of 0.3 to 0.6 percent annually—because bonds are simpler to trade than stocks and the fund's strategy is systematic rather than requiring heavy analyst involvement.

The distributions PSDM makes consist mostly of coupon income from the underlying bonds. That income is taxed as ordinary income, not capital gains, which means holders in high tax brackets may want to consider whether a tax-sheltered retirement account is a better home for the fund. Municipal bonds within the fund might pay tax-free income to some investors, but that benefit shows up in the overall distribution picture rather than as a separate line item.

## Interest-rate risk and credit risk

The main risk PSDM carries is that credit conditions deteriorate and borrowers start defaulting on their bonds. In a severe [recession](/recession/), even investment-grade corporate bonds can suffer losses. The diversification across sectors and bond types helps, but it is not a guarantee. A deep financial crisis would likely hit PSDM's holdings, though less severely than a junk-bond fund would suffer.

[Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) is muted by the short duration, but it is not absent. If rates rise sharply, all bond prices fall, including those in PSDM. The losses would be smaller than in a longer-duration fund, but they would still sting.

Sector concentration is possible. If PSDM overweights any particular industry—for example, financial services—and that industry suffers a shock, the fund would outperform a fully neutral basket in some scenarios and underperform in others.

## How to research PSDM

Start with the fund's fact sheet and holdings report, available on Prudential's website. These documents break down exactly what sectors the fund holds, what the weighted average maturity is, and what the [current yield](/current-yield/) is.

Compare PSDM's yield to alternatives: a simple Treasury ladder, a short-duration Treasury ETF, or a corporate-bond ETF. Understanding the yield advantage (or disadvantage) helps you judge whether the diversification is worth the complexity.

Check historical returns over periods including rising-rate environments (2022, for instance, was brutal for bonds) and falling-rate environments. How did PSDM fare? Did the short duration protect it as expected?

Look at the fund's credit quality breakdown. What percentage is in investment-grade corporates versus Treasurys versus municipals? That mix tells you how much credit risk you are taking relative to a plain Treasury fund.

If you own the fund in a taxable account, model the tax cost of a year's distributions at your marginal rate to understand the after-tax yield. That yield may be substantially lower than the pre-tax yield suggests, especially if you are in a high bracket.
