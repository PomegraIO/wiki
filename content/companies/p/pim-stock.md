Putnam Master Intermediate Income Trust is a closed-end investment fund that manages a portfolio of [bonds](/bond/) and other fixed-income securities on behalf of thousands of individual and institutional investors. The fund, listed on the NYSE under the ticker PIM, allows investors to buy a single share in order to gain exposure to a professionally managed basket of debt instruments—an arrangement that streamlines access to [diversification](/diversification/) and professional management that would be expensive or impractical for a small investor to assemble alone.

[Closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/) operate differently from the [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) familiar to many retail investors. When a closed-end fund launches, it raises a fixed pool of capital; that money is then invested according to the fund's stated strategy. New investors do not give money to the fund itself but instead buy existing shares from other investors on the [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/), just as they would buy a corporate stock. This design means the fund's capital base is fixed rather than fluctuating as investors come and go. The trade-off is that a closed-end fund's share price can diverge from the underlying value of its holdings—trading at a premium if investors are eager to own it, or a discount if they are not, independent of whether the bonds inside are performing well.

Putnam, a long-established asset-management firm founded in the 1950s, structures this particular fund around a deliberate maturity profile. The "Intermediate Income" label signals that the fund focuses on bonds with durations in the three- to ten-year range, as opposed to short-term instruments (which offer less yield) or long-term bonds (which carry greater [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/)). By concentrating on intermediate maturities, Putnam aims to capture a middle ground—enough yield to justify holding bonds in a rising-rate environment, without the volatility exposure of longer-duration positions.

The fund's holdings break into broad categories, each serving a different role in the income portfolio. Government and agency bonds—U.S. Treasuries and debt backed by government agencies such as [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae/)—form a stable foundation that offers safety and liquidity. Investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) issued by financially sound companies provide higher yield than governments while remaining relatively safe. [High-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/), also called "[junk bonds](/junk-bond/)," issued by companies with weaker [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/) but still serviceable debt, add incremental income at greater [credit risk](/credit-risk/). Asset-backed and mortgage-backed securities, which are bonds whose principal and interest are secured by pools of mortgages or other loans, offer attractive yields but can suffer if the underlying borrowers default or if prepayment patterns shift.

The fund distributes monthly income to shareholders—the interest income and [dividends](/dividend/) earned from all these holdings, minus expenses. That regular payout is the chief appeal to income-seeking investors, though it comes with an important caveat: the fund's distribution is not fixed in dollar amount. When [interest rates](/interest-rate/) are high and the bonds in the portfolio yield well, shareholders receive more. When rates fall or bond values drop, the distribution shrinks. Moreover, the fund sometimes distributes its own capital (not just earned income) to maintain a constant payout rate; this is called "return of capital" and is a form of accounting maneuver that can mask whether the underlying portfolio is truly generating income or merely returning shareholders' own money.

Putnam's role as manager is to navigate the bond market on behalf of the fund, selecting securities, adjusting maturity and credit-quality exposure, and managing duration. The manager's skill lies in making these decisions well—buying bonds before their credit improves, rotating out of sectors at risk, avoiding the worst credits, and timing the portfolio's interest-rate exposure. The [management fees](/management-fee/) Putnam charges for this work are deducted from fund assets, reducing the net income available for distribution.

The fund sits in an intricate spot in the financial supply chain. Upstream, it depends on functioning bond markets, [credit rating](/credit-rating/) agencies that assess risk, and the ongoing confidence of investors in the creditworthiness of corporations and governments. Downstream, it serves income investors—retirees, trusts, and others seeking a steady cash flow from their portfolios. If credit conditions deteriorate (defaults rise), the fund's holdings lose value and distributions may shrink. If interest rates surge unexpectedly, the market value of existing bonds falls, and shareholders who sell shares on the exchange lose capital. Conversely, in periods of economic strength and stable rates, intermediate-bond portfolios tend to perform well.

The key metrics for evaluating a closed-end fund like PIM are the yield (annual distribution divided by current share price), the discount or premium at which shares trade relative to [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), and the credit quality of the underlying holdings. A fund trading at a discount may look attractive if the underlying bonds are sound, but it signals that investors value the fund less than its true assets—sometimes warranted, sometimes an opportunity. The 10-K filing with the SEC details the complete portfolio, the use of leverage (if any), performance attribution, and fee disclosures. For investors studying [income funds](/income-fund/), the critical questions are whether the manager is taking on credit risk that seems fairly compensated, whether the distribution level is sustainable from actual income rather than capital return, and whether the fund's leverage (if used) amplifies returns or merely compounds risk in a downturn.
