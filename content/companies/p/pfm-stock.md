PFM screens the US equity market for a specific trait: companies that have raised their dividend payouts for at least 10 consecutive years. The theory is straightforward. If a firm has the financial strength and earnings growth to sustain and grow its dividend over a full decade, it likely has durable competitive position, disciplined capital allocation, and a degree of business stability. The index underlying PFM — the NASDAQ OMX Dividend Achievers Index — systematically selects only these dividend growers.

The dividend-achiever screen is a backward-looking filter. A company enters the index only after it has proven a 10-year streak of increases; it exits if that streak is broken. This built-in lag means the index avoids the most aggressive dividend payers (which often lack sustainable earnings) and tends to skew toward mature, profitable, moderately growing businesses in defensive sectors like consumer staples, utilities, healthcare, and industrials. Dividend growers typically offer steadier earnings and less volatile stock prices than growth stocks, but they also offer less capital appreciation potential.

Costs are lean. PFM is a passive index fund — it simply owns the holdings of its benchmark index in their prescribed weights, rebalancing when components change. Its expense ratio reflects the minimal overhead of tracking the index. Liquidity is deep: PFM trades thousands of shares daily. No transaction fee to buy or sell; only the market bid-ask spread applies, typically a fraction of a percent for a fund of this size and popularity.

The dividend-achiever universe is not recession-proof. An economic downturn can still pressure the stocks held — if earnings decline sharply, companies may struggle to maintain or grow dividends, and that forced dividend cut triggers an index exclusion. It happened in 2008–2009, when many dividend stocks fell hard. The screen does provide some cushion: if a company's earnings stumble but management sustains the dividend temporarily (the bet is earnings will recover), the dividend itself survives. But the strategy offers no protection against outright dividend suspension or cut.

Concentration risk is real. The index typically holds 250–300 companies, but a handful of large, well-known dividend payers — utilities, energy majors, consumer staples — often dominate the portfolio. A downturn in a single large sector or an interest-rate spike that devalues bond-yielding stocks will hit PFM hard. The fund is not a diversified equity portfolio; it is a bet on dividend-paying maturity.

Investors drawn to PFM often seek income plus some capital appreciation — the combination a long-term holder receives from both dividends and share-price gains. For someone building a portfolio that prioritizes steady cash payouts and lower volatility than the broad market, PFM aligns with that goal. Someone seeking growth, or exposure to volatile, high-potential sectors like technology, should look elsewhere.

The research documents are straightforward. The NASDAQ OMX Dividend Achievers Index rules and constituent list are published and freely available; the fund's prospectus spells out the selection criteria, and the fact sheet shows current holdings. A quick scan of the top 20 holdings usually captures the fund's character. The ten-year dividend growth track record is also where to look for conviction: companies in the index have all hit that milestone, but the dividend growth rate varies widely, and some achievers grow slowly while others accelerate. That heterogeneity is worth noting when assessing the fund as an income source.
