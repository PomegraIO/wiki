Purebase Corp is a small agricultural-input company that sources and processes natural minerals—leonardite, kaolin, laterite, and others—into products for sustainable and conventional farming. The company was incorporated in 2010 and rebranded to Purebase in 2015, shifting away from its original e-commerce identity to focus on the agricultural minerals market.

The company operates in a straightforward segment: it buys or extracts mineral feedstocks, processes them into useful agricultural inputs, and sells them to farms, agricultural distributors, and specialty retailers. The products include Purebase Shade Advantage WP (a natural mineral plant protectant that reduces sunburn damage to fruit and vegetable crops), humic acid derived from leonardite (which improves soil structure and water retention), and various soil amendments and bio-stimulants. All revenue comes from the sale of these agricultural products.

## The mineral feedstock advantage

Purebase's underlying thesis is that certain natural minerals have genuine agronomic value but are under-utilized by most farmers. Leonardite—oxidized lignite, a precursor to coal—is rich in humic acid, which improves soil carbon content, enhances microbial activity, and increases water-holding capacity. Kaolin clay has distinct properties: it reflects sunlight and can be applied to leaves and fruit to prevent heat stress and sunburn, a significant problem in hot climates and during heat waves. Laterite contains iron oxides and other minerals that are valuable in tropical soils.

The business model hinges on sourcing these materials cost-effectively and processing them into forms that farmers and agricultural operators will buy. If Purebase can mine or purchase leonardite at low cost and convert it into a humic acid product that farmers recognize as valuable, the margin is the difference between raw material cost and the retail price of the finished product.

This is capital-light compared to other agricultural businesses: no farmland, no breeding programs, no complex supply chains. The company is, in essence, a materials-processing and distribution business. Its investments are in processing equipment, storage, and distribution logistics.

## The market and the pitch

The agricultural-input industry is enormous and fragmented. Farmers use fertilizer, soil amendments, crop protectants, and bio-stimulants to maximize yield and manage specific agronomic problems. The conventional approach is to use synthetic nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium fertilizers; modern farmers are increasingly interested in organic, sustainable, or regenerative approaches that build soil health over time rather than relying solely on chemical fertility.

That is where Purebase positions itself: on the sustainability and soil-health side of the market. The company's products appeal to organic farmers (who cannot use synthetic inputs), regenerative operations (that prioritize soil carbon and microbial activity), and conventional farmers dealing with specific problems like sunburn on high-value crops.

The market opportunity is real but distributed. No single farmer or farm operation is so large that Purebase can rely on it. Instead, the company must build relationships with a large number of smaller farms, agricultural retailers, and specialty distributors. Each sale is relatively small, and repeat purchases depend on the farmer perceiving value in the next season.

## Financial reality and scale

The company is very small. Annual revenue has fluctuated in the range of $300,000 to $600,000 in recent years. In 2024, the company reported $310,511 in revenue; in 2025, it reported $564,000. The trailing twelve-month revenue is approximately $286,000. These are micro-cap numbers, reflecting a company that is more venture-stage than an established operating business.

With revenue in that range, Purebase does not have much room for error. Overhead must be minimal. The company likely has very few employees and operates with a lean structure. Profitability is marginal at best; the company is probably fighting year to year to cover basic operating costs and fund product development or mineral [acquisition](/acquisition/).

The company has approximately 278 million shares outstanding (a typical marker of a company that has issued shares to raise capital multiple times) and a [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) of roughly $6 million, putting it squarely in the micro-cap range. This is a highly illiquid, speculative security.

## Market dynamics and sourcing

Purebase operates in a competitive but loosely organized market. Agricultural input suppliers range from global chemical giants selling commodity fertilizers to small regional players in specialty products like soil amendments and bio-stimulants. The advantage of being small is that Purebase does not compete on price with multinationals; instead, it competes on farmer relationships, product reputation, and distribution to niche channels. The disadvantage is lack of scale—the company cannot match the marketing spend, distribution infrastructure, or product breadth of larger competitors.

The sourcing of raw materials is a critical operational consideration. Leonardite is found in specific deposits (primarily in the Northern Great Plains of the United States); kaolin clay in various locations around the world. Purebase must either secure reliable mineral supplies through partnerships, leases, or purchases, or face volatility in input costs. The company has not disclosed public details about its sourcing strategy, which leaves some uncertainty about the durability of its supply chain and the sustainability of its margins.

Demand for these products is tied to broader agricultural trends. Organic farming has grown steadily but remains a small fraction of total agricultural acreage. Regenerative farming—a newer and more stringent standard focused on carbon sequestration and soil health—is still primarily a marketing concept with limited mainstream adoption. If mainstream agriculture does not adopt soil-amendment practices on a large scale, Purebase remains a small niche player. If adoption accelerates, the company could have runway to grow significantly.

## The kaolin opportunity and future direction

One concrete development worth noting is the company's work on a kaolin-based product designed to help create lower-carbon concrete. This represents a pivot from the agricultural focus into the construction-materials space. If the company can produce a kaolin-based supplementary cementitious material that lowers the carbon footprint of concrete while maintaining performance, the market would be vastly larger than agriculture. Concrete is essential to modern infrastructure; any material that reduces its carbon intensity attracts significant interest from builders, developers, and regulators.

This is still developmental, and it is unclear what stage it is at or whether it has any commercial traction yet. It is worth monitoring because if the kaolin-concrete initiative proves viable, it could be the stepping stone to a larger business than the agricultural products alone support.

## What the numbers tell you

Revenue of $300,000 to $600,000 annually is barely enough to sustain operations for a public company. Purebase must be run very lean, relying on the founder or small management team and keeping overhead minimal. The question is whether the company is in a genuine market opportunity with room to scale or whether it is simply a small lifestyle business that will remain small indefinitely.

The fact that management has explored the kaolin-concrete angle suggests they are trying to find a larger addressable market. The agricultural-minerals play is real but slow-growth and fragmented. The construction-materials play could be bigger, but the company would need to pivot significantly and likely partner with larger construction companies or building-product manufacturers to get real scale.
