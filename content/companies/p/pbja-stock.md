**What is a [buffer ETF](/buffer-etf/), and why would anyone use one?**

A buffer ETF trades the upside of the market for downside protection. PGIM's S&P 500 Buffer 20 ETF - January (PBJA) wraps S&P 500 exposure with a floor and ceiling: if stocks fall, you lose no more than 20% in a year; if they rise, you capture around 20% of the gain. The rest of the market return is forfeited. It is insurance in ETF form, reset each January.

**How does the January reset work?**

PBJA's observation period runs from January 1 to December 31 each calendar year. On January 1, PGIM refreshes the options that define the floor and cap. If the market fell 25% in the prior year and you held PBJA, you lost only 20% — your floor held. But that protection resets. In the new January-to-December period, you get a fresh 20% floor and fresh 20% cap. This is mechanically different from a perpetual protection plan; it is a year-by-year reboot.

**Who chooses a buffer ETF over a plain [index fund](/index-fund/)?**

Someone uncomfortable watching the market swing wildly. If a 40% crash would tempt you to sell at the bottom, paying for a 20% floor might prevent that panic move, which would otherwise wreck your long-term returns. You are paying a cost — the capped upside — for peace of mind. If you can tolerate drawdowns without flinching, you do not need it, and a plain S&P 500 fund will outperform over time. If you cannot tolerate them and would sell, a buffer is a sensible hedge against your own behavior.

**What is the actual cost of this protection?**

The fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is higher than a vanilla S&P 500 ETF because PGIM must buy and manage options daily. Beyond the stated ratio, there is the "silent cost" of capped upside: in a decade with strong years, owning a 20% cap means you miss the extra 10%, 15%, or 20% returns. Over many years, that gap compounds into meaningful underperformance. A buffer works best when the market is volatile but not sustainably trending higher. In a long [bull market](/bull-market/), you regret buying it.

**How does the S&P 500 compare to other buffer-eligible indices?**

PBJA focuses on the S&P 500, a broad, liquid index of 500 large-cap U.S. stocks. PGIM offers variants tracking other indices (Russell 2000, international equities), but the S&P 500 is the flagship: it is the most familiar benchmark, the easiest to hedge with options, and the most liquid for a large fund. If you want buffer protection on a narrower index, options become more expensive and less efficient, so the cost of that protection rises.

**What happens if the market crashes before the reset?**

Your 20% floor applies to the annual calendar period. If the market drops 35% from January to March, your fund floor means you lose 20% in that first quarter. The floor is in effect. But you still have nine months left in the year. If the market does not recover much, you have live to December with a loss in the neighborhood of 20%. If it recovers sharply, your upside is capped at 20% gain for the full year. The timing of when crashes occur within the year matters, but the floor and cap frame the full-year return regardless.

**Is this a tax-efficient holding?**

Like most ETFs, PBJA is reasonably tax-efficient because it does not trigger excess capital gains. However, the capped structure means you are giving up gains you would otherwise have captured in taxable accounts. From a pure tax standpoint, holding an uncapped index fund in a taxable account and a [buffer fund](/buffer-fund/) in a retirement account makes more sense, though the cost of the buffer typically outweighs any tax benefit of sheltering it.

**How does PBJA compare to PBFB?**

Both are PGIM buffer products with identical mechanics, but PBJA resets in January (calendar year) and PBFB resets in February. The February reset means PBFB's observation period is February 1 to January 31. For a buy-and-hold investor, the calendar difference is minor; it mainly affects the timing of when the protection resets and how performance is measured relative to calendar-year benchmarks. If you are layering multiple buffer products, staggering the reset dates can smooth the overall protection profile, but holding only one makes the choice arbitrary.

**When does a buffer ETF shine in practice?**

After a market crash, if you held PBJA and stocks fell 40%, you lost 20%. Now the reset arrives. You did not panic-sell, and your cost-of-waiting is visible: you lost 20% instead of 40%. That regret is cured by the reset — you have a fresh floor for the next year. That is the fund's entire value prop: it catches the crash, lets you sleep, and reboots the protection. In calm years, you will lag a plain fund because of the cap. Over a full [market cycle](/market-cycle/), the net depends on the severity and timing of drawdowns and whether you would have actually lost money by panicking without the buffer.

**How would you research this fund before buying?**

Read the prospectus on PGIM's website to confirm the exact floor, cap, and reset mechanics. Pull up historical performance data for PBJA and compare it to the S&P 500 and to PBFB (its February cousin). Look at years with significant drawdowns (2020, 2022, etc.) and see how the floor performed. Look at strong years and see what the cap cost you. Run a few scenarios: if you had invested a lump sum at the start of 2008, when would this fund have been worth holding versus painful? If you had invested steadily over the past decade, would the protection have been worth the capped upside? The answer will depend on your own comfort with volatility and your track record of selling at the bottom. If you have never panicked and sold, PBJA is not for you. If you have, or if you are worried you might, it is worth serious consideration.

**What should a fund researcher watch going forward?**

Monitor the size of the options market and overall volatility. If volatility spikes, the cost of the floor and cap rises, and PGIM's expense ratio may need to adjust. Watch PGIM's asset flows into buffer products to see if this strategy is gaining or losing popularity. Read about new regulations on [structured products](/structured-product/) to see if compliance costs are rising. And keep an eye on long-term [interest rates](/interest-rate/): options pricing depends partly on rates, so a shift in the rate environment can change the economics of buffer products over time. None of these changes is dramatic, but they are the forces that shape the true cost of holding PBJA.
