Power REIT is a publicly traded real estate investment trust that owns energy-generating assets and industrial properties across North America, leasing them to operators under long-term agreements. The company operates at a modest scale relative to major REITs, making it a micro-cap investment that is relatively illiquid but structured to deliver recurring income to shareholders.

What is a REIT, and why does Power REIT use that structure? A real estate investment trust is a tax-efficient vehicle for owning income-producing property. REITs are required by law to distribute at least ninety percent of taxable income to shareholders as dividends, and in exchange they pay no corporate income tax — the tax burden flows through to shareholders. This structure is ideal for owning land and buildings that generate steady rental cash flow, because the trust can return nearly all income to investors without the friction of corporate-level taxation. Power REIT uses this framework to own energy and industrial assets that are leased to operators, capturing the real estate economics without operating the plants themselves.

What does Power REIT actually own? The company's portfolio consists of power generation facilities (including both conventional fossil-fuel plants and solar installations), distribution infrastructure for renewable energy, and miscellaneous industrial properties. The assets are geographically dispersed, and the company leases them to established operators under triple-net lease agreements. In a triple-net lease, the tenant is responsible for all property taxes, insurance, and maintenance costs, while the landlord (Power REIT) collects a fixed or escalating rent payment. This arrangement minimizes the landlord's operational burden and makes the revenue stream highly predictable — the tenant's costs are their own problem, while Power REIT's income is insulated from rising property taxes or maintenance expenses.

How does Power REIT differ from larger REITs? The company is substantially smaller than major industrial REITs such as STAG Industrial, STORE Capital, or Realty Income. Scale confers advantages: larger REITs can raise capital more cheaply, can negotiate better rates with tenants through economies of scale, and can weather a single tenant default more comfortably. Power REIT's smaller size means higher volatility in share price, less analyst coverage, lower trading liquidity, and a greater sensitivity to any single tenant's financial performance. However, the smaller size also means the company can focus on niche opportunities in energy infrastructure that larger, more bureaucratic REITs might not pursue.

What makes the power and renewable energy sector attractive for a REIT? Energy infrastructure is increasingly valued for its stable, inflation-protected cash flows. Utilities and industrial operators require reliable facilities to generate or distribute power, and they are willing to commit to long-term leases at predictable rents. Many renewable energy projects (wind farms, solar installations) are backed by power purchase agreements with investment-grade utilities or governmental bodies, making them lower-risk tenants. This shift toward valuing renewable energy has made assets in that space more attractive to investors, and a REIT with exposure to solar and other renewables can benefit if tenant demand and lease rates rise.

What are the main risks Power REIT faces? As a micro-cap REIT, Power REIT is vulnerable to any single tenant's failure. If a major lessee defaults or fails to renew a lease at the end of its term, the company loses that rent stream and must find a new tenant — a process that can take months or years and may require accepting lower rent to attract a replacement. The company is also exposed to regulatory changes affecting energy policy; a sudden shift away from certain power sources (for instance, accelerated coal-plant retirement) could force early termination of leases and asset write-downs. Real interest rates also matter — as the cost of capital rises, the value of long-term fixed-rent cash flows declines relative to assets that can raise rents with inflation. Finally, Power REIT's small size makes it illiquid and difficult for large institutional investors to acquire, which limits the potential buyer base and can suppress the share price.

How should an investor research Power REIT? The company's 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001532619) details the lease portfolio, the identity and creditworthiness of tenants, and the remaining lease terms. This information is critical — a REIT with most of its rent expiring in the next three years is in a different position than one with twenty years of visibility. Examine the lease escalation rates (do rents rise with inflation, or are they fixed?), the financial strength of the largest tenants, and the company's debt structure. Power REIT's dividend yield is higher than most REITs', which reflects both the smaller scale and higher risk; compare it to the yield on utility stocks and larger industrial REITs to contextualize whether the premium is justified by the company's fundamentals. Track quarterly earnings and any announcements about significant leases maturing or new properties being acquired, as these drive changes in the cash flow available for distribution.
