Western Asset Investment Grade [Income Fund](/income-fund/) is a closed-end [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/) that holds a diversified portfolio of investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), mortgage-backed securities, and other fixed-income instruments. Shares trade on the [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) under the ticker PAI, and the fund's attraction lies in its high [current yield](/current-yield/) — paid as a distribution to shareholders — and the way it competes against traditional open-end [bond](/bond/) funds by moving to a market price that may diverge from its underlying [net asset value](/net-asset-value/).

## What is a closed-end bond fund, and why does PAI exist?

An open-end bond fund accepts deposits from new investors every day and redeems shares on demand at net asset value. A closed-end fund raises a fixed pool of capital once, then trades in the [secondary market](/secondary-market/) like a stock. Western Asset Income manages that pool by assembling corporate bonds that earn interest income; shareholders receive distributions from that interest, supplemented by realized gains when bonds mature or are sold. The fund's appeal is both structural and practical: its fixed capital base means no dilution from new investors, and its ability to trade at a discount to net asset value can offer entry-point bargains to buyers who believe the underlying bonds are sound.

## Why investment-grade matters

The fund restricts itself to bonds rated BBB or higher by major agencies — the boundary between "likely to pay" and "riskier than cash-equivalent securities." This matters because it sits the fund between [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) (essentially risk-free, lower yield) and [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) (higher yield, higher risk). The typical buyer of a fund like PAI is an individual investor or small institution seeking current income without the complexity of picking individual bond positions.

## Distribution rates and the yield puzzle

Western Asset Income advertises a distribution yield — the annual payout as a percentage of share price. That yield is often attractive compared to bond yields themselves, which draws retail investors. The subtlety is that distributions may include "return of capital" — a portion that is not from earnings but from the fund's original pool of assets. An aggressive fund might declare a 9% distribution even if the underlying portfolio yields 5%, by returning some principal to shareholders. That arithmetic eventually runs down the fund's net asset value, so the distribution is not infinitely sustainable at that rate. The critical discipline for any closed-end fund investor is distinguishing between sustainable distributions rooted in bond income and yields that are gradually liquidating the fund.

## Risk and the discount-to-NAV opportunity

PAI shares trade on the exchange and move in price based on buyer demand, not solely on changes in the value of bonds inside. It is common for [closed-end funds](/closed-end-fund/) to trade at a 5–15% discount to net asset value — meaning you can buy a dollar of underlying assets for 85–95 cents. This creates an opportunity for patient investors but also a trap for those who buy at a premium and expect mean reversion. The discount reflects, in part, the market's view of the fund's management, its fee structure, and the likelihood that the distribution is sustainable.

## Competitive dynamics

Fixed-income investors face a spectrum of choices: individual bonds, Treasury ETFs, open-end bond funds, and closed-end funds like PAI. Low-cost [index funds](/index-fund/) (iShares, Vanguard [bond ETFs](/bond-etf/)) have drained assets from actively managed closed-end funds because they charge less and are transparent. Closed-end funds survive by pursuing a differentiated mandate — in PAI's case, a focus on investment-grade corporate debt with a history of distributing income. The fund's value depends on whether Western Asset's credit selection and bond management earn enough to justify its [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) and to support the advertised distribution without eroding capital.

## How to evaluate PAI as an investment

Anyone considering PAI should examine the fund's latest [annual report](/10-k/) and fact sheet for the current distribution rate, the breakdown of holdings by sector and [credit rating](/credit-rating/), and the fund's discount or premium to net asset value. The prospectus lays out the fees and the fund's strategy. Critically, compare the current yield to yields available in comparable options — other income-focused closed-end funds, intermediate bond ETFs, and the prevailing yield on investment-grade corporate bonds themselves. Watch whether the fund's net asset value is drifting downward (a sign distributions exceed earnings) or holding steady. The closed-end fund universe is thin and offers room for mispricing, but it also punishes investors who do not do the homework on sustainability and relative value.
