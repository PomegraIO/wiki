*The **Invesco S&P SmallCap Financials ETF** (PSCF) holds approximately 120–150 small-cap U.S. financial institutions — predominantly regional and community banks, smaller insurers, and specialized lenders. It tracks the S&P SmallCap 600 Financials Index and reflects the peculiar history of American banking: a system once dominated by thousands of independent, local banks that has consolidated dramatically over decades while leaving a persistent ecosystem of small players.*

## The American banking heritage and consolidation

PSCF's existence reflects the long history of American banking as a radically decentralized system. Before federal laws began permitting interstate branching in the 1990s, thousands of independent banks operated in distinct regional and local markets, forbidden from expanding across state lines. These banks served their communities, held deposits, originated mortgages and business loans, and were often family-owned institutions lasting generations.

That landscape began to fragment in the 1980s and accelerated through the 1990s and 2000s. Interstate branching deregulation, technology that reduced the geographic advantages of local networks, and the rise of national supermarket banking all drove consolidation. Large banks acquired regional operators and independent community banks at an accelerating pace. The 2008 financial crisis supercharged the trend: weak banks failed, stronger ones were seized and merged, and the sector emerged far more concentrated.

Today's small-cap banks are the survivors of that consolidation — institutions that either endured decades of competitive pressure or emerged as newer entrants finding niches where larger banks chose not to compete. A small-cap bank today might focus on small-business lending in a specific region, or agricultural finance, or equipment lending, or serve customers in areas where megabanks have closed branches. The insurance segment of PSCF is similarly consolidated but remains fragmented because underwriting different risks requires specialized expertise.

## The interest-rate and credit cycle

Small-cap financials move with [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and economic activity in ways large banks do not. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises rates, the immediate effect on small-cap banks is often positive: the spread between what banks earn on loans and what they pay on deposits — the net interest margin — widens. Banks can charge more to borrowers while keeping deposit rates modest, and earnings can expand quickly because a bank's [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) is leveraged.

However, that benefit compounds with risks. Higher rates dampen borrowing demand as both consumers and businesses become more cautious. Home-price declines reduce the collateral value of mortgage portfolios. Unemployment rises in [recessions](/recession/), increasing loan losses. Small banks, concentrating often in a single state or one type of lending, lack the product and geographic [diversification](/diversification/) of megabanks. A community bank focused on mortgages in Arizona faces existential risk in a housing crash. A small-cap lender specialized in [commercial real estate](/commercial-real-estate/) loses on both loan losses and [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) if the economy stalls.

Conversely, in expansions, small banks thrive. Credit demand accelerates, deposit bases grow, loan losses are minimal, and leverage amplifies earnings. A small bank's earnings can grow 15% or 20% annually in strong expansions, whereas a megabank might grow 5% or 10%.

## From crisis to stability to stress

The 2008 financial crisis was catastrophic for small-cap financials. Regional banks that had invested heavily in mortgage-backed securities and concentrated portfolios of subprime mortgages failed or were seized. Surviving banks emerged with damaged capital, tighter lending standards, and a lasting wariness of leverage. The following decade of near-zero interest rates compressed net interest margins and made banking harder, even as banks rebuilt capital.

From 2016 to 2018, as the Federal Reserve raised rates, small-cap banks experienced a resurgence. Rising net interest margins benefited depositories, and investors sought exposure to interest-rate-sensitive financial stocks. Tax reform in 2017 improved profitability. Small-cap banks outperformed sharply during this period.

The environment reversed in 2020 when pandemic fears prompted the Federal Reserve to slash rates to zero. Small-bank earnings compression followed. In 2022–2023, rapid rate hikes aimed at controlling [inflation](/inflation/) created a new crisis: banks' [bond](/bond/) portfolios deteriorated sharply, deposit rates could not keep pace with rising Treasury yields, and depositors shifted money to money-market funds. Several mid-size regional banks failed amid deposit runs, and small-cap bank stocks crashed amid systemic concerns.

## Today's environment and remaining vulnerabilities

The small-cap banks and insurers operating today have learned from history. Capital ratios are stronger. Risk management disciplines are tighter. Many institutions have repositioned themselves as relationship lenders serving underserved segments — small businesses, specialized lending, niche markets. This positioning makes them less systemically important but also less vulnerable to broad macroeconomic shocks that hit megabanks harder.

However, vulnerabilities remain. Rising interest rates help banks' net interest margins but hurt their bond portfolios and can trigger loan losses in downturns. Depositor flight to safer institutions remains a risk in stress scenarios. Regulatory burden — capital requirements, [stress testing](/stress-testing/), data protection rules — weighs more heavily on small institutions than on the largest banks because compliance costs do not scale linearly with size.

Insurance companies in PSCF face different dynamics: claims experience, investment returns, and rate regulation affect profitability. In recessions, claims typically rise and investment returns decline simultaneously. Smaller insurers, lacking the diversification across lines of insurance and geographies that large carriers possess, face sharper earnings swings.

## Suitable for whom

PSCF is suitable for investors who expect interest rates to rise or remain elevated without triggering recession, who believe the economic cycle will remain stable, or who explicitly want financial-sector exposure. It is unsuitable for conservative investors, those uncomfortable with leverage, or anyone who cannot tolerate severe drawdowns during financial crises. The 2008 crisis and the 2023 regional banking stress showed that small-cap financials can lose 50% or more of their value in acute crises.

## How to research PSCF

Review Invesco's fund fact sheet and the S&P SmallCap 600 Financials index methodology. Examine the fund's holdings to understand the composition — what fraction are banks versus insurance and other financial services? Identify the top 20 holdings and research their specific market focus, capital ratios, deposit composition, and loan portfolios. Plot PSCF's returns against interest-rate movements (track the 10-year Treasury yield) and economic indicators (unemployment, recessions) to understand the correlation. Review the fund's performance during the 2008 financial crisis and the 2023 regional banking stress to understand potential drawdowns. Monitor regulatory developments affecting capital requirements, deposit insurance, and lending standards, as these often move small-cap financial stocks substantially before they move megabanks.
