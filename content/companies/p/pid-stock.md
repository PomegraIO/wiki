The Invesco International [Dividend](/dividend/) Achievers ETF (ticker PID) targets investors who want dividend income from outside the United States while screening for a specific kind of company: those with a consistent record of raising their payout. The fund tracks the Invesco International Dividend Achievers Index, which is restricted to non-US companies that have increased their dividends annually for at least ten consecutive years. The ten-year test is designed to weed out one-time dividend payers and isolate what index designers view as genuinely committed payers — companies with strong cash generation and management intent to reward shareholders steadily.

## History and launch

PID launched in 2006 as Invesco pursued a portfolio of thematic [dividend-focused ETFs](/dividend-focused-etf/) tailored to different geographic regions and investor needs. The international dividend space was underserved at the time; most yield-focused funds in the US market concentrated on domestic stocks, leaving investors who wanted international income without an efficient, low-cost vehicle. The Invesco International Dividend Achievers Index was purpose-built to fill that gap, applying a quantitative screen — a decade of unbroken dividend growth — to non-US developed-market stocks. That screening philosophy reflected a hypothesis: companies disciplined enough to raise dividends every year through economic cycles and downturns are likely financially sound and focused on shareholder returns. The index has been refined over the years to adjust for market evolution and rebalancing mechanics, but the core screening rule has remained constant.

## What the fund holds

The index that PID tracks covers developed-market companies in Europe, the Asia-Pacific region, and Canada — essentially, the economically mature, publicly traded [stock markets](/stock-market/) of the world outside the United States. The screening is strict: only firms that have demonstrated ten or more consecutive years of dividend increases at the time of inclusion qualify. Within that population, the index applies a weighting mechanism that emphasizes stocks with higher yields, giving greater portfolio weight to companies paying higher dividends relative to price. This combination means the fund tends to hold mature, established companies with strong cash flows and a shareholder-friendly payout culture.

The holdings vary with the composition of developed-market dividend stocks, but they commonly include large-cap pharmaceuticals, consumer staples, telecommunications firms, and financial services companies from countries like Switzerland, the United Kingdom, Australia, and the Netherlands. These are typically conservative, slow-growing businesses that prioritize returning cash to shareholders over reinvesting in aggressive growth.

## Structure and strategy

PID is a straightforward ETF that seeks to track its underlying index. It is not leveraged, inverse, or synthetically replicated; it holds actual stocks and aims for price and dividend returns in line with the index. The fund rebalances periodically (typically quarterly or annually, depending on Invesco's schedule) to maintain target weights and to incorporate new stocks that meet the dividend-growth criterion while removing those that have broken their streak.

Because the fund is international in scope, it carries [currency risk](/currency-risk/) — share prices fluctuate with movements in the euro, [British pound](/british-pound/), [Australian dollar](/australian-dollar/), and other currencies relative to the [US dollar](/us-dollar/). An investor holding PID is implicitly betting that their foreign-currency exposure either hedges other currency bets in their portfolio or is a deliberate position. PID itself does not hedge currency exposure; it passes through all gains and losses from exchange-rate movements.

## Risks and real considerations

**Currency exposure.** The fund's non-US holdings mean that a strong US dollar can be a headwind, as overseas profits, when converted back into dollars, are worth less. Conversely, a weak dollar boosts returns. This [currency volatility](/currency-volatility/) is in addition to stock-price movements.

**Dividend sustainability risk.** The ten-year screening rule reduces but does not eliminate the risk of a surprise dividend cut. Economic shocks, sector downturns, or management shifts can force even long-term dividend-payers to reduce or suspend payouts. When that happens, the stock typically falls sharply, compounding losses for income-focused investors.

**Limited capital growth.** Because the index favours established, mature companies with high yields, the fund is not a growth vehicle. Long-term total returns often come primarily from reinvested dividends rather than share-price appreciation. In low-yield or rising-rate environments, such a profile can underperform more-diversified international stock funds.

**Index survivorship and selection bias.** The dividend-growth screen naturally creates a bias toward historically stable countries and sectors. Companies from emerging or unstable regions are largely excluded. A shareholder is getting a slice of "dividend winners" in the developed world, which is more stable but less economically diverse than a true global portfolio.

**Fee drag over time.** While the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is reasonable, ongoing dividend reinvestment, [currency hedging](/currency-hedging/) (if used), and trading costs can add up over decades. An investor comparing this fund to a low-cost, broad international [index fund](/index-fund/) should account for that friction.

## Who this fund suits and how to research it

PID is designed for investors seeking regular dividend income from international stocks, who believe that companies with a long track record of raising dividends are safer and less likely to cut. Income investors, retirees reinvesting dividends, and those wanting geographic [diversification](/diversification/) while maintaining a yield tilt are natural holders. The fund is less suitable for growth-oriented investors or those uncomfortable with currency exposure.

Prospective investors should start with Invesco's fund fact sheet and prospectus, which list the current holdings, the exact expense ratio, and the fund's historical [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/). The Invesco International Dividend Achievers Index methodology document lays out the precise screening rules and rebalancing schedule. To understand the underlying businesses, reading earnings reports and dividend announcements from a few major holdings — a European pharmaceutical company, an insurance firm, a telecom operator — gives insight into how these mature companies generate and sustain their dividend payments. Currency-exposure articles and currency-forward pricing help frame the foreign-exchange dimension. For international investors specifically, understanding tax implications of international dividend withholding is also worthwhile, as many countries levy taxes on dividends paid to foreign shareholders.
