The Invesco California AMT-Free [Municipal Bond](/municipal-bond/) Portfolio (PWZ) is an exchange-traded fund that holds a portfolio of municipal [bonds](/bond/) issued in or for the state of California, with a specific constraint: every bond in the fund is structured to avoid triggering the [Alternative Minimum Tax](/alternative-minimum-tax/) (AMT) for the investor. For a California resident in the highest federal and state tax brackets, PWZ offers something rare—income that is exempt from both federal and California state income taxes, without the AMT complication that sometimes follows municipal-bond ownership.

Municipal bonds are debt issued by states, cities, counties, and special-purpose authorities to fund infrastructure, schools, and services. The bonds pay interest, and that interest is exempt from federal income tax. If the bond is issued by your home state, the interest is also typically exempt from state income tax. California, as a high-income-tax state, makes this distinction matter: a California resident holding California municipal bonds can keep 100% of the interest free from both federal and state levy. That tax shield is why municipal bonds offer lower yields than taxable bonds of comparable credit quality—investors are willing to accept lower cash returns in exchange for the tax benefit.

The AMT complication arises because the federal government imposes an alternative minimum tax on high-income individuals to prevent them from using too many tax preferences to eliminate their tax liability altogether. Some municipal bonds—particularly those issued to fund certain private activities like sports stadiums or pollution-control equipment—are "AMT bonds," meaning the interest counts as a tax preference item. High-income investors who already have high tax preferences from elsewhere can find themselves owing AMT even if their regular income tax would be zero. PWZ sidesteps this by holding only "non-AMT" or "AMT-free" municipals—bonds where the interest does not trigger the alternative minimum tax.

The fund's portfolio typically includes general-obligation bonds (backed by the state or municipality's full taxing power), [revenue bonds](/revenue-bond/) (backed by specific revenue streams like water sales or toll roads), school bonds (common in California, often general-obligation), and bonds from special districts like water agencies. Because California has strong credit (the state's bonds trade at relatively tight spreads versus US Treasuries), defaults are rare, and the fund's credit quality is generally solid. The fund rebalances periodically, adding newly issued California munis and allowing maturing bonds to roll off, so it does not hold every single California municipal ever issued—only a representative, actively managed sample.

The real audience for PWZ is narrow and high-income. If you live in California, are in the top federal tax bracket, and have significant taxable income from other sources, the tax shield of California AMT-free municipals is material. A bond yielding 3% tax-exempt is equivalent, for someone in a 40% combined federal-state marginal bracket, to a taxable bond yielding 5%. That is a significant advantage. If you are in a lower tax bracket, or live outside California, PWZ does not make as much sense; you could buy municipals from other states or taxable bonds and come out ahead.

[Interest rate](/interest-rate/) risk is the main driver of PWZ's value. If interest rates rise, the prices of existing bonds fall (because new bonds offer higher yields). If rates fall, bond prices rise. Because PWZ holds a portfolio of bonds of varying maturities, a sharp rise in rates causes the value to drop; a falling-rate environment lifts values. Investors who might need their money back in 2–3 years face real price risk if held in what amounts to a medium-duration bond portfolio.

[Credit risk](/credit-risk/) also matters, albeit more subtly. California's bonds are solid, but not risk-free. A severe [recession](/recession/) or a fiscal crisis in the state could impair credit quality, and individual bond issuers within California (municipalities, districts) vary in creditworthiness. PWZ is not insured against all credit losses, so investors bear the risk that a California municipality could struggle or default, though the historical frequency is low.

For readers evaluating PWZ, the starting point is the fund fact sheet, which details the maturity profile and shows the largest holdings by issuer. A portfolio dominated by short-term bonds behaves very differently from one weighted toward longer-dated bonds. The prospectus explains the AMT-free selection criteria. And for a personal tax decision, it is essential to run the math: compare the after-tax returns of PWZ against what you could earn from taxable bonds in a brokerage account, given your specific marginal tax rate. If you live in California, are in a top bracket, and expect to stay in California, PWZ can be part of a tax-efficient bond allocation. If circumstances change—you move out of state, your income drops, or you shift to a Roth or tax-deferred account—the logic shifts immediately.
