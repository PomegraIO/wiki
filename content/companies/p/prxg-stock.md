*The **Praxis Impact Large Cap [Growth ETF](/growth-etf/)** (ticker PRXG) is a fund that tracks large-cap growth companies while filtering them through an environmental and social impact lens. Rather than hold every stock in a broad growth index, it begins with that universe and removes firms whose business models or practices conflict with sustainable-investing principles — then weights the remaining holdings much like an [index fund](/index-fund/) would. The result is something between pure passive indexing and a hand-picked portfolio: systematic, rule-based, but narrowed.*

## What the fund actually holds

PRXG begins with the same raw material as most large-cap [growth funds](/growth-fund/): the 500 or so largest and fastest-growing publicly traded U.S. companies. But before building its portfolio, the fund applies negative screens — it removes sectors and companies it deems incompatible with environmental and social criteria. Energy firms with fossil-fuel exposure, weapons manufacturers, tobacco companies, and firms with poor labor or human-rights records land on the exclusion list. So do some financials, depending on lending practices.

What remains is still a large universe of growth stocks — technology, healthcare, industrials — but with certain industries and business lines culled. The fund then holds those remaining stocks in a weighting scheme that closely mirrors a broad growth index, which is why PRXG behaves more like a screened index fund than an actively managed portfolio. Turnover is lower than a traditional fund, fees are modest, and the portfolio evolves gradually as the underlying index changes.

The key trade-off is obvious: by excluding entire sectors — energy, weapons, high-impact chemicals — PRXG exposes holders to [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/). A narrow exclusion list that removes a large chunk of the market means fewer stocks to choose from and potentially uneven sector weightings. If energy or materials outperform in a given year, PRXG lags simply because those holdings are unavailable to it.

## The issuer and the mechanics

Praxis Funds is a family of socially screened investment funds that predates most modern ESG product lines by decades — the organization traces its roots to the 1980s, and socially responsible investing was far more niche then. The fund operates under the exchange-traded fund structure, meaning shares trade on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) during market hours like any stock, with intraday pricing and tight [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) for most investors. Unlike a [closed-end fund](/closed-end-fund/), PRXG can issue or redeem shares in large blocks to keep the fund price closely tied to the [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) of its holdings.

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) — the annual cost as a percentage of assets under management — sits at qualitatively moderate levels for a screened, actively managed product, higher than a plain-vanilla [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) fund but lower than a traditional active fund that employs a team of stock pickers. Because much of the work is rule-based (the screens are published and consistent rather than idiosyncratic), the fund's costs remain closer to passive territory than to fully active management.

## ESG screens and real limitations

Negative screening is straightforward in principle but opaque in practice. What counts as "poor labor practices"? How much fossil-fuel revenue disqualifies a diversified conglomerate? PRXG publishes its screens, but the boundaries are judgment calls — two impact-focused funds can arrive at different exclusion lists even when they share the same stated values. That judgment risk belongs to the fund sponsor.

A subtler risk is what academics call the "impact wash" problem: a portfolio that looks good on paper (no oil companies, no weapons makers) may or may not produce measurable environmental or social benefit. Excluding a company from your fund does not change its behavior or capital costs; if the firm raises money in another market instead, the net impact is unclear. Investors drawn to PRXG for impact motivations should be clear-eyed about that uncertainty.

The screening also creates a hidden concentration. Because PRXG excludes entire sectors, it ends up overweight in technology, healthcare, and consumer discretionary — the sectors that pass the screens most easily. That concentration can amplify the fund's swings relative to a broad market index. When growth stocks fall, PRXG can fall further precisely because it has no ballast in energy or utilities to soften the move.

## Costs and how it trades

PRXG trades during NASDAQ hours with sufficient liquidity for most retail investors — the bid-ask spread (the cost to buy or sell a single share) is typically pennies. The expense ratio is quoted as a percentage; compare it directly to competitors like EUSA or VUUG to get a sense of whether you are paying a premium for the screening.

Because PRXG is an ETF, you pay no transaction fee to most [brokers](/broker/), though you will incur a capital-gains tax when you sell at a profit. The fund itself aims to minimize [capital gains distributions](/capital-gains-distribution/) by using the ETF structure's built-in tax efficiency, an advantage over a traditional [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/).

## How to research it

Start with the prospectus and fact sheet on the Praxis Funds website, which lay out the exact screening criteria, the current holdings, and the fund's risk disclosures. Then compare PRXG's performance and holdings against plain-vanilla large-cap growth funds (like VOO or SPY) and against other ESG-screened alternatives — the differences reveal what you are gaining and what you are sacrificing for impact. Watch the sector weightings over time; a fund that claims neutrality but is heavily tilted toward tech deserves scrutiny. Finally, read the fund's [annual reports](/10-k/) or impact reporting if available, which may articulate what outcomes the organization hopes to achieve and whether the exclusions are producing visible change. Impact investing is a real investment choice, but it requires honest thinking about the trade-offs.
