> "Nanosecond pulses. No thermal damage."

That is the core promise of Pulse Biosciences. The company manufactures medical devices that apply extremely short, high-intensity electric pulses—measured in billionths of a second—to selectively eliminate cancer cells and certain skin lesions while leaving surrounding healthy tissue largely intact. The platform is called nsPEF, for nanosecond pulsed electric field. The stock trades on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) as PLSE.

## The physics and the appeal

Most energy-based cancer treatments work through heat. Surgical ablation, radiofrequency, laser, and other approaches generate temperatures high enough to destroy cells, but heat is indiscriminate—it damages everything in its path, including nerve, blood vessel, and connective tissue. That collateral damage limits how aggressively doctors can treat, especially in sensitive areas near the eye or face.

Pulse Biosciences' nsPEF technology operates on a different principle. An electric pulse applied for a few billionths of a second can destabilize the cell membrane and trigger a form of cell death (apoptosis or irreversible electroporation) without generating significant heat. The field penetrates the cell, causes the damage, and then stops—before thermal energy accumulates. The result, in theory, is selective tumor destruction with less damage to surrounding normal tissue and no thermal scarring.

The physics has been well-studied in academic literature. The challenge is engineering it into a practical clinical device and proving that the real-world benefit justifies the development effort and cost.

## The lead product and early commercialization

Pulse's CellFX System is a medical device that applies nsPEF pulses to skin lesions, primarily melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma. A physician positions the applicator against the lesion, initiates a treatment cycle, and the device delivers the pulses. The procedure is minimally invasive—no incision, no sutures—and can often be completed in an outpatient setting. Treated tissue is removed after the procedure for pathological examination to confirm the response.

The company has pursued regulatory approval in the United States and Europe for melanoma and keratinocyte cancers (basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma). Early clinical data suggested efficacy and a favorable safety profile compared to traditional surgical and ablative alternatives. The addressable market is not small—skin cancer is the most common malignancy—but it is also fragmented, with established treatment modalities (surgery, topical therapy, radiotherapy) entrenched in clinical practice.

Adoption requires proving clinical superiority or offering a genuine convenience advantage: faster healing, better cosmetic outcome, or better tolerability. Persuading dermatologists and surgeons to add a new device to their workflow and to train in a novel technique is a slow, costly process, even if the underlying technology is sound.

## The broader vision and pipeline

Pulse has not stopped at skin cancer. The company has explored applications of nsPEF in other cancers and dermatological conditions—melanoma of the mucous membranes, certain Barrett's esophagus lesions, and other indications where minimal tissue damage and avoiding thermal injury are particularly valuable. However, most programs remain in earlier development stages, meaning clinical proof and regulatory approvals are still years away.

The fundamental appeal of the platform is that it could extend to other cancer types and organs if clinical trials demonstrate benefit. But that future depends on clinical evidence, regulatory success, and the company's ability to fund development—all uncertain.

## Business model and the path to profitability

Pulse Biosciences is a medical device company, which means revenue comes from selling or leasing the CellFX System to hospitals, cancer centers, and dermatology practices, plus ongoing consumable supplies (applicators, sterile kits). The installed base determines recurring revenue; the speed of new customer [acquisition](/acquisition/) determines growth.

The company is not yet profitable. It has spent heavily on R&D, regulatory submissions, and clinical trials. Revenue from current sales is growing but does not yet cover operating expenses. The business model assumes that as adoption scales, gross margins on consumables will be strong enough to support the company's cost structure and eventually generate [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/).

That inflection point—from cash burn to positive cash generation—is the critical milestone. If adoption accelerates and the company reaches significant scale in its current indications, profitability becomes achievable. If adoption stalls or new competitors enter the space, the company could face years of losses and capital needs.

## Competition and technological risk

Pulse competes against entrenched treatment modalities (surgery, laser, radiotherapy) and against other emerging technologies (radiofrequency, cryotherapy, newer photodynamic approaches). None of these technologies is going away, and Pulse's success depends on carving out a niche where its particular advantages—lower thermal damage, faster recovery, convenience—provide real clinical value to patients and surgeons.

The core risk is that the clinical evidence, once fully developed, does not match the promise. If CellFX produces comparable efficacy to existing treatments but does not offer a compelling advantage in safety or cosmetic outcome, the device will struggle to gain traction. Additionally, larger medical-device companies could enter the space and out-market or out-distribute a smaller competitor.
