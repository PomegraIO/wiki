*The Putnam PanAgora ESG Emerging Markets [Equity ETF](/equity-etf/) (PPEM) holds stocks of companies in emerging-market countries that meet environmental, social, and governance (ESG) quality standards. Rather than holding all emerging-market companies equally (as a broad emerging-market index would), PPEM screens the universe for firms with stronger ESG profiles and weights them accordingly. This means it excludes or underweights companies with poor environmental practices, weak governance, or significant social risks, while overweighting those with better-managed ESG characteristics. The result is a tilted emerging-markets fund: still exposed to growth in developing economies, but filtered through an ESG lens.*

## The emerging-markets supply chain role

Emerging markets are where global manufacturing supply chains originate. Factories in Vietnam and Bangladesh produce apparel and electronics. Mining companies in Peru and Zambia extract copper and cobalt essential to batteries and renewable energy. Agricultural exporters in Brazil and Indonesia feed global commodity markets. Technology firms in India and China serve global customers.

These supply chains face acute ESG risks. A garment factory with poor labour conditions, a mine that contaminates local water, or a timber company cutting primary forest are all material risks to investors—they face regulatory action, customer backlash, supply disruption, or forced closures. PPEM's filtering logic is to hold emerging-market exposure while avoiding the worst offenders and preferring companies that manage these risks better.

The practical effect is that PPEM may own a textile manufacturer in Vietnam that has invested in worker safety and facility standards, while excluding or underweighting a competitor with a history of labour violations. Both are exposed to the same emerging-market cycle and supply-chain demand, but one faces lower ESG-related risk.

## Environmental screening

Environmental risks in emerging markets are substantial. Rapid industrialisation without mature regulations has led to air pollution, water pollution, and deforestation in major emerging economies. For an investor, these risks translate into company-level exposure: a coal-heavy power utility in India faces declining demand and stranded assets. A beverage company in a water-stressed region faces input costs and supply risk. A palm oil producer with exposure to deforestation faces regulatory and customer pressure.

PPEM's environmental screening likely excludes or downweights:

- Thermal coal producers and power plants dependent on coal
- Companies with significant environmental liabilities or spills
- Deforestation-linked operators (palm oil, logging, cattle ranching)
- Heavy industrial polluters without abatement plans

It may overweight or prefer:

- Renewable energy operators
- Water and waste management companies
- Companies with environmental certifications and science-based emissions reduction targets
- Manufacturing operations powered by renewable energy

The screening is not absolute—PPEM does not eliminate all fossil fuel exposure or exclude all heavy industry—but it tilts the portfolio toward lower environmental risk.

## Governance and capital allocation

Governance quality varies sharply across emerging markets. Some countries have strong minority shareholder protections and transparent capital allocation. Others concentrate power in founder families or state-owned entities that may operate more opaquely. A company with a weak independent board, no minority protections, or a controlling shareholder that extracts value is a governance red flag.

PPEM's governance screening likely favours:

- Independent board structures
- Minority shareholder protections
- Transparent capital allocation and M&A processes
- Low related-party transaction risk
- Clear audit and internal control standards

And disfavours:

- Founder-dominated or family-controlled structures without independent oversight
- State-owned enterprises with political influence over decisions
- Opaque [acquisition](/acquisition/) or [dividend](/dividend/) policies that benefit insiders
- Weak accounting standards or audit quality

The effect is that the fund may own an Indian bank with a professional management structure and independent board, while avoiding or underweighting a competitor with concentrated family ownership and murky decision-making. Again, both are banks exposed to the same emerging-market [credit cycle](/credit-cycle/), but the first faces lower governance-related risk and likely better capital allocation.

## Social criteria and workforce risks

Social risks in emerging markets centre on labour standards, supply-chain labour practices, and community relations. A manufacturer with poor working conditions, forced labour in its supply chain, or a history of conflicts with local communities faces operational disruption, customer pressure, and reputational damage.

PPEM's social screening likely filters for:

- Fair wages and reasonable hours
- Safe working conditions and worker health programmes
- Community relations and grievance processes in resource-extraction operations
- Supply-chain labour standards (especially relevant for garment and electronics manufacturers)
- Anti-corruption and anti-bribery practices

And filters against:

- Companies with documented labour violations
- Operations reliant on migrant workers with few protections
- Mining or resource operations in conflict zones or with poor community relations
- Widespread corruption or bribery linked to senior management
- Suppliers with known forced labour or child labour

For investors, this reduces the risk of sudden disruptions from labour actions, supply-chain exposure, or reputational cascades.

## The performance trade-off

Screening for ESG tilts a portfolio away from the market-cap-weighted emerging-market index. The question is whether that tilt improves or harms returns. The evidence is mixed and time-dependent. In periods when ESG is rewarded globally (low-carbon investing, strong governance), ESG-tilted emerging-market funds have outperformed. In periods when value and growth diverge, or when commodity cycles favour resource-heavy companies, ESG screens can underperform the broad index. PPEM's returns depend not just on emerging-market growth but on whether the ESG tilt adds or subtracts value in the current environment.

One underappreciated fact is that ESG screening reduces [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/). A broad emerging-market index is heavily weighted to the largest, often state-controlled companies (Chinese banks and state enterprises, for example). ESG screening, by disfavouring opaque state ownership and governance risks, diversifies the portfolio toward better-managed, often smaller companies. This [diversification](/diversification/) can be a headwind in [bull markets](/bull-market/) (when the mega-cap Chinese tech stocks rally fastest) and a tailwind in corrections (when governance strength matters).

## How to research PPEM

Start by understanding what ESG screening means in practice. Read the fund's prospectus and methodology document to see which specific ESG criteria it uses and how strictly it applies them. Different managers weight environmental, social, and governance factors differently. Some funds exclude entire sectors; others tilts within them. PPEM's specific approach matters for understanding what you are buying.

Then compare PPEM's portfolio to a broad emerging-market index (like the [MSCI Emerging Markets Index](/msci-emerging-markets-index/)). What sectors is PPEM overweighting or underweighting relative to the market? Is it dramatically under-exposed to banks, state enterprises, or materials companies? This tells you whether the ESG tilt is meaningful or cosmetic.

Track PPEM's returns over trailing periods against a comparable ESG-screened fund and against an unscreened emerging-market index. Does the ESG tilt add value, destroy it, or does it depend on the market environment? Watch also for flows—when investor appetite for ESG investing peaks, ESG-tilted funds outperform, and flows reverse when the trend reverses.

Finally, monitor news from the fund's holdings. Have any companies faced environmental scandals, labour controversies, or governance failures? If so, how did the fund respond—did the ESG screen catch the issue early, or was the company already exiting the fund? This real-world test tells you whether the screening methodology is robust or superficial.

PPEM is a way to gain emerging-market exposure with a tilt toward companies with lower ESG-related risks. But emerging markets are volatile by nature, and the ESG tilt can magnify or dampen that volatility depending on the broader investment environment.
