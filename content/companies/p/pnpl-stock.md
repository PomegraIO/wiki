## What Pineapple is and why it exists

Pineapple, Inc. emerged from the cannabis industry's transition from illicit markets to regulated, state-licensed operations. Founded in 1983, the company has operated for decades in the cannabis and related markets, but its modern structure reflects the opportunities and constraints of legalized cannabis in the United States. Cannabis operators need physical locations, have complex regulatory compliance requirements, and face banking and capital constraints because federal prohibition still dominates, leaving banks reluctant to lend. Pineapple carved out three business segments to serve these needs: property leasing to licensed cannabis operators, retail sales of hemp-derived cannabidiol (CBD) products, and professional consulting on cannabis licensing and regulatory compliance.

## Revenue streams and operational structure

The property-leasing business is the most straightforward. Pineapple procures properties—real estate with the right zoning, location, and characteristics to host licensed cannabis retail or cultivation—and leases them to licensed operators. This is a real-estate arbitrage: identify undervalued or available properties, negotiate favorable lease terms, and collect recurring lease payments. Margins are attractive because the landlord's cost basis is typically fixed while leases can command premiums from operators who have limited options. The business is recurring and relatively stable provided the underlying regulatory environment does not shift dramatically.

The Pineapple Wellness subsidiary operates a nationwide CBD retail business, selling hemp-derived cannabidiol products through online and in-store channels. CBD products occupy a regulatory gray zone in the United States: while cannabis containing high levels of THC (the intoxicating compound) remains federally illegal, hemp-derived CBD products are legal at the federal level if they contain less than 0.3 percent THC. This legality makes CBD a consumer product that can be sold through normal retail channels, shipped across state lines, and advertised more openly than cannabis itself. Margins on CBD products are generally healthy, but the market is increasingly crowded—every supplement brand, mainstream retailer, and convenience chain now offers CBD. Competition and commoditization are relentless pressures.

Pineapple Express Consulting provides cannabis business licensing and consulting services. As more states legalized cannabis and opened licensing systems, operators needed expert guidance on applications, regulatory requirements, compliance infrastructure, and operational setup. Early movers in consulting captured outsized returns. But as the market matured, legalization spread, and regulatory frameworks stabilized, demand for one-off consulting engagements cooled. The business has structural headwinds from commoditization and the fact that most licensing consulting is a project business, not recurring.

## Cyclical pressures and the regulatory pivot

Cannabis markets are extraordinarily cyclical. When a state first legalizes, prices are high, margins are fat, and everyone seeks a piece of the action. Operators rush in, competition drives prices down, and the market consolidates. Property leasing benefits in the early stages from scarcity and seller desperation, but as the market matures and real-estate becomes plentiful, lease rates compress. Consulting demand spikes on legalization and falls as the regulatory framework settles. CBD product margins follow the same compression pattern: early adopters pay premium prices for novelty; once it is mainstream, prices fall toward commodity levels.

A major threat is further federal legalization of cannabis. Should cannabis be rescheduled or deschedule at the federal level, the entire competitive landscape would shift. Banks would finally enter the market, capital would flow in massively, large corporations would move into cannabis cultivation and retail, and small operators like Pineapple would face competition from well-capitalized, operationally sophisticated companies. Conversely, Pineapple's property-leasing advantage depends on the current cannabis operators' difficulty accessing real estate—a difficulty created by federal prohibition and banking constraints. If those constraints eased, the advantage would erode.

Pineapple has signaled strategic expansion into functional mushroom products and cannabis retail management as of March 2025. Functional mushrooms (lion's mane, reishi, cordyceps, etc.) are marketed as cognitive and immune-support supplements. Like CBD, they sit in the nascent functional foods market and face increasing competition. The cannabis retail management offering suggests the company is attempting to offer operations and management services to cannabis retailers, going beyond just real-estate ownership. These are reasonable bets on growth, but they also signal that the core business is reaching saturation and pushing the company to diversify.

## Research and valuation cues

An investor studying Pineapple should examine the 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001654672) to understand the revenue contribution and profitability of each segment. What percentage of revenue comes from property leasing, from Pineapple Wellness CBD sales, and from consulting? What are the margins in each? Is the CBD business profitable or is it a growth play? How many properties does Pineapple own or control, what is the typical lease term and rent per property, and what is the occupancy rate? Are lease renewals easy to obtain or is there turnover risk?

Also critical: what is the company's capital structure and debt load? Real-estate businesses often lever their acquisitions; if Pineapple financed property acquisitions with debt, a downturn in cannabis operator profitability could force operators to break leases or default, leaving Pineapple with negative free cash flow. Watch the regulatory environment closely—any major federal legalization or state-level regulatory changes will reshape the business opportunity. Monitor the CBD market for signs of commoditization and margin compression. If functional mushrooms are a new growth vector, investors should understand the competitive positioning and whether Pineapple has any particular advantage in that market or is simply entering a crowded space. Finally, because the company trades on OTC markets, liquidity is thin and information is limited—dependence on SEC filings and management guidance is acute, and investors should be comfortable with the information gap.
