You can buy a genetic test online. Spit in a tube, mail it in, wait a few weeks, and get a report describing your genetic risk for certain diseases and conditions. [Prenetics Global](/pre-stock/) Ltd makes and sells those tests. The company operates in two parts: one side runs diagnostic testing services for cancer and other conditions; the other side serves consumers who want to know what their DNA says about their health and ancestry.

## What Prenetics does, in plain terms

Prenetics runs two businesses. The diagnostics side serves doctors and hospitals. When someone is suspected of having cancer, their tissue sample goes to Prenetics for genetic testing that helps identify which treatment will work best. These are precision oncology tests—using a patient's unique DNA profile to make treatment decisions. This side targets healthcare providers and their patients, selling to the system.

The consumer side is different. Prenetics sells direct-to-consumer genetic tests under two main brands: CircleDNA and IM8. CircleDNA offers whole-genome sequencing—reading your entire DNA code and reporting back on disease risks, ancestry, traits, and wellness insights. This is not a doctor ordering a test; this is an individual choosing to learn about themselves. Prenetics also sells stool-based DNA tests for early colorectal cancer screening. These appeal to people who want preventive health information without a doctor's referral.

Beyond genetics, Prenetics distributes sports nutrition products under the Europa brand name and sells health and wellness products under IM8. These appear to be lower-margin fulfillment and distribution businesses.

## The two different customers

The diagnostics customer is a hospital system or a cancer center. They need fast, reliable molecular testing to guide treatment. This is a B2B sale in a regulated environment. There are clear competitors, established relationships, and insurance reimbursement (or lack thereof) affects whether the service gets used.

The consumer customer is an individual buying curiosity, reassurance, or empowerment. A person ordering CircleDNA wants to understand their genetic health risk. A person buying an at-home colorectal cancer screening test wants early detection without the inconvenience of a doctor's office. Prenetics is selling peace of mind and information. This B2C business scales through marketing, user experience, and brand trust.

These two customer types have very different buying patterns, margins, and growth dynamics. Diagnostic testing is recurring but relationship-dependent. Consumer testing is higher-margin but customer-acquisition dependent.

## How Prenetics went public

Prenetics began as a private company founded in Hong Kong in 2014. In 2022, it went public through a [merger](/merger/) with an existing SPAC, Artisan [Acquisition](/acquisition/) Corp., founded by Adrian Cheng, a prominent Hong Kong investor. The combined company began trading on [Nasdaq](/nasdaq/) under the symbols PRE (common shares) and PRENW (warrants) in May 2022.

The SPAC merger gave Prenetics access to public capital markets, funding for growth, and the credibility of a Nasdaq listing. For early shareholders, it meant liquidity and the ability to trade their stake. For Prenetics, it meant capital to expand its testing labs, marketing, and consumer platforms.

## The market for genetic testing

Consumer genetic testing is no longer novel. Companies like AncestryDNA and 23andMe have normalized the idea of buying a DNA test online. But the market remains fragmented and competitive. Some players focus on ancestry; others on health; still others on wellness traits. Prenetics positions CircleDNA as a comprehensive offering—ancestry, health risks, and wellness traits in one test.

The diagnostics side is more specialized. Precision oncology testing is a growing field, but it is dominated by established players and faces pricing pressure from health systems negotiating lower reimbursement rates. Prenetics competes on speed, accuracy, and the breadth of its testing panels.

## Risks and pressures

Consumer genetic testing faces persistent headwinds. First, brand maturation: as the novelty of DNA testing wears off, customer acquisition becomes costlier. Growth depends on marketing and brand loyalty, not on the market discovering the product. Second, regulatory risk: genetic testing is lightly regulated in some countries and strictly regulated in others. The European Union, for instance, has tightened rules on direct-to-consumer genetics. Third, privacy concerns: people increasingly worry about genetic data being misused or sold. Prenetics must maintain trust and data security.

On the diagnostics side, the primary risk is reimbursement. If insurance companies and healthcare systems stop paying for precision oncology testing or reduce reimbursement rates, revenue falls. Prenetics also competes against larger, better-capitalized diagnostic companies with established hospital relationships.

Prenetics' profitability depends on getting unit economics right: keeping the cost to process each test low while customers pay enough to make it worthwhile. This is especially true for consumer tests, which are price-sensitive. If acquisition costs stay high and retention is weak, unit economics deteriorate.

## How to research Prenetics as an investment

Prenetics' SEC filings, located under CIK 0001876431, provide the official record. Focus on revenue by segment—how much comes from diagnostics vs. consumer testing, and which is growing faster. Watch gross margins; improving margins suggest better cost control or better-mix revenue. Track [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/); a cash-burning genetic testing company faces sustainability questions.

In earnings calls, listen for commentary on reimbursement rates, customer acquisition costs, and retention among repeat testers. These operational metrics reveal whether the business model is working. Also note management's strategy for international expansion; Prenetics was founded in Hong Kong and serves global customers, but the United States is its largest market. Growth outside the U.S. is still unproven.

Finally, watch for developments in genetic testing regulation and privacy law. A major regulatory shift—stricter data handling rules, mandatory genetic counseling, insurance reimbursement changes—could reshape the industry quickly.
