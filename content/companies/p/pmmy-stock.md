*A **defined-outcome [buffer ETF](/buffer-etf/)** is a rules-based fund that tracks the S&P 500 but truncates its returns — capping upside gain within a stated band each month and absorbing losses below a specific floor — in exchange for reducing volatility and the sting of downturns. PMMY, sponsored by Prudential Global Investment Management, locks in its buffer and cap on a May-to-May cycle.*

The core promise is economical: take the broad market, keep most of the upside (not all), give away less downside. Most investors understand this as a trade-off — buying stability on the way down by accepting a ceiling on the way up. The mechanics, though, are what matter. PMMY does not simply own the 500 stocks and hold them. Instead, it synthetically replicates the index through derivatives, primarily [call spreads](/call-spread/) and [put spreads](/put-spread/), that are settled and rebalanced monthly. On the first trading day of June, the hedges reset and the band resets with it. This monthly recontracting means PMMY is not a buy-and-hold fund in the traditional sense; it is a series of one-month contracts rolled forward, which makes it useful for specific [holding periods](/holding-period/) but not for multi-year passive indexing.

## The buffer, the cap, the reset cycle

PMMY's prospectus states the mechanics clearly: investors get a stated **buffer floor** (typically somewhere near 15 per cent downside protection) and a **cap** on upside (roughly 13–15 per cent gain, depending on market conditions at reset). Between those bands, it tracks the S&P 500 mark-for-mark. Outside them, gains are capped and losses are cushioned. The floor resets monthly because PGIM recalculates the hedge cost weekly and locks in new spreads on the first trading day of the following month. If the market falls 20 per cent in a month, PMMY falls only to its floor. If it rises 20 per cent, PMMY rises only to its cap. The typical investor using PMMY either holds for specific windows (say, May to May, aligning with the reset calendar) or rolls in and out as their view on volatility changes.

This structure makes PMMY appealing to investors who believe the broad market will advance over time but want to sleep better through the downturns. Retirees drawing income, trusts in drawdown phases, and tactical allocators often find defined-outcome funds fit naturally into a portfolio. The trade, though, is real: capping upside of a rising market is a genuine cost. Over a decade of strong gains, PMMY would lag a traditional [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) fund by 40 per cent or more, because every month it left money on the table.

## Costs, holdings, and how it is held

PMMY has an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) commensurate with active derivative management — not cheap relative to a plain S&P 500 ETF (which costs 0.03 per cent annually), but reasonable given the embedded hedging cost is substantial. The fund holds either the stocks of the S&P 500 or cash and derivatives that synthetically replicate them, depending on market conditions and the hedge structure in place at any given reset date. Holdings are not static. Investors checking the fund's holdings page will see the 500 stocks plus cash and a options or swap position; the exact ratio depends on when in the monthly cycle they look.

Liquidity is tight. PMMY trades on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) and the [secondary market](/secondary-market/) is modest — not illiquid, but spreads can be noticeable compared to the core S&P 500 ETF. For a buy-and-hold retail investor buying a small number of shares and holding through a calendar month, this is rarely an issue. For institutions moving large blocks, even the intraday spread deserves a look.

## The real limits and what regulators watch

Defined-outcome ETFs occupy a middle space in the regulator's view. They are not leveraged, not inverse, and not marketed as speculative instruments. They are transparent — PGIM publishes the buffer and cap daily. Yet they are derivatives-heavy, and that creates [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) in the options market itself. If volatility spikes dramatically in a single month, the cost to hedge may exceed what the fund can pay from its expense budget, forcing a buffer reduction or, in extreme cases, an early unwind.

The SEC has required these funds to publish pro forma returns and risk disclosures clearly, and most sponsor disclosures now include a clear explanation: "This is not the S&P 500. This is the S&P 500 with a cap and a floor." Investors who buy PMMY expecting it to rise as fast as the broad market in good years will be disappointed. Those who buy it as a defined-risk sleeve within a larger portfolio understand the trade and often find it worth the cost.

## How to research this fund

Start with PGIM's prospectus and fact sheet, which spell out the exact buffer percentage, cap, and reset date for the current cycle. Read the risk section carefully — it will note concentration risk in the derivatives market, the possibility of cap/buffer changes at reset, and any tax quirks (derivatives can generate unusual gains or losses). Check the fund's daily NAV and intraday price on NASDAQ; watch the spread when you trade. Look at what the underlying S&P 500 has done in the prior May-to-May period; PMMY will have underperformed by roughly the cap amount if the market rallied strongly, and outperformed if it fell within the buffer range. Finally, before committing a large sum, ask yourself: Am I comfortable forgetting about 13 per cent of a bull-market rally in order to dodge the first 15 per cent of a [bear market](/bear-market/)?
