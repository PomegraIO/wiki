*PMOC is a defined-outcome ETF that replicates the S&P 500 through a monthly options strategy resetting each October, offering investors a pre-set floor against losses and a ceiling on gains in exchange for a higher [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) than traditional indexing.*

> "I am buying the market, but not all of it." This is the core logic of PMOC and every buffer fund in PGIM's suite. You accept a cap on how much you can gain in a strong month in order to know exactly how much you can lose in a weak one.

## The October-reset cycle and its mechanics

PMOC is bound to the October calendar. On the first business day of each November, the prior year's synthetic hedge expires and PGIM enters a new contract with new parameters. For the next twelve months, the fund will track the S&P 500 within bounds locked in on that day. If [implied volatility](/implied-volatility/) is high, PGIM can afford a thicker buffer and a more generous cap; if volatility is low, the hedging cost is cheaper and the bounds tighten. This volatility-dependent reset structure is how PGIM manages the fund's economics across [market cycles](/market-cycle/).

An investor holding PMOC from November through the following October experiences one complete defined-outcome period. The buffer and cap are fixed for that year. If the S&P 500 falls 30 per cent in, say, February, PMOC falls only to its buffer (typically 12–15 per cent). If the market rallies 22 per cent in March through September, PMOC captures only to its cap (commonly 10–14 per cent). The truncation works both ways, and both ways are intentional. The fund is not meant to beat the market in strong years; it is meant to participate in it without the [tail risks](/tail-risk/).

## Why October, and what that means for holding periods

The October cycle is arbitrary — PGIM offers versions keyed to every month, each with its own reset date. Choosing PMOC is partly about the calendar convenience (if you align your annual review or tax year to October) and partly about the specific volatility environment in which the hedges were last set. An investor who believes October 2024 implied volatility captured the "true" cost of hedging the next year might have a small preference for PMOC over, say, PMNV, all else equal. In practice, the differences between month-cycle variants are typically small enough that the choice is often driven by convenience rather than analytical insight.

Holding PMOC across its reset boundary is a decision point. If you own PMOC on November 1st as the prior year's hedge expires, you seamlessly roll into the new twelve-month contract. If you own shares continuously, you will experience a series of monthly resets. Some investors deliberately buy and sell PMOC near reset dates if they believe the previous hedge was particularly generous or stingy; others simply buy and hold, treating each roll as automatic.

## Derivative structure and counterparty risk

PMOC achieves its buffer and cap through options contracts — primarily [call spreads](/call-spread/) (capping upside) and [put spreads](/put-spread/) (providing downside protection) — replicated synthetically across the S&P 500 constituents. The fund does not own the individual stocks in significant quantities; instead, it holds cash and a swaps and options book that track the index. This derivative-heavy structure is efficient from a cost perspective, but it introduces [counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/). If one of PGIM's major hedge counterparties (a large investment bank) fails during an extreme market stress, the fund's hedges could be compromised. The SEC requires PGIM to disclose this risk and manage it through collateral agreements, but the risk remains nonzero.

Daily valuations of PMOC reflect the marked value of both the equity index tracking component and the embedded options. On days of high implied volatility, the value of the put spreads (the downside insurance) rises, which can create days where PMOC rises in absolute terms even as the S&P 500 falls slightly — a counterintuitive outcome that surprises some investors. This is not an error; it is the geometry of the hedge.

## Costs and tax efficiency compared to other products

PMOC's expense ratio, typically around 0.79 per cent annually, is high relative to a plain [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) ETF (0.03–0.04 per cent) but modest relative to [actively managed funds](/actively-managed-fund/) (0.5–1.5 per cent). Much of that cost is the embedded cost of the options hedges, which would be otherwise invisible if PMOC were structured as an active [separately managed account](/separately-managed-account/). By holding it in fund form, investors pay a transparent all-in fee.

The tax picture is more complex. Because PMOC rebalances its hedges monthly and may realize gains and losses on rolled or unwound options, it typically generates short-term capital-gains distributions each year. A traditional S&P 500 index ETF, held passively, generates minimal distributions. For an investor in a high tax bracket using a taxable account, the higher annual distributions from PMOC can meaningfully drag on after-tax returns. In a tax-deferred account (401k, IRA), this is irrelevant.

## Who PMOC is for, and how to research it

PMOC is suited to investors with moderate risk tolerance who expect the [stock market](/stock-market/) to advance over time but want explicit protection against severe drawdowns. It works especially well as a core holding for retirees or endowments beginning systematic withdrawals — the known floor on losses is psychologically valuable. It is less suitable for investors with a long time horizon and high risk tolerance, for whom the opportunity cost of the cap outweighs the comfort of the buffer.

To evaluate PMOC, start with PGIM's prospectus and the current fact sheet, which state the exact buffer percentage and cap for the active October-to-November cycle. Model what the fund would have returned over the prior twelve-month period and compare it to the naked S&P 500; the shortfall (if the market rallied significantly) or the outperformance (if it fell) illustrates the hedge's real value or cost. Check the daily intraday spread on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) — tight spreads indicate demand and low transaction friction; wide spreads suggest trading in PMOC is harder. Finally, consider tax impact: run the math of distributions and capital gains if holding PMOC in a taxable account, and decide whether the tax drag is acceptable given your goal for the position.
