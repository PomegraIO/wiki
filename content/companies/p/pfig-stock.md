*The **Invesco Fundamental Investment Grade [Corporate Bond](/corporate-bond/) ETF (PFIG)** holds the debt issued by solid mid-to-large US corporations — the [bonds](/bond/) that fund factories, [acquisitions](/acquisition/), and operations. Unlike a market-cap-weighted bond index that tilts toward whoever has issued the most debt, PFIG selects individual bonds using fundamental credit analysis, favoring issuers with strong [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/) and stable cash flows.*

## What PFIG holds

PFIG's portfolio consists of investment-grade corporate bonds — debt issued by companies rated BBB– or higher by credit-rating agencies, meaning the companies are judged unlikely to default in the near term. These range from the bonds of mega-cap industrial firms (3M, Honeywell, [Caterpillar](/cat-stock/)) to mid-market financials, healthcare companies, and energy firms. The bonds carry [coupon rates](/coupon-rate/) (annual interest payments) typically in the 4–7% range, depending on the issuer's credit quality and the broader interest-rate environment.

The fund is actively managed within the investment-grade universe. Rather than holding every corporate bond in the market, Invesco's team selects issuers using fundamental criteria: balance-sheet strength ([debt-to-equity ratio](/debt-to-equity-ratio/), interest coverage), revenue stability, industry position, and management quality. This tilts the portfolio toward companies with fortress balance sheets and away from highly leveraged firms that happen to have large debt issuances.

## How fundamental selection differs from market-cap weighting

A market-cap-weighted corporate bond index holds bonds in proportion to the total amount of debt issued. This means if Company A has issued $10 billion of bonds and Company B has issued $1 billion, the index is 90% A and 10% B, regardless of credit quality. If Company A later weakens and issues another $10 billion to refinance aging debt, the index grows its A exposure further — exactly when A's risk is rising.

PFIG inverts this: the fund applies a credit screen and fundamental analysis to each issuer, then constructs a portfolio that balances credit quality across the holdings. The result is a portfolio that, in theory, is less likely to be overweight in deteriorating credits at exactly the moment they are deteriorating.

## The income and duration profile

PFIG is fundamentally a fixed-income fund, so its purpose is to deliver interest income plus any capital gains or losses as [interest rates](/interest-rate/) move. When interest rates fall, bond prices rise (because the coupons look more attractive relative to new issuances), and PFIG gains. When rates rise, bond prices fall, and PFIG loses. The fund's duration — a measure of interest-rate sensitivity — is typically moderate, reflecting a portfolio of bonds across the maturity spectrum.

Yields on PFIG have varied from around 3–5% in recent years, depending on the level of corporate bond yields overall. The fund distributes income monthly, making it attractive to investors seeking regular cash flow.

## Sector and issuer composition

PFIG holds a diversified mix of corporate bond issuers. Financials (banks, insurance) are ordinarily the largest sector weight, followed by industrials, healthcare, energy, and consumer staples. Utilities often have a meaningful allocation because their stable, regulated cash flows and lower default risk make them attractive to a fundamental-credit-focused fund. Technology and consumer discretionary are typically lighter, as firms in those sectors have more volatile cash flows and often carry higher debt loads.

Within the investment-grade universe, PFIG tends to tilt toward the higher-credit-quality end (issuers rated A or AA) rather than the lower-quality end (BBB) because stronger issuers offer more safety, even if yields are lower.

## Risk and credit considerations

PFIG carries [credit risk](/credit-risk/): if one of the major issuers in the portfolio deteriorates and its bonds are downgraded or default (rare, but possible), the fund's value falls. The portfolio is diversified across many issuers, so any single default is absorbed, but widespread defaults (triggered by a severe [recession](/recession/)) would hurt all holdings.

[Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) is also present. If the Fed hikes rates aggressively, newly issued corporate bonds offer higher coupons, making the older, lower-coupon bonds in PFIG's portfolio less valuable. A shareholder who sells before maturity would take a loss.

Liquidity in the underlying corporate bond market is generally good, but it is not as liquid as stocks. In market stress, [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) widen and trading can slow. PFIG's own trading on the exchange is liquid, but under stress the ETF's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) (NAV) can widen away from the price due to illiquidity in the underlying bonds.

## Yields, spreads, and economic sensitivity

PFIG's yield fluctuates with the level of corporate bond yields overall, which is driven by Fed policy, credit conditions, and investor risk appetite. When the Fed is hiking, corporate bonds typically offer higher yields and PFIG is more attractive on a forward-looking basis. When rates are falling, new bond purchases capture lower yields, and PFIG's forward income profile weakens.

[Credit spreads](/credit-spread/) — the extra yield corporations must offer above [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) to compensate for default risk — also move with economic conditions. In recessions, spreads widen (corporations must offer more yield to attract buyers) and bond prices fall. In expansions, spreads compress and bond prices rise. PFIG is exposed to this [spread risk](/spread-risk/) just like any corporate bond fund.

## Costs and tax implications

PFIG's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically 0.40–0.50%, moderate for an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) but higher than a passive corporate bond index ETF. The active management is the value proposition: if the fundamental selection process identifies stronger credits or avoids deteriorating ones more effectively than a market-cap-weighted approach, it can add return.

In taxable accounts, PFIG distributions are taxed as ordinary income (coupon interest). Bond funds are tax-inefficient in taxable accounts; they are ordinarily better held in tax-advantaged accounts (IRAs, 401k plans).

## How to research PFIG

Start with the [fund prospectus](/fund-prospectus/) and the current holdings file to see which companies and sectors are in the portfolio and how concentrated it is. Check the credit-rating breakdown: what percentage of the fund is rated AAA, AA, A, or BBB?

Monitor corporate bond spreads and credit-market conditions. PFIG's performance is tied to whether corporate credit is tightening or widening, which reflects Fed policy and economic outlook. Read the fund's quarterly fact sheet for information on new purchases and sales.

Also compare PFIB's yield and total return against passive corporate bond indices and competing actively managed corporate [bond ETFs](/bond-etf/). If the fundamental approach is working, PFIG should show tighter credit selection (fewer heavily downgraded issuers) and similar or superior returns relative to broader indices.
