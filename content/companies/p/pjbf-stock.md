PJBF is an exchange-traded fund that marries a concentrated, growth-tilted equity strategy with explicit screens for environmental and social impact. The fund targets companies the manager believes are positioned to solve major sustainability challenges — energy transition, waste reduction, access to healthcare and education — while also delivering above-market returns. It is an attempt to bridge the gap between investors who want portfolio [diversification](/diversification/) through an ETF structure but are unwilling to own traditional [index funds](/index-fund/) that hold the entire market without distinction.

## The underlying investment thesis

PJBF operates from the premise that companies solving environmental and social challenges are fundamentally better positioned for long-term outperformance than those ignoring them. The portfolio manager — PGIM Jennison, an investment firm owned by [Prudential Financial](/pru-stock/) — screens the US equity universe for candidates that meet specific sustainability criteria: reducing carbon emissions, improving resource efficiency, expanding access to affordable services, or strengthening governance and board diversity. The goal is not purity — the fund is not a strict ESG exclusion basket — but rather an intentional tilt toward businesses the manager believes have competitive advantages linked to sustainability.

Within that filtered universe, PJBF then builds a concentrated portfolio. Rather than holding a representative sample of the market, it selects a smaller number of highest-conviction positions. This concentration amplifies the potential upside of correct stock picks and the downside of mistakes; it is a bet that active selection can beat the broad market, not a bet that all sustainable companies outperform, but specifically that Jennison's stock pickers can identify the strongest ones.

## Holdings and the concentration trade-off

The fund typically holds between 50 and 100 securities, a meaningful reduction from the thousands of holdings in a total-market index fund. This raises both potential and risk. On the potential side, if the manager's theses are right, the concentrated positions mean larger gains than an indexed, diversified approach would deliver. On the risk side, a single wrong conviction — an expensive company that fails to grow into its valuation, a sustainability trend that does not materialize as expected — has more impact on the portfolio's return than it would in a 3,000-stock fund.

The sustainability screens create a natural bias toward certain sectors and away from others. Energy companies focused on fossil-fuel extraction are largely excluded; renewable-energy producers and efficiency-technology firms are overweighted. Healthcare and information-technology holdings tend to cluster around firms selling to underserved populations or using technology to lower costs. The resulting portfolio often looks quite different from a traditional [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) — lighter on old industrial companies and concentrated in newer, faster-growing businesses.

## Costs and liquidity

PJBF's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is moderate by active fund standards, typically a moderate absolute figure that reflects the cost of active management and trading. It is higher than a passive index ETF — which charges fractions of a percent — but lower than many actively managed [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/), because the ETF wrapper has no sales load and lower distribution costs. As an ETF listed on [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/), PJBF trades with transparent intraday prices during market hours; an investor can buy or sell shares mid-day at the market price rather than waiting for the end of trading day like a mutual fund investor.

Liquidity is good. PJBF sees steady trading volume sufficient to execute buy and sell orders at tight spreads, meaning an investor is unlikely to be penalized for the difference between the bid and asking price.

## The real risks: concentration and conviction

The primary risk is concentration itself. If Jennison's core conviction — that sustainability-screened, growth-tilted companies will outperform the broad market — fails to hold, the portfolio has no cushion of diversification to soften the blow. Periods in which the market favors large, stable, low-growth businesses (often correlated with rising [interest rates](/interest-rate/)) can be especially painful for a fund biased toward smaller, faster-growing firms.

A second risk is style bias. By filtering for growth and sustainability, PJBF is not a true broad-market holding; it is an expression of a specific market view. An investor holding it is implicitly betting not just on the companies within it, but on the durability of the growth-and-sustainability narrative itself. If the market shifts to favor value or energy-intensive industries, PJBF could underperform.

Third, the sustainability criteria themselves can drift or be questioned. Standards for what counts as "better future" or truly sustainable are contested and evolving, and a company that meets the screens today might lose favour as climate science or social priorities shift.

## Researching PJBF

The fund's prospectus and fact sheet lay out the exact sustainability criteria the manager applies and the investment philosophy in full. The fund's homepage provides quarterly holdings reports showing the specific companies held and their weightings. Any investor considering PJBF should compare its performance and holdings to a broad US equity index fund over multiple [market cycles](/market-cycle/), not just periods favourable to growth and sustainability; the concentrated, thematic nature means it is explicitly not meant to be held as a core portfolio holding but rather as a satellite position reflecting a specific conviction about the future of capitalism.
