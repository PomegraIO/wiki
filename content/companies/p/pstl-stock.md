Postal Realty Trust is a [real estate investment trust](/real-estate-investment-trust/) that owns properties leased to the United States Postal Service. It is the first publicly traded REIT to focus explicitly on postal facilities, turning what had been a fragmented and invisible market into an investable asset class. The company owns more than 2,300 properties across 49 states and operates as an internally managed REIT, meaning it maintains control of both its properties and its operations in-house rather than delegating to third parties.

## Why is the Postal Service's real estate worth investing in?

The USPS needs thousands of facilities to function. Post offices, distribution centers, processing hubs, and last-mile sorting facilities are the physical backbone of mail delivery. For decades these properties were owned piecemeal by local operators, franchisees, and property companies with no public way to invest in the sector as a unified whole. Postal Realty was created by Tony Weisman and others in 2021 to consolidate this [fragmented market](/fragmented-market/), buy existing facilities, and create a steady-income REIT from properties whose tenant is effectively the federal government.

## What makes Postal's tenant reliable?

The USPS is not a typical commercial tenant. It cannot simply relocate or go out of business. The Postal Service is constitutionally mandated to operate and is a quasi-governmental entity, which gives its lease obligations an unusual level of certainty. A private company might cut costs by closing underperforming locations; the USPS is legally required to maintain universal service, which means it cannot abandon vast swaths of the country. This creates a long-term, predictable base of rent for the properties Postal leases.

The company's 2,300-property portfolio was 99.8 percent occupied as of late 2025, with roughly 7.1 million net leasable square feet. The weighted average rental rate across the portfolio was about eleven dollars and eighty-eight cents per square foot, with last-mile and flex properties commanding about fourteen dollars per square foot and industrial properties renting for about four dollars per square foot.

## How does Postal make money, and how much is recurring?

Postal generates revenue almost entirely from rent. The company leases properties to the USPS under long-term net lease agreements, where the tenant pays rent plus many of the ongoing operating costs—property taxes, maintenance, insurance. This is the standard structure in real estate investing because it separates the landlord's asset-appreciation upside from the tenant's operational costs.

The bulk of Postal's revenue is the rent itself. Beyond its owned portfolio, the company generates additional revenue from third-party property management services, managing an additional 322 properties on behalf of other owners. This fee-based arm provides [diversification](/diversification/) away from USPS leasing alone and creates incentive alignment with other postal property owners in the market.

Postal has the cash flow characteristics of a mature, income-focused business: strong, predictable revenue from long-term leases, and the legal obligation to pay out most of its net income to shareholders as a REIT [dividend](/dividend/) to maintain its tax status. This makes it a vehicle for income rather than capital appreciation, appealing to investors seeking quarterly distributions from a government-backed tenant.

## Is there a growth story here, or is this purely a yield play?

Growth exists, though it is measured rather than explosive. The United States has approximately 25,000 USPS-leased properties across its territory, and Postal Realty owns roughly 2,300 of them—less than ten percent of the addressable market. The company's management has stated acquisition guidance in the $115 million to $125 million range for 2026, which reflects an ongoing effort to consolidate fragmented holdings into the public company.

Growth also comes organically as property values appreciate and rents reset over time. But the fundamental business is not built for hypergrowth; it is built for stability. An investor in a PSTL share is buying exposure to the USPS real estate sector's recurring cash flows, betting that mail delivery and postal facilities remain essential infrastructure for the foreseeable future.

## What are the genuine risks?

The single largest risk is that the USPS's role in American commerce fundamentally shrinks. If mail volume declines so sharply that the postal service closes vast numbers of facilities or downsizes significantly, Postal's tenant base erodes. The company has some insulation because the USPS is mandated to maintain service, but if decline accelerates, even a mandated service might reduce its footprint.

A second structural risk is interest-rate sensitivity. REITs borrow money to fund [acquisitions](/acquisition/) and operations, and higher rates increase debt costs, which compress distributions to shareholders. Postal is highly leveraged—typical for REITs—so changes in the cost of capital matter.

A third risk is concentration: Postal's tenant is singular. The company owns properties leased almost entirely to the USPS. Diversification is limited. If something fundamentally changes in the relationship or if the postal service becomes financially distressed, Postal has few other tenants to cushion the blow.

## How would a reader track this investment?

Start with Postal's quarterly 10-Q and annual 10-K filings (SEC CIK 0001759774), which detail the portfolio breakdown by property type, occupancy rates, and lease expirations. The earnings calls reveal management's thinking on acquisition pace, the USPS's changing mail volumes, and any strategic shifts in how the company deploys capital.

Key metrics to watch include [funds from operations](/funds-from-operations/) (FFO), a REIT-specific measure that adjusts net income for [depreciation](/depreciation/) and other non-cash items to show the cash available for distributions. The distribution yield—the annual dividend divided by share price—shows what income you receive, and trends in funds from operations growth reveal whether distributions are sustainable. Watch also the weighted average lease [expiration date](/expiration-date/); longer lease life suggests more revenue stability.

The mail-delivery industry itself is worth monitoring. USPS volume trends, changes in postal service policy, and any federal legislative moves around the Postal Service's finances or operations all ripple through Postal Realty's business. The company is not a bet on growth but a bet on continuity, so the continuity thesis is everything.
