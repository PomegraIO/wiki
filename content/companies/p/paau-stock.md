PAAU began as a simple idea: give investors a way to gain two times the daily return of [Sprott Physical Silver](/pslv-stock/) Trust (PSLV), which itself holds physical silver bullion. The fund uses leverage—borrowed capital—and daily rebalancing to achieve that 2X multiple. On any given day, if PSLV rises 1 percent, PAAU is engineered to rise roughly 2 percent. The tradeoff, which many leveraged-ETF investors discover too late, is volatility decay: over extended periods, especially in choppy markets, the compounding of daily returns deviates sharply from two times the long-term return of the underlying, often falling significantly short.

## The lineage: from Sprott to leverage

Sprott Physical Silver Trust is a [closed-end fund](/closed-end-fund/) that holds allocated physical silver bullion in secure vaults. PSLV is itself a way to gain exposure to silver without owning the metal directly—no storage hassle, no insurance, no vault fees. Its price tracks the spot price of silver fairly closely, with a small discount or premium depending on demand.

PAAU launched with the intent of giving traders and risk-tolerant investors a tool to amplify silver exposure. A silver bull who believes prices are headed meaningfully higher might use PAAU instead of PSLV to double down on that bet without having to buy twice as much outright. The 2X daily leverage appeals to short-term traders and tactical bets rather than buy-and-hold investors.

## How leveraged ETFs work, and where PAAU differs from a simple trade

PAAU uses derivatives—primarily swaps and futures—to synthetically recreate 2X exposure to PSLV. The mechanics are straightforward in principle: borrow money, invest it alongside investor capital in PSLV or PSLV derivatives, and capture twice the daily move. The fund rebalances daily (usually at market close) to maintain the 2X ratio. If PSLV outperforms, the fund scales back leverage slightly; if PSLV underperforms, it scales it up, keeping the daily multiplier consistent.

This daily reset is both the fund's feature and its built-in cost. On any single day, it works as advertised. But over multi-day periods with oscillating prices, the math breaks down. If PSLV rises 5 percent, falls 5 percent, then rises 5 percent again (a volatile path that leaves it close to flat), a 2X daily-reset fund will have suffered mark-to-market losses during the downturns, amplified by leverage, and will not fully recover those losses even when prices rebound—because the rebalancing locking in losses is permanent.

## Volatility decay and the silent tax

PAAU carries an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) in the 1 percent range annually, but the true cost to a buy-and-hold investor is far larger: volatility decay. In any market exhibiting normal volatility, holding a [leveraged ETF](/leveraged-etf/) for months or years will generate returns materially worse than two times the underlying's long-term return. The longer the [holding period](/holding-period/) and the higher the volatility, the worse the decay.

Consider a concrete example: PSLV oscillates between $25 and $30 over a year, ending at $27.50 (a 10 percent gain). An investor holding PSLV realizes a 10 percent return. An investor holding 2X PAAU experiences both positive and negative daily resets over that year, with the negative resets (days PSLV falls) amplified by 2X leverage. The compounded effect often leaves PAAU finishing with a 15 percent return instead of the naïve 20 percent expected, losing 5 percentage points to volatility drag—a silent tax extracted by the daily rebalancing math.

## The silver price bet embedded in PAAU

PAAU's returns depend entirely on the direction and volatility of silver prices. Silver is an industrial metal used in electronics, solar panels, and dentistry, and also a store of value held by [central banks](/central-bank/) and investors seeking [inflation](/inflation/) hedges. Silver prices are volatile—they swing 30–40 percent annually in many years—which makes silver attractive to traders and catastrophic for leveraged ETF holders.

The typical PAAU investor is betting on one of a few narratives: that inflation will accelerate and silver will rally as a store of value; that a stock-market crash will drive investors into commodities and precious metals; or that industrial demand for silver will surge. The problem with leveraging into that bet using a daily-reset ETF is that even if the thesis proves right over a multi-year period, PAAU will likely underperform 2X the gain because of compounding drag.

## Who PAAU is actually for, and who it hurts

PAAU is designed for day traders and swing traders betting on short-term silver moves—people who expect to hold the fund for days or weeks, not months or years. A trader convinced silver will rally 8 percent in the next two weeks might use PAAU to capture a 16 percent gain, booking profits before volatility drag has time to compound.

PAAU is toxic for long-term buy-and-hold investors. Someone who believes silver is heading to $100 an ounce and wants to hold for five years should simply buy PSLV, or silver futures, or physical metal—not PAAU. The daily rebalancing will silently erode their returns, and they will wonder why their 2X leveraged ETF did not deliver 2X the long-term gain.

## Risks beyond volatility decay

[Interest rates](/interest-rate/) affect leveraged ETFs because the cost of borrowing money to finance leverage depends on short-term rates. In a rising-rate environment, PAAU's cost of leverage increases, widening the drag beyond volatility decay alone. If the Fed is tightening, holding PAAU is fighting two headwinds: volatility decay plus the rising cost of leverage.

Silver itself is illiquid relative to stocks; during market panics, [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) widen and trading slows. PSLV, which underpins PAAU, can itself face redemption pressure or premium compression in stressed markets, transmitted to PAAU. Finally, Sprott Physical Silver Trust occasionally faces questions about vault transparency and insurance; any loss of confidence in PSLV's management would cascade to PAAU.

## Using PAAU responsibly or not at all

PAAU can serve as a tactical tool for experienced traders making explicit short-term bets on silver volatility. But it is often sold to retail investors as a long-term silver exposure, which is a misuse of the product. Anyone considering PAAU should first understand volatility decay by running a simulation or consulting a financial advisor. A better approach for most investors seeking silver exposure is a non-leveraged silver ETF or a direct holding of PSLV; the drag-free return makes the lower nominal return irrelevant.

For research, check the fund's semi-annual reports for leverage levels and holdings. Understand the spot price of silver by tracking futures or major commodity news (CNBC, Bloomberg). Note that PAAU's daily movements often diverge from a simple 2X calculation, especially over longer periods. Finally, set a holding-period expectation before buying—if it is longer than a month, reconsider whether you should own PAAU at all.
