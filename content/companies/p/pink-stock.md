*The Simplify Health Care ETF (ticker PINK) is an actively managed exchange-traded fund that invests in a concentrated portfolio of U.S. healthcare companies — pharmaceutical manufacturers, medical-device makers, healthcare service providers, and biotech firms — selected for their valuations, [dividend](/dividend/) potential, and secular tailwinds. It does not track an index, instead relying on a fund manager's stock-picking judgment to outperform the broader healthcare sector.*

> "Healthcare is not a cyclical sector — it is essential and aging."

The fund is built on the premise that healthcare demand is structural, not cyclical. Ageing populations in developed economies, advancing therapies for chronic disease, and rising per-capita healthcare spending create a runway for earnings growth that the [stock market](/stock-market/) does not always price in fairly. Simplify's manager aims to identify companies within that sector that are undervalued or offering attractive income, then weight them selectively.

## What an actively managed healthcare fund does differently

Most healthcare exposure comes through passive [index funds](/index-fund/) that hold the whole sector. PINK inverts that approach: the manager is empowered to hold only 30 to 60 stocks, overweight her conviction bets, and leave out companies she views as overpriced or facing structural headwinds. This concentration is a double-edged sword. It can outperform an index in years when the manager's selections prove prescient — identifying a drug company before a successful clinical trial, or a medtech firm before a new technology gains adoption. But it also means underperformance when the manager's bets are wrong or when the cheapest healthcare stocks happen to underperform the flashiest growth stories.

A second characteristic is the emphasis on dividend income. Many large pharmaceutical and healthcare stocks pay substantial dividends — a reflection of their mature, cash-generative nature. PINK tilts toward these income payers, making it attractive to investors seeking a steady yield alongside potential price appreciation. That focus also shapes the portfolio's risk: stable, dividend-paying companies tend to be larger and lower-volatility than high-growth biotech, so the fund carries less volatility than a full healthcare sector index would.

## The healthcare sector through the fund's lens

The fund draws from across the healthcare landscape. Large-cap pharmaceutical companies — whose business models centre on bringing drugs to market, managing patent cliffs, and extracting pricing power — form a core holding. Medical-device makers like Johnson & Johnson's devices division, [Boston Scientific](/bsx-stock/), and [Zimmer Biomet](/zbh-stock/) serve hospitals and surgeons and enjoy recurring revenue from consumables and replacement cycles. Healthcare service providers, insurers, and pharmacy benefit managers represent another layer. Biotech companies, smaller and often pre-profitable, appear more selectively; PINK favours the larger, more established biotech firms over early-stage names.

What unites these holdings is that healthcare spending is largely non-discretionary. Patients do not delay cancer treatment because the market is in a drawdown. Hospitals do not cancel hip replacements. Insurers still process claims. This structural demand gives the sector a relative resilience that energy, consumer discretionary, and financials lack. It also means pharmaceutical pricing power — the ability to raise drug prices faster than [inflation](/inflation/) — is a real competitive advantage, though it invites persistent regulatory scrutiny.

## Valuation and selectivity as the edge

Actively managed healthcare funds live or die by their ability to spot mispricings. The healthcare sector includes hundreds of publicly traded companies, and the market does not price them all with equal efficiency. A manager might find that a well-established drug company has fallen out of favour despite steady earnings growth, making it cheap relative to peers. Or she might identify a small-cap device maker about to launch a new product that will drive years of growth before the market has priced it in. PINK's returns depend on whether such calls succeed more often than chance alone would predict.

That selective approach also means the fund can tilt away from sectors that are consensus-expensive. If the market has bid up all the high-profile biotech names to valuations that assume perfection, PINK might hold fewer of them and overweight cheaper, lower-growth names that offer value. This contrarian stance has historically worked well in periods when expensive growth stocks fell sharply, but it underperforms when growth is in favour and investors are willing to pay for it.

## Costs and transparency

The fund carries an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) in line with other actively managed ETFs — typically in the 0.5% to 0.75% range — which is higher than a passive healthcare index ETF but lower than a traditional healthcare [mutual fund's](/mutual-fund/) fee might be. Active management has a cost, but an ETF structure keeps trading expenses and tax inefficiency below what a mutual fund incurs, a modest structural advantage.

The fund is also transparent in the regulatory sense: it discloses its holdings daily, so investors can see exactly what stocks the manager holds at any moment. That transparency is a feature of the ETF structure that traditional [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) do not offer. It allows investors to scrutinize the manager's choices and assess whether they are comfortable with the positioning.

## Risks specific to active healthcare management

The biggest risk is underperformance. If the manager's stock picks do not outpace the broader healthcare index, the fee eats the difference immediately. Healthcare is a sector where index funds are genuinely good — the index captures the structural demand trend and the [diversification](/diversification/) benefit. A manager has to meaningfully outperform to justify her fee, and not all do.

A secondary risk is concentration. PINK's smaller portfolio of 30 to 60 stocks means a single bad call — a drug that fails trial, a device maker that loses market share to a rival, a company that cuts its dividend — affects the fund more acutely than it would in a diversified index. Investors comfortable with that can benefit from higher returns in good environments; those seeking defensive stability should consider a broad index instead.

## How to research this fund

Begin with the fund's latest fact sheet and holdings list on the Simplify website. Read the manager's commentary on why she is overweighting certain pockets of healthcare and underweighting others. Compare PINK's holdings to those of a broad healthcare index ETF and ask: are the stocks she is excluding now performing better or worse? That historical lens reveals whether her selectivity has been an advantage.

Next, examine the [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) and the fund's distribution history. Income is central to the pitch, so track whether the yield is stable, growing, or declining. Run a three-year performance comparison against a passive healthcare index — it will show whether active management has paid for itself.

Finally, consider your own view of healthcare valuations and the sector's direction. If you believe the market has broadly misprice a category of healthcare stocks — say, it has shunned profitable generics makers in favour of unprofitable biotech — then an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) might capture that view for you. If you believe the market prices these stocks fairly on average and you prefer low-cost diversification, a passive index is the better choice.
