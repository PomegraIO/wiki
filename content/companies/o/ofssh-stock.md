OFS Capital Corporation operates as a [Business Development Company](/business-development-company/) (BDC) regulated under the Investment Company Act, with the primary mission to deploy capital as debt and equity investments to small and mid-sized companies that lack efficient access to traditional banking and capital markets. The OFSSH ticker represents the company's preferred shares—a distinct security from the common equity—which carry a fixed [liquidation preference](/liquidation-preference/) and specific [dividend](/dividend/) terms. Like the broader OFS Capital platform, the preferred shares participate in the company's earnings through dividends, but with lower volatility and priority over common equity in a [liquidation](/liquidation/) scenario.

## The BDC lending engine

OFS Capital's core business operates through several interconnected segments, each designed to address gaps in the small and mid-sized business financing market. The company originates and manages [debt financings](/debt-financing/)—secured and unsecured loans, first-lien and second-lien structures, revolving credit facilities—to portfolio companies typically valued between fifty million and five hundred million dollars. These are fundamentally lending operations: the company deploys capital, earns interest, charges fees, and either holds the debt to maturity or sells it into [secondary markets](/secondary-market/).

The **senior secured lending segment** provides first-lien debt to portfolio companies, often backed by collateral (equipment, real estate, receivables). These loans carry lower [interest rates](/interest-rate/) than junior debt because they have priority in repayment, and they are considered lower-risk. A first-lien loan might yield eight to eleven percent annually, depending on borrower credit quality, market conditions, and loan structure.

The **subordinated debt segment** is where OFS Capital accepts higher risk for higher returns. Second-lien loans, mezzanine debt, and other junior structures are repaid only after senior creditors are satisfied, so they carry higher interest rates—often in the thirteen to seventeen percent range—to compensate for that extra risk. If the borrower gets into trouble, second-lien holders often lose principal, while first-lien holders recover most of their money.

The **equity and equity-linked investments** segment represents a smaller piece of the portfolio. OFS Capital occasionally takes equity stakes or earns upside through warrants and conversion rights embedded in debt structures. These positions can generate outsized returns if a portfolio company succeeds, but they also introduce illiquidity and [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/).

## Investment strategy and portfolio management

OFS Capital's investment committee targets middle-market companies across multiple industries—technology, healthcare, business services, consumer discretionary, manufacturing, and others. The company performs underwriting on each loan opportunity, assessing the borrower's [cash flow, management](/cash-flow-management-personal/) quality, market position, and ability to service debt. It aims to avoid highly leveraged borrowers or unstable industries, though by definition the company is lending to smaller, less creditworthy entities than a traditional bank would finance.

The company typically holds loans for three to five years, though some are longer-dated and others sold earlier. The portfolio is actively managed, meaning OFS Capital monitors borrower performance, renegotiates terms when needed, works out troubled loans, and exits successful investments when returns justify a sale. Active management reduces losses and improves overall returns compared to a passive buy-and-hold approach, but it is also resource-intensive and requires experienced credit professionals.

Geographic and industry [diversification](/diversification/) is built into the strategy to reduce concentration risk. No single borrower should represent too much of the portfolio, and no single industry should dominate either. This diversification does not eliminate [credit risk](/credit-risk/)—in a severe [recession](/recession/), defaults will rise across all industries and geographies—but it spreads risk across many smaller exposures rather than concentrating it.

## The preferred shares and capital structure

OFS Capital issues both common and preferred equity to fund its lending business. The preferred shares (OFSSH) are senior to common shares in a liquidation and carry a stated liquidation preference—typically around twenty-five dollars per share. This means preferred shareholders must be paid their stated value before common shareholders receive anything. The preferred shares also carry a fixed dividend rate, which provides more income certainty than common shares (whose dividends fluctuate with company earnings).

The trade-off is that preferred shares have limited upside appreciation. If OFS Capital's portfolio performs exceptionally well and the common shares appreciate significantly, the preferred shares' returns are capped by their fixed dividend and stated liquidation preference. Preferred shareholders are essentially trading upside potential for downside protection and income certainty.

From OFS Capital's perspective, issuing preferred shares is a way to raise capital with a fixed cost. If the company can earn higher returns on its loans than the cost of the preferred dividend, it creates leverage—deploying the preferred capital to earn spreads. However, if the company's credit losses or operating costs rise, the preferred dividend is still owed before anything flows to common shareholders, which can constrain management flexibility.

## Operational dynamics and spread economics

The fundamental spread—the difference between what OFS Capital earns on its loan portfolio and what it costs to fund that portfolio—is the core driver of economic returns. If the average loan yields eleven percent and the company's cost of capital (a blend of preferred dividends, common equity returns, and debt funding) is six percent, the spread is five percentage points. Applied to a one-billion-dollar portfolio, that spread would generate fifty million dollars in pretax income, which would be paid out as dividends after operating expenses and loan loss provisions.

Operating expenses include personnel (investment professionals, credit analysts, compliance and legal), office costs, and fees to external managers or advisors. These expenses are fixed to a degree—you need a certain amount of infrastructure to manage a portfolio—so they create operating leverage (a larger portfolio spreads fixed costs over more assets, improving percentage returns). However, expenses can also creep up if the company grows rapidly without controlling costs.

Loan losses are both predictable and surprise-driven. The company maintains a reserve for expected losses based on historical charge-off rates and the quality of the current portfolio. But unexpected deterioration—a recession, a major borrower failure, or a deterioration in portfolio quality—can cause actual losses to exceed the reserve, which would reduce distributions.

## Risks specific to preferred shares and the BDC structure

**[Refinancing risk](/refinancing-risk/).** If OFS Capital's preferred shares were issued at a fixed dividend that is now below market rates (because overall interest rates have risen), the company might face pressure to refinance them at higher rates. Alternatively, if market conditions worsen, the company might struggle to refinance maturing preferred shares, which could create financial pressure.

**[Credit cycle](/credit-cycle/) vulnerability.** When the economy weakens, BDCs typically experience rising loan losses. If OFS Capital's losses mount faster than expected, the company might cut its preferred dividend to preserve capital. Preferred shareholders would lose the income they were relying on, and the share price would likely fall.

**Interest-rate environment.** OFS Capital's returns are sensitive to where interest rates go. Higher rates increase the company's funding costs (squeezing spreads on variable-rate debt) and can reduce the rates the company can charge new borrowers (because borrowers shop for cheaper alternatives). Lower rates are better for new lending but worse if the company has already locked in higher-yielding loans.

**Market sentiment toward BDCs.** Preferred shares, like all BDC securities, can be hit by broad market sentiment. If investors become pessimistic about the lending environment or the BDC sector as a whole, valuations compress, spreads widen (meaning it becomes more expensive for BDCs to raise capital), and existing shares underperform regardless of the underlying portfolio quality.

## How to research OFS Capital preferred shares

Start with the company's most recent quarterly and [annual reports](/10-k/), which disclose the composition of the loan portfolio, credit quality metrics (like the non-accrual rate), and the portfolio yield (the weighted average interest rate earned on outstanding loans). Compare the portfolio yield to the company's cost of capital to understand the spread dynamics.

Key metrics to monitor include the [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) (NAV) per preferred share, which reflects the underlying value of the assets backing the shares; the dividend coverage ratio (whether earnings are sufficient to support the stated dividend, or whether capital is being returned instead); and the charge-off rate (actual loan losses relative to portfolio size). A rising charge-off rate is a warning sign.

The 10-K (SEC CIK 0001487918) details the preferred shares' terms (dividend rate, liquidation preference, call provisions, any specific restrictions). Understand when the shares are callable—if the company can redeem them, your upside is capped.

Track the broader lending environment and interest rates. Watch the [Federal Reserve's](/federal-reserve/) policy direction because it affects both the company's funding costs and the spreads it can earn. In a high-rate environment, spreads may be wide, but defaults also rise; in a low-rate environment, spreads narrow, but defaults fall. The ideal scenario for a preferred shareholder is modest, stable growth with strong portfolio performance—which is easier to wish for than to time.
