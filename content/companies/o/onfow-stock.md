Onfolio Holdings operates as a holding company that acquires and consolidates independent business-to-business creative and digital agencies into a single, technology-enabled platform. Rather than building new agencies from scratch, the company buys established firms with client relationships and revenue, then layers shared infrastructure, automation, and artificial intelligence across them to reduce costs and unlock new revenue streams. This roll-up model is fundamentally a capital play: it requires raising money to acquire agencies, proving that AI and process integration can improve their economics, and then using improved cash generation to fund the next wave of acquisitions.

## How Onfolio funds itself and grows

The company's strategy for capital deployment is explicit and directional. In 2025, Onfolio secured a $300 million convertible note financing facility, with approximately $6 million drawn to date. Convertible notes are a hybrid instrument — they function as debt but with the optionality to convert into equity, which lets a capital-intensive growth company raise money without immediately diluting existing shareholders. The choice of convertible debt signals management's confidence that the underlying business will generate enough cash flow to service the debt without conversion, or that future equity will be worth enough that conversion is acceptable.

Beyond traditional financing, Onfolio initiated a digital asset treasury strategy, holding approximately $2.3 million in Bitcoin, Ethereum, and Solana as of 2025. This is distinct from operating cash and reflects a separate capital allocation decision: holding cryptocurrency rather than fiat currency or Treasury securities. The stakes generate additional returns through staking rewards, adding a yield component to the balance sheet.

The strategic intent is self-funding through operational improvement and accretive acquisitions. Management has published a detailed roadmap to reach cash-positive status by disciplined cost control, improving the margins of existing agencies, and acquiring only businesses that increase earnings per share in their first year of ownership. This is the conventional playbook for roll-up companies: each acquisition must be a accretive from day one, or it erodes shareholder value.

## The five agency businesses and their roles

Onfolio's operating assets are five B2B creative and digital agencies, each serving different vertical markets and client types. The company is consolidating these businesses onto a unified technology platform rather than operating them as independent subsidiaries. This consolidation is meant to create shared services — centralized sales and marketing, unified back-office operations, common data infrastructure, and AI-driven tools that multiply the output of small teams.

The theory is that a $10 million agency run independently can support perhaps three salespeople, a handful of creative staff, and a few project managers. But if that same agency is one of five on a shared platform, it shares the cost of the underlying technology, benefits from peer learning, and gains access to AI tools that handle routine work (copywriting, design variations, scheduling) that would normally require headcount. In theory, that leverage lets the combined entity serve more clients with the same people, or the same clients with fewer people.

Revenue in 2025 reached $10.73 million, up 36% from the prior year. But the growth comes partly from acquisitions and partly from organic expansion within existing agencies. Disentangling these is crucial for understanding whether the consolidation thesis is working — whether Onfolio is buying its way to higher headline numbers or building real operating leverage.

## The challenge: converting consolidation into margin

The simplest way to grow via acquisitions is to buy businesses, keep them separate, and add their revenue to the parent's total. But that creates no leverage; it just makes you larger. Onfolio's thesis is that active consolidation — moving clients and projects onto shared infrastructure, retiring duplicate tools, consolidating back offices — can improve margins across the platform.

In 2025, operating profit across the portfolio rose from approximately $1.4 million to $1.8 million. That is modest growth, and it is below the growth rate of revenue. The company also remains unprofitable at the net level, with net losses of $3.06 million in 2025. This is common for acquisition-stage companies — you are spending cash on integration, overhead, corporate costs, and financing expense faster than individual agencies can improve margins. The question is whether that investment in infrastructure and consolidation eventually pays off in markedly higher margins than the agencies would have generated independently.

EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization — a proxy for cash generation from operations) turned positive in 2025 at $151 thousand, versus a loss of $588 thousand in 2024. That is directionally encouraging. It means the underlying agencies, stripped of corporate overhead and financing costs, are approaching breakeven on a cash basis. But the magnitude is still small relative to revenue.

## Capital allocation and the path to independence

The leverage of the AI thesis is where Onfolio's capital story lives. If machine learning tools can materially reduce the effort required to produce creative work, or to manage client projects, that reduces future headcount needs and improves margins without shrinking the revenue base. It is not guaranteed, and it requires the company to invest continuously in building or integrating these tools. But if it works, it changes the unit economics of the entire platform.

The convertible note provides runway: $300 million in capacity lets the company fund acquisitions and development without needing immediate profitability. But convertibles are not free money. If the company does not reach profitability before the notes mature (typically five to seven years), the debt comes due and either needs to be refinanced or converted into equity, diluting existing shareholders.

Management's public roadmap frames the core question: can the company discipline itself to acquire only agencies where integration is feasible, where the owner is incentivized to stay and lead consolidation, and where AI tools create defensible margin expansion? If yes, the company reaches self-funding and compounds. If no — if it acquires indiscriminately or if consolidation fails to materialize — it will run through capital, debt will come due, and the model breaks.

## Research pathways

The 10-K and quarterly 10-Q filings show the detailed segment performance of each agency, the pace of acquisitions, and the burn rate of cash. Watch the trajectory of EBITDA, the gross margin trend within each business unit, and management's commentary on which AI investments are translating to actual cost reductions or revenue expansion. The earnings calls, held quarterly, are where color on integration progress emerges: how many agencies are now on the shared platform, whether clients are consolidating their relationships across multiple Onfolio agencies, and whether team members are being redeployed productively across units.

Key metrics to track are revenue growth rate (organic versus acquired), EBITDA margin expansion, and drawdown against the convertible facility. As with any capital-intensive consolidation play, the margin story matters more than the top-line number — acquisitions are straightforward if you have cash, but only good if they improve the business fundamentally.
