## What the fund tracks and why it exists

OILK holds futures contracts on West Texas Intermediate crude oil, the benchmark price for light, sweet crude produced in the United States. Most crude-oil investments come through either direct futures trading (where profits are taxed as Section 1256 commodities) or through master limited partnerships and other vehicles that issue K-1 tax forms — documents that complicate filing and require detailed basis tracking.

ProShares created OILK to sidestep that friction. By holding crude oil futures inside a standard ETF wrapper, the fund delivers commodity-price exposure in a format taxed as a regular security, not a partnership interest. For US taxpayers, that means no K-1 form; you simply report the gain or loss on [Schedule D](/schedule-d/) like any other security. That simplicity has made it attractive to retirement accounts and individual investors who want oil exposure but do not want to manage the administrative complexity of direct futures positions or partnership tax reporting.

## How the fund works — structure and strategy

The fund operates by holding a rolling portfolio of crude oil futures contracts rather than physical barrels. Each contract tracks a standardized quantity of [WTI crude](/wti-crude/) for delivery at a future date. As contracts approach their expiration, ProShares sells them and buys newer, further-out contracts to maintain continuous exposure — a practice called rolling the contracts.

This rolling creates a structural headwind known as **[contango](/contango/) risk**. When crude prices are in contango (future contracts cost more than near-term ones), the fund sells cheaper contracts and buys more expensive ones every month, which can drag returns below the current spot price of crude. Conversely, in [backwardation](/backwardation/) (future contracts are cheaper than near-term ones), the rolling process can aid returns. Neither is guaranteed; the fund's month-to-month performance versus the current oil price depends on the shape of the crude curve at each rebalancing point.

OILK rebalances daily, which means it sells or buys contracts each trading day to keep the fund's crude exposure consistent. This daily activity keeps the fund liquid and easy to trade during market hours, but it also creates small cumulative costs that shave a bit off pre-expense returns.

## Costs and liquidity

The fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is low — typically in the range of 0.5% or less annually. For a [commodity ETF](/commodity-etf/), this is reasonable, though it should be weighed against the contango drag mentioned above. Investors should expect total costs (including the indirect impact of rolling contracts) to be somewhat higher in contango environments and lower in backwardation.

Liquidity is solid. OILK trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) with sufficient daily volume to allow entry and exit without wide [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) for most trade sizes. The fund is large and established enough that it does not have the liquidity quirks that plague niche or newly launched ETFs.

## Real risks — what to watch

The obvious risk is crude oil itself — prices move sharply on geopolitical events, production surprises, demand shocks, and interest-rate changes. An investor holding OILK is taking a concentrated bet on the direction and volatility of a single commodity. Unlike a diversified portfolio, there is no buffers from holdings in [bonds](/bond/), stocks, or other assets.

Contango drag is the second structural risk. If crude remains in contango for years, the cumulative cost of rolling contracts can result in poor performance even if the current oil price stays flat. This is not catastrophic, but it is real, and it deserves more attention than marketing often gives it.

Finally, although K-1 avoidance was the fund's original design advantage, investors should verify current tax treatment with a tax adviser. Rules change, and the tax benefit depends on the fund's exact holdings and the investor's jurisdiction.

## Who this fund is for

OILK suits investors who want direct crude oil exposure as a tactical or strategic allocation, prefer the simplicity of an ETF over futures or partnerships, and want to avoid K-1 tax reporting. It is not a bet on energy companies (it holds no stocks), nor is it insurance against [inflation](/inflation/) (though oil prices do move with some economic factors). It is pure commodity exposure, suitable for:

- Investors who believe crude prices will rise and want a transparent, tax-efficient vehicle to express that view.
- Portfolio managers looking to add a commodity allocation without the operational overhead of futures accounts.
- Retirement accounts that cannot easily hold futures or receive K-1 forms.

## How to research and monitor OILK

Start with ProShares' fund fact sheet and prospectus, which detail the exact contract strategy, the expense ratio, and any recent changes to the rolling methodology. The prospectus will also explain how the fund handles contango and backwardation in practice.

Watch the shape of the WTI futures curve — you can see this on any major futures platform or commodity-data service. If crude is in contango, the fund will be paying a steady drag; if in backwardation, there is a tailwind. This is not a reason to buy or sell, but it is useful context for understanding whether OILK's return versus spot crude will be a help or a headwind in the months ahead.

Monitor crude's underlying drivers: inventory levels, OPEC production decisions, US shale output, global demand forecasts, and geopolitical risk. OILK will move with these factors, and staying aware of them helps an investor understand whether the position is playing out as intended.
