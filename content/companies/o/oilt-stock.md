*The **Texas Capital Texas Oil Index ETF** (OILT) is an exchange-traded fund built to track companies engaged in oil and gas extraction and production in Texas — offering investors regional and sector-specific exposure to the upstream arm of the energy industry.*

## Inception and the Texan energy thesis

The Texas Capital Texas Oil Index ETF was conceived during a period when energy investors sought more granular exposure to specific regions rather than the broad energy sector or commodity futures. Texas, home to the Permian Basin, Eagle Ford Shale, and the Gulf of Mexico shallow-water fields, is the epicentre of US crude production. The fund's creators designed OILT to give investors a focused way to gain equity exposure to the companies that extract and produce oil and gas in that region, rather than holding a diversified energy index or investing in refiners, pipeline operators, or service companies.

The fund's structure reflected a specific investment thesis: that Texas-based oil production companies would continue to be central to US energy security, that the Permian Basin would remain prolific, and that investors willing to concentrate their energy allocation in a single region could capture the upside of Texan upstream growth. Unlike a broad energy ETF (which might include refiners, utilities, or international majors), OILT honed in on the extraction companies themselves — the producers whose profits rise and fall most directly with crude prices.

## Evolution and market positioning

As the energy landscape has shifted, OILT has remained focused on its original thesis: Texas-based oil and gas extractors. The fund's holdings typically include independent oil and gas producers operating in the Permian, as well as companies with significant Texas operations. Over time, the composition has changed with industry consolidation, bankruptcies during oil price downturns, and the entry of new players into Texas shale.

The fund sits in an unusual corner of the market — narrower than a broad energy [sector ETF](/sector-etf/), but broader than a single-stock position. It captures exposure to a geography and an industry segment rather than a specific commodity (like OILK) or a broad theme. This makes OILT useful for investors building a deliberate energy allocation, but it also means the fund is less liquid and often less visible than large, broad-based competitors.

## Current holdings and structure

OILT typically holds a portfolio of Texas-based oil and gas exploration and production companies — often referred to as E&P companies. These are equity stakes in corporations that own reserves, drill wells, and sell the [crude oil](/crude-oil/) and [natural gas](/natural-gas/) they produce. The fund is not leveraged or inverse; it is a straightforward long-only equity position in these companies.

Holdings tend to include mid-cap and smaller independent producers rather than mega-cap integrated energy companies. This means individual stock risk is more pronounced than in a diversified energy ETF, and a single company's operational problem — a dry well, a regulatory setback, or a [merger](/merger/) — can move the fund's value noticeably. Turnover in the index is typically managed to keep costs down, though the exact rebalancing rules are detailed in the fund's prospectus.

## Costs, liquidity, and trading

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is modest for a sector ETF, typically in the mid-0.5% to 1% range annually, though the exact figure should be verified in the fund's current fact sheet. Trading volume is lighter than mainstream broad-market or energy-sector ETFs, which can mean wider [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) for large trades, particularly during periods of low market interest in energy stocks.

Since OILT tracks a defined index of Texas oil companies, the fund's holdings are relatively fixed after index-rebalancing dates. This creates a certain predictability — investors know what they own — but it also means there is no active manager adjusting the portfolio in response to oil-market dynamics or company-specific news.

## The risks of regional and sector concentration

The most obvious risk is commodity exposure. OILT holds equities in oil-producing companies, so it moves with crude oil prices. When oil is weak, these stocks tend to weaken; when oil rallies, they tend to rally. An investor in OILT is making a directional bet on the global crude market.

The second risk is operational. Oil companies face drilling risk (wells may not produce as expected), reserve replacement risk (they must find new oil to replace what they produce), regulatory risk (Texas regulates drilling, and the US government sets export policy), and commodity-market risk. A fund concentrated in a single region and industry has no buffer from [bonds](/bond/), consumer stocks, or other assets.

Finally, OILT's lighter trading volume can make entry and exit more costly than they appear, particularly for large positions. During periods of market stress, liquidity can dry up entirely. The fund is best suited to long-term positions, not frequent trading.

## Who OILT is for and how to research it

OILT appeals to investors with a specific view: that Texas oil and gas producers will outperform, or who want a pure equity play on Texan energy production (as opposed to the [commodity ETF](/commodity-etf/) OILK). It is suitable for:

- Portfolio managers building a deliberate energy allocation and wanting Texas-specific exposure.
- Investors bullish on shale production and the Permian Basin in particular.
- Those seeking equity upside from oil companies rather than commodity price exposure.

To research OILT, start by reviewing the fund's prospectus and index-methodology document, which name the exact companies included. Then familiarize yourself with the major holdings — typically names like independent E&P companies operating in the Permian. Read their [annual reports](/10-k/) and earnings calls to understand reserve trends, cash costs, and capital allocation.

Monitor crude oil prices, Texas regulatory trends, and any announcements about mergers or bankruptcies among the fund's constituents. Unlike a diversified energy index, OILT's performance is driven both by crude prices and by the specific health of the Texas oil companies it holds. A crude price rally means little if the fund's holdings are simultaneously dealing with operational problems or production declines.
