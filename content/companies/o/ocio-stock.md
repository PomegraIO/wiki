*ClearShares OCIO ETF holds the diversified portfolio that a professional outsourced chief investment officer would construct for a mid-market institutional investor — a blend of US equities, international stocks, fixed income, and alternative strategies arranged to balance growth and stability without requiring active daily trading.*

## The OCIO premise

An Outsourced Chief Investment Officer, or OCIO, is a professional or firm hired by a pension fund, endowment, or corporation to build and manage the institution's entire investment portfolio. The OCIO takes responsibility for [asset allocation](/asset-allocation/) (how much in stocks versus [bonds](/bond/)), manager selection (which active or passive funds to own), rebalancing, and reporting. Instead of having an in-house investment team, the organisation delegates the job entirely to an expert.

OCIO mandates have grown because they offer scale and expertise to organisations too small to hire a full internal investment department. A mid-sized pension plan might hire an OCIO to manage 200 million to 2 billion dollars. The OCIO, typically managing multiple clients, achieves economies of scale in technology, research, and trading costs.

ClearShares built OCIO as an ETF designed to bring that same professional approach to retail investors. The fund is actively managed to hold the kind of portfolio an OCIO would design: diversified across asset classes, rebalanced periodically, and designed for a moderate-risk investor rather than either an aggressive growth seeker or a conservative income-focused retiree.

## The equity allocation

Roughly 50–60% of the fund is in equities, split between US large-cap stocks, mid-cap exposure, and international developed-market and emerging-market shares. The US equity holding tracks a broad-market approach similar to the S&P 500 or a total-market index, though ClearShares may tilt toward quality or dividend-paying names. International developed-market stocks add geographic [diversification](/diversification/) and exposure to cyclical economies. Emerging-market stocks are held at a smaller weight, 5–10%, to capture growth while limiting [currency volatility](/currency-volatility/) and emerging-market-specific risk.

Within equities, the fund avoids concentrated sector bets. The holdings are broadly representative of the overall equity market, without major tilts toward technology or financials, though these naturally carry larger weights due to their size in the broader market.

## Fixed income and alternatives

The fixed-income allocation — roughly 30–40% of the fund — typically includes US government bonds, investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), and international bonds. This gives the portfolio steady income, duration risk management, and a source of ballast when equities decline. Bond holdings are laddered across maturities, and credit quality is maintained at high levels; the fund avoids [junk bonds](/junk-bond/) and concentrated [credit risk](/credit-risk/).

The remaining 5–15% is allocated to alternative strategies or non-traditional assets. This might include real-asset exposure (commodities, inflation-linked bonds), hedge-fund-like strategies (low-volatility or market-neutral approaches), or [real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/). The exact mix varies with market conditions and ClearShares' outlook, but the intent is to reduce correlation with traditional stocks and bonds while providing diversification.

## Active management and rebalancing

Unlike a passive [index fund](/index-fund/), OCIO is actively managed. ClearShares' team makes tactical allocation decisions — shifting the equity weight from 55% to 60% if the outlook is favourable, or raising cash if valuations look stretched. The team also rebalances on a regular schedule, typically quarterly or semi-annually, to maintain target allocations. When equities surge and drift above their target weight, the manager sells some stocks and buys bonds, locking in gains. When stocks slump, the rebalancing process buys the dip and sells bonds.

This active approach comes with a cost: the [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically 0.40–0.65%, higher than a passive index fund but lower than a traditional actively managed [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/). The higher expense is justified by the [rebalancing discipline](/rebalancing-discipline/) and the avoidance of concentrated bets.

## Liquidity and trading

OCIO is an ETF, meaning it trades on an exchange like a stock and can be bought or sold during market hours. The fund is moderately liquid; institutional investors and financial advisors are the largest shareholders. Daily volumes are sufficient for most retail orders but not as deep as a mega-cap S&P 500 ETF. The fund's holdings are transparent, disclosed daily, so investors know exactly what they own.

## Who this fund suits

OCIO appeals to investors who want a complete, professionally managed portfolio in a single holding. It is particularly useful for investors who lack either the expertise or the time to build their own diversified portfolio or who want a second opinion on their allocation. Financial advisors often use OCIO as a core holding in recommended portfolios, especially for conservative or moderate clients. It also suits individuals delegating portfolio decisions to a robo-advisor, who might recommend OCIO as the underlying holding.

OCIO is less suitable for investors with strong conviction about sector or geographic bets, since the fund's diversified approach avoids concentrated positions. Nor is it ideal for tax-sensitive investors in high tax brackets, since active rebalancing can generate capital gains inside the fund — though the ETF structure is more tax-efficient than a mutual fund would be.

## The cost-benefit trade-off

OCIO charges more than a simple [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/) fund, and over long periods in a [bull market](/bull-market/), the diversification and rebalancing drag will cost something. But in sideways or down markets, the fixed-income allocation and the disciplined rebalancing act as a shock absorber, potentially outperforming a pure equity fund. The real benefit is for investors who would otherwise be tempted to abandon diversification, chase performance, or panic-sell in downturns.

## How to research OCIO

Start with ClearShares' fact sheet and prospectus, which lay out the current allocation to equities, bonds, and alternatives, plus the list of top holdings. Compare the expense ratio to other diversified allocation ETFs and mutual funds, and ask whether the active management is likely to add value given recent track record. Look at the fund's performance across different market environments — bull markets, [bear markets](/bear-market/), and sideways years — to understand where the diversification adds value and where it costs performance.

Monitor the fund's turnover rate and any commentary on recent rebalancing decisions. Understanding the manager's reasoning for allocation shifts reveals whether the active management is based on systematic rules or discretionary timing calls. Finally, evaluate whether the fund's target allocation (how much equity, how much fixed income) matches your own risk tolerance and time horizon.
