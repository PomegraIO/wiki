## What exactly does ORLG do?

ORLG tracks O'Reilly Automotive stock with 2X daily leverage. When O'Reilly shares rise 1% in a day, ORLG aims to rise 2%. When O'Reilly falls 1%, ORLG should fall 2%. The fund is issued by Leverage Shares, a London-based firm that specializes in leveraged products, and it trades on U.S. exchanges. The leverage is achieved through derivatives—primarily futures contracts on the O'Reilly stock itself—rather than through simple borrowing and buying shares.

## Who created this fund and why?

Leverage Shares builds leverage products for retail and institutional investors who want amplified exposure to specific stocks without requiring margin accounts or direct access to futures markets. The firm is registered as a broker-dealer and an investment adviser. ORLG was created to give investors a simple way to get 2X leveraged exposure to O'Reilly Automotive, an automotive parts and accessories retailer with strong earnings and steady cash generation. By wrapping leverage into an ETF structure, Leverage Shares made what would otherwise require a margin account available to any investor with a brokerage account.

## How does the daily reset mechanism work?

Each trading day at the close, the fund's derivatives positions are adjusted so that the fund is positioned to deliver exactly 2X O'Reilly's next day's move. This reset is mathematically important. Imagine O'Reilly stock gains 2% on Monday and loses 2% on Tuesday, ending flat. A simple 2X leveraged position would end flat as well—up 4% Monday, down 4% Tuesday. But ORLG resets daily. It gains 4% Monday. At Tuesday's open, it is reset to track 2X again. It then loses 4% Tuesday. Net result: it ends down slightly (approximately 0.16%) despite O'Reilly stock being flat. That small loss is volatility decay, the cost of rebalancing in a choppy market.

## What's the catch with holding this fund long-term?

The catch is decay. In a volatile market where a stock bounces around, daily rebalancing causes a leveraged fund to lose ground to the mathematics of compounding. The more the stock whipsaws, the more the fund decays. If O'Reilly trades sideways for six months while remaining volatile, ORLG will likely underperform 2X the stock's actual return over that period. The fund works perfectly for what it is designed to do—deliver 2X the daily move—but daily moves are not the same as long-term moves when volatility is high.

## When is ORLG actually useful?

ORLG is useful for tactical directional bets on O'Reilly Automotive that last days to a few weeks. A trader who believes O'Reilly will rally over the next ten trading days can use ORLG to amplify that conviction without a margin account. The tight bid-ask spread and reliable daily tracking make it efficient for short-term entry and exit. Hedge funds and sophisticated traders also use products like ORLG for specific positioning within a portfolio during volatile periods.

## What costs are involved?

The expense ratio is typically around 0.7% to 0.9% annually. This covers the cost of rolling futures contracts, managing the leverage structure, and operational overhead. Because O'Reilly is highly liquid—it trades tens of millions of shares daily—the fund's manager faces low costs when adjusting positions. The prospectus and fact sheet provide the exact current expense ratio and any performance fees.

## How much volatility decay should I expect?

The exact amount depends on O'Reilly's realized volatility. In a very calm market where the stock trends steadily in one direction, decay is minimal and compounding actually helps returns. In a volatile sideways market, decay compounds and becomes significant—perhaps 0.3% to 1% per month in an extremely choppy environment, though this is not guaranteed and depends entirely on the actual path of prices. There is no way to predict decay in advance; it is purely a function of what prices actually do.
