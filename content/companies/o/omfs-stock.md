*The **Invesco Russell 2000 Dynamic Multifactor ETF** (ticker OMFS) is an exchange-traded fund that holds small-cap US company shares selected and weighted according to a formula that favours multiple financial characteristics at once — what fund managers call a multifactor or smart-beta approach. Rather than tracking the [Russell 2000 index](/russell-2000/) with all its companies equally weighted, OMFS applies a mechanical filter that tilts toward stocks with attractive valuations, strong quality metrics, and favourable price momentum.*

## What is OMFS, and why does it exist?

The Russell 2000 is the standard benchmark for US small-cap equities — roughly 2,000 publicly traded American companies with smaller [market capitalizations](/market-capitalization/) than the largest 1,000 firms. A plain [index fund](/index-fund/) holding all 2,000 equally treats a profitable, financially sound business the same as a speculative, overleveraged shell. OMFS solves that problem by layering a quantitative filter on top of the Russell universe.

The fund applies scores across four main factors: **value** (stocks trading cheaply relative to earnings or book value), **quality** (profitable, stable, low-debt businesses), **size** (favours smaller names within the small-cap universe), and **momentum** (stocks showing upward price trends). The methodology dynamically rebalances quarterly, adjusting the weighting as these metrics change. The result is a portfolio of perhaps 300–400 holdings — still diversified, but concentrated toward stocks that tick multiple boxes simultaneously rather than holding every Russell 2000 constituent with equal weight.

This approach reflects a widely held belief among quantitative investors that certain measurable characteristics predict outperformance. Small-cap stocks themselves have historically offered higher returns than large-cap peers over long periods, though with greater volatility. A fund that narrows the Russell 2000 to its most "attractive" companies — by value and quality metrics — aims to capture that small-cap premium while reducing downside risk.

## How OMFS fits into a portfolio

OMFS is meant for investors wanting exposure to small-cap equity with a tilt toward quality and value. It is not a core holding but rather a specialist position. Someone building a simple all-cap US equity strategy might own broad index funds covering the entire market; someone convinced that small-cap value still offers opportunity might use OMFS as that sleeve. The fund's turnover is moderate (driven by the quarterly rebalancing), so costs from trading are not extreme.

The fund competes in a crowded market. Alternatives include iShares Russell 2000 Value ETF (IWN), which explicitly targets value small-caps; Vanguard's extended market offerings; and any number of other multifactor small-cap products from Invesco's rivals. Most offer similar mechanics — a screen applied to the Russell 2000 — and differ mainly in the specific weights assigned to each factor and the rebalancing schedule.

## The risks that matter

Small-cap stocks are inherently more volatile than large-cap peers. They receive less analyst coverage, trade in smaller volumes, and are more sensitive to economic slowdowns and changes in investor appetite for risk. A fund holding them is riding that volatility whether it applies a multifactor tilt or not.

The specific risk of a factor-based approach is that the chosen factors may underperform for extended periods. Value has been out of favour for years in some [market cycles](/market-cycle/); momentum can whipsaw in reversals. There is no guarantee that stocks scoring high on Invesco's quality and value metrics will beat a straightforward Russell 2000 index fund. Backtests suggest the advantage, but past returns do not dictate future results.

Concentration is a second risk. By tilting away from the broadest index toward selected characteristics, OMFS holds fewer names and can have larger positions in individual stocks. That raises the impact of any single holding's trouble on the portfolio.

## Expense ratio and trading

OMFS charges an annual [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) in the range of 0.35–0.40%, which is reasonable for a small-cap equity product with active screening and quarterly rebalancing. It is not a bargain compared to passive Russell 2000 index funds (which charge 0.04–0.10%), but it is not expensive for a factor-tilted strategy. The fund trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) and is reasonably liquid; spreads are typically tight.

## How to research OMFS

Start with Invesco's fund factsheet, which lists the current holdings, factor exposures, and recent performance versus the Russell 2000 benchmark. The prospectus lays out the methodology in detail — which factors are used, how they are scored, and how often rebalancing occurs. For context, read the fund's annual and semi-annual reports to see turnover and any changes to the screening criteria. Compare performance over full market cycles (three, five, ten years) against simple Russell 2000 index funds and competing multifactor products. Small-cap returns vary sharply year to year, so a single-year snapshot is misleading. Finally, reflect on whether your own view of small-cap value and quality aligns with Invesco's algorithm — if you are sceptical that a mechanical factor screen beats the broader universe, a plain index fund may serve you better.
