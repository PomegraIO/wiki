Onward Medical is a specialized biomedical company headquartered in the Netherlands that designs devices and therapies to help people with spinal cord injury and other neurological conditions regain movement and function. The company works at the intersection of neuroscience and engineering — developing electrical stimulation systems that interface with the spinal cord to restore voluntary movement in limbs that would otherwise be paralyzed. It is a clinical-stage company focused on a small but devastating patient population and a global opportunity that spans multiple regulatory markets.

## The rehabilitation segment: Targeted Therapy and the RestoreSensor System

Onward's most advanced product is RestoreSensor, a surgically implanted spinal cord stimulation system designed for people with chronic spinal cord injury. The device delivers electrical pulses to specific nerve pathways in the spinal cord, stimulating them in patterns that can help restore voluntary movement and some sensation in legs and trunk. RestoreSensor is paired with external controllers and therapy protocols that patients and clinicians use to optimize stimulation patterns for each individual.

The clinical evidence supporting RestoreSensor is substantial. In trials, patients with complete or near-complete paralysis who received the implant and underwent rehabilitation protocols showed measurable recovery of voluntary movement — not full restoration of walking for most, but restoration of meaningful function: the ability to move a leg voluntarily, to stand with support, to improve circulation and prevent secondary complications of paralysis. For a spinal cord injury patient, even modest restoration of motor control can meaningfully improve quality of life and independence.

RestoreSensor addresses a key limitation of existing spinal cord stimulation devices, which were originally designed to treat pain. Onward's system is optimized for motor recovery, with refined electrode placement, stimulation patterns, and patient training protocols. This specificity is important: existing stimulation devices are not licensed or optimized for motor recovery in spinal cord injury, creating a regulatory pathway and clinical niche for RestoreSensor.

The commercial model is to provide the implanted device, the external control systems, and ongoing clinical support and therapy optimization. This creates recurring revenue from patient follow-up, software updates, and hardware replacements. Patients who benefit from the system have strong motivation to remain engaged with Onward's clinical and technical support.

## The locomotion segment: Epidural Stimulation and Gait Recovery

Onward's other major program is an epidural stimulation therapy designed to help people with spinal cord injury walk again. Unlike RestoreSensor, which is implanted into the spinal cord itself, epidural stimulation delivers electrical pulses through electrodes placed outside the spinal cord (but inside the dura, the outermost membrane surrounding it). This is less invasive than intraspinal stimulation.

Early clinical data from epidural stimulation studies showed remarkable results: some patients with previously paralyzing injuries were able to walk short distances with minimal support, or to step overground with body-weight support. However, these results were achieved in specialized rehabilitation centers with intensive training protocols. The question for commercialization is whether the benefit can be delivered outside specialized centers, whether patients can maintain gains over time, and how reimbursement systems will view the therapy relative to its cost.

Onward has been developing a commercial version of epidural stimulation with the goal of making the therapy more practical and accessible than early research protocols. This requires advancing the technology (miniaturizing devices, improving wireless communication), demonstrating safety and efficacy in larger clinical trials, and navigating the regulatory pathway to approval.

## Regulatory and geographic positioning

Onward is strategically positioned across multiple regulatory markets. The company is incorporated in the Netherlands and has operations there; it also has sites in the United States (where major clinical trials are underway) and Switzerland. This distributed structure reflects the geography of regulatory approval, reimbursement, and clinical development.

The European Union and the United States have different pathways and timelines for approving medical devices. The EU's Medical Device Regulation and the CE mark process have a different standard and approval timeline than the US FDA's premarket approval. Onward can pursue approval in both markets, which diversifies regulatory risk and allows the company to access patients and reimbursement in multiple regions. However, it also means the company must manage two regulatory processes simultaneously, which is complex and costly.

Reimbursement is a critical unknown. RestoreSensor and epidural stimulation are novel therapies with no established reimbursement codes or clinical pathways in most health systems. In the United States, Onward would likely need to work with the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services and private insurers to establish coverage and reimbursement rates. In Europe, reimbursement varies by country. Building the economic case — demonstrating that the therapy improves outcomes in ways that justify its cost — is a long process and a significant commercialization challenge.

## Clinical stage, pre-revenue challenges, and the path to profitability

Onward is a pre-revenue or low-revenue company (depending on when this entry is read and how quickly the company has progressed through trials and approvals). The company burns cash funding research, clinical trials, manufacturing scale-up, and regulatory filings. This model is not unusual for medical-device companies with advanced therapies, but it creates risk: the company must raise capital (through equity offerings, debt, or partnerships) to fund operations until products are approved and commercially available.

The path from clinical trials to approved product to profitable business takes years and typically costs hundreds of millions of dollars. Onward has cleared some major hurdles — RestoreSensor has been studied in humans and has shown safety and efficacy — but still faces the final gauntlet of regulatory approvals, manufacturing scale-up, market launch, and building clinician and patient awareness.

Reimbursement risk is the largest commercial unknown. Even if the devices work, insurance and health systems may not pay enough to sustain a profitable business. Onward has been exploring partnerships with larger medical-device companies to share development and commercialization costs — an indication that the company recognizes the scale of investment required.

## The patient population and market opportunity

The spinal cord injury population is relatively small — approximately 250,000 to 350,000 people in the United States living with chronic spinal cord injury, with a few thousand new injuries each year. Globally, the population is larger but still modest compared to other neurodegenerative diseases. However, the medical need is acute: spinal cord injury often strikes young, active people and leaves them with profound disability. The economic and personal burden is enormous.

This small population is actually an advantage for a specialist company like Onward. The total addressable market may be constrained, but the patients are motivated, and the clinical and economic case for a therapy that restores even modest function is strong. A company does not need to treat millions of patients to build a valuable, profitable business if each patient represents significant clinical benefit and willingness to pay.

The broader opportunity is beyond spinal cord injury. Spinal cord stimulation technology could eventually be applied to other conditions with neurological paralysis — stroke, traumatic brain injury, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. But Onward's current focus is spinal cord injury, where the clinical evidence and regulatory pathway are most advanced.

## The competitive and technological landscape

Onward is not the only company pursuing spinal cord stimulation for spinal cord injury. Several larger medical-device companies (Medtronic, Abbott, Boston Scientific) have spinal-stimulation portfolios primarily focused on pain management, and a few are exploring motor recovery. Academic and smaller biotech companies are also researching epidural stimulation, some with promising early data.

Onward's advantages are technical (it has developed optimized hardware and software) and clinical (it has invested heavily in clinical validation and data). Its disadvantages are scale and resources — the larger competitors have more capital, manufacturing capacity, and global sales infrastructure.

The technology itself is not locked behind a patent moat that prevents competition. Other companies can develop similar devices and therapies. But the clinical data, regulatory approvals, and established reimbursement pathways create barriers to entry that take years to overcome.
