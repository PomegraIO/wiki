*Longview Advantage Fixed [Income ETF](/income-etf/) — ticker LVIG — is an actively managed exchange-traded fund that holds a broad mix of [bonds](/bond/) across credit qualities and maturities. It seeks to provide income and capital preservation by dynamically allocating across investment-grade corporates, government securities, and higher-yielding bond categories, using a systematic methodology rather than following a preset index.*

The fund trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) under the symbol LVIG and carries the structural transparency and [intraday liquidity](/intraday-liquidity/) of an ETF while maintaining the discretionary flexibility of active management. This hybrid structure — an actively managed strategy wrapped in ETF form — appeals to investors who want professional bond selection but prefer the tax efficiency and trading convenience that ETF wrapper provides.

## What LVIG holds and how it builds its portfolio

Rather than tracking a published bond index, LVIG employs a rules-based approach to construct its holdings. The fund core sits in investment-grade debt — [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), government securities, and agency-backed instruments — but it deliberately holds a meaningful portion in higher-yielding securities including [high-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/) and distressed-credit opportunities. The weighting between these tiers shifts based on the fund manager's assessment of value and risk.

The portfolio is genuinely diversified across sectors, geographies, and credit profiles. Unlike single-sector or single-country bond funds, LVIG does not concentrate in tech debt, bank debt, or emerging-market risk — it spreads across the entire credit spectrum. This breadth is the trade-off between pure income (higher-yield specialists often pay more) and stability (a diversified bond fund tends to have calmer returns).

## Who runs it and how the fund structure works

LVIG is sponsored and managed by Longview Asset Management, which oversees the investment decisions and portfolio rebalancing. As an actively managed ETF, it operates with all-day trading liquidity — you can buy or sell shares at any moment the market is open at prices set by supply and demand, rather than waiting until a daily close like traditional [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/) shares.

The fund is registered as an open-end management company and must hold a prospectus filed with the [Securities and Exchange Commission](/securities-and-exchange-commission/). The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) — the annual cost to hold the fund — is quoted qualitatively as moderate, meaning neither a bargain basement offering nor a premium charge. Investors pay this cost whether the fund outperforms or underperforms, which matters over long [holding periods](/holding-period/).

## Income, volatility, and the interest-rate gamble

> An actively managed bond fund can shift its stance if the outlook changes, but that timing skill cannot be guaranteed.

The fund distributes income to shareholders regularly, typically in the form of [dividend](/dividend/) payments reflecting the coupon income and any gains from security sales within the portfolio. The yield — the annualized income stream as a percentage of share price — varies with [interest rates](/interest-rate/); when rates rise, bond prices fall and the yield often steps higher, and vice versa. This inverse relationship between rates and bond values is the core risk of any bond fund.

Because LVIG holds bonds across the credit spectrum, it carries two distinct risks. [Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) affects all bonds equally: if rates rise, the fund's holdings decline in value. [Credit risk](/credit-risk/) — the danger that a bond issuer defaults — falls most heavily on the high-yield portion. In economic downturns, distressed credits suffer sharply, which means LVIG's returns will be worse than a pure [investment-grade bond](/investment-grade-bond/) fund, though better than a junk-only fund.

The actively managed structure allows the fund to shift its stance if the outlook changes. A manager believing rates will rise can trim long-duration positions; one anticipating [recession](/recession/) can lighten credit exposure. This discretion is powerful in hindsight but carries no guarantee of success. Timing bond markets is no easier than timing stocks, and fund returns depend heavily on the manager's actual skill versus the fees charged.

## Cost, trading, and who owns it

The expense ratio is the primary ongoing cost to shareholders. Secondary costs appear at trade time: when buying or selling ETF shares, you pay a [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) (the difference between the bid price and the ask price) if you trade with other investors or [market makers](/market-maker-trading/). This spread is typically tighter for high-volume funds like LVIG than for specialized [bond ETFs](/bond-etf/) with little trading.

The fund is held by a broad base of individual investors, advisors, and institutions seeking a diversified income stream without the complexity of picking individual bonds. Bond markets are opaque compared to [stock markets](/stock-market/) — corporate bond prices are scattered across multiple venues and reported with delay — so for most investors, a fund provides easier access to a professional-grade portfolio than direct bond ownership would.

## Researching LVIG as a holding

Anyone considering LVIG should begin with the fund's prospectus and the current fact sheet, both available on the Longview Asset Management website. The prospectus details the fund's investment objectives, strategies, risks, and fee structure in legal language. The fact sheet provides a snapshot: the current portfolio composition, the top holdings, the yield, the assets under management, and recent performance.

Key metrics to track are the yield (which tells you what income to expect), the average portfolio duration (which measures sensitivity to interest-rate moves), and the credit quality breakdown (percentage in investment-grade versus high-yield). Performance matters, but a short-term outperformance or underperformance tells you little; bond-fund strength shows over years, not quarters. Compare LVIG's returns and volatility to a broad bond-index ETF to see whether the active management has added value after fees.
