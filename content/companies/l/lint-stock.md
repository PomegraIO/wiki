LINT is a leveraged exchange-traded fund that tracks [Intel](/intc-stock/) (INTC) [common stock](/common-stock/) with 2x daily reset mechanics. It is not a long-term investment vehicle; it is a tactical instrument built for traders who expect Intel to move sharply upward over days or weeks. Direxion manages the fund.

*LINT aims to deliver twice the daily price movement of Intel stock — if Intel rises 2% on a given day, LINT is engineered to rise roughly 4%. The mechanism is simple in theory but has real consequences: because the reset happens every single trading day, the fund's long-term performance diverges from twice the underlying stock's performance, especially in choppy or declining markets. This phenomenon is called volatility decay, and it is the central reason leveraged daily-reset funds work best for short, directional bets rather than buy-and-hold.*

## How the 2x leverage works

LINT holds a mix of Intel stock and index [futures contracts](/futures-contract/), which allows Direxion to amplify the price exposure without owning twice as much stock outright. Every trading day, the fund rebalances back to exactly 2x the Intel stock price, which means it sells winners on up days and buys losers on down days. In a steadily rising market, this daily rebalancing actually caps the fund's gains below 2x the underlying return. In a falling or sideways market, it accelerates losses.

As a stylised example: if Intel rises 10% over five days in a straight line, LINT roughly doubles that, rising about 20%. But if Intel rises 5%, falls 5%, rises 5%, falls 5%, and rises 5% — the same net gain — LINT's daily resets lock in smaller gains on the winning days and larger losses on the falling days, ending up with a smaller total return, even though the underlying stock recovered to the same level. The longer the [holding period](/holding-period/) and the choppier the price action, the more pronounced this decay becomes.

## Liquidity, costs, and who actually trades it

LINT trades on the NYSE Arca under the ticker LINT with reasonably tight intraday spreads, reflecting moderate daily volume. Like most [leveraged ETFs](/leveraged-etf/), it charges an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) that covers the fund's hedging costs and Direxion's [management fee](/management-fee/). The ratio is a small percentage, but it accrues daily, compounding the friction.

The fund is built for day traders and short-term tactical players — people who believe Intel will have a sharp run-up over the next few days or a couple of weeks, and who want to amplify their exposure without using margin directly through a brokerage account. For anyone holding beyond a few weeks, especially through market swings, the decay eats into returns meaningfully. For anyone holding through a decline, leverage amplifies losses in both direction and magnitude. Retail investors sometimes buy leveraged ETFs passively and hold for months or years, unaware of the decay mechanics; this almost always results in losses larger than the underlying decline would have warranted.

## Real risks beyond leverage

Volatility decay is not the only risk. If Intel falls sharply, LINT falls twice as fast — a 20% drop in the stock becomes roughly a 40% drop in the fund. That kind of move is recoverable from a stock perspective but psychologically brutal in a leveraged product. There is also the risk that Intel itself faces structural challenges — competition, product cycles, cyclical downturns — which a 2x lever turns into a 4x story.

The fund also carries [counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/) if Direxion's hedges fail or if the derivative positions it holds become harder to maintain in a market stress. In theory, the leverage is perfectly rebalanced each day, but markets can move too fast; there is always a lag between the fund's rebalancing and the actual price moves.

## How to research it

Anyone considering LINT should read Direxion's prospectus, which lays out the exact mechanics and the daily-reset schedule, along with past performance data and volatility charts. Understanding the difference between daily return and cumulative return is essential — the prospectus shows both. Intel's quarterly earnings calls and 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0000050156) matter enormously, because Intel-specific events — chip delays, market share losses, guidance revisions — directly move the leverage equation.

A simple reality check: if you cannot explain why you expect Intel to move a specific direction over a specific time frame — days or weeks, not months — LINT is probably the wrong instrument. If you find yourself holding it beyond your original thesis, the costs and decay are working against you.
