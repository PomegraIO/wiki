*The **FT Vest 20+ Year Treasury & Target Income Exchange-Traded Fund** (LTTI) is an income-focused fund that holds U.S. [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/) maturing 20 years or longer, paired with an overlay of options strategies designed to boost yields above what an unadorned long-bond index would deliver. It is built for investors who want exposure to the safest asset class in existence — U.S. government [bonds](/bond/) — but find the yields currently offered insufficient, and are willing to accept some complexity in exchange for higher cash distribution.*

The fund is managed by FT Vest, the ETF arm of Financial Technologies International, and is built on a simple insight: long-term Treasury bonds are extraordinarily safe but have been offering vanishingly low yields for extended periods. A 20-year Treasury can provide a floor of safety that stocks and [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) cannot match — the United States is unlikely to default on its obligations — yet the coupon alone is often too low to support the retirement income or portfolio yield that an investor needs. By layering in covered-call strategies (selling [call options](/call-option/) against the Treasury holdings), LTTI attempts to generate additional return without abandoning the safety profile of the underlying bonds.

The appeal is straightforward to traditional bond investors. Treasury yields have been the baseline of the [risk-free rate](/risk-free-rate/) in global markets for more than a century, and owning a 20-year Treasury is as close as an investor can get to complete certainty of principal repayment. Yet certainty has a cost: yields on the long end of the curve tend to be modest compared to what stocks or corporate debt demand. For a 65-year-old living on portfolio income, a 4% Treasury yield can be compelling; for a 45-year-old with decades ahead, the same yield feels spartan. LTTI bridges that gap by extracting a yield premium from the fund's options activity.

The mechanics are straightforward. LTTI holds a basket of Treasury bonds maturing beyond 20 years, often weighted toward the 20–30 year part of the curve where duration is pronounced and yields are reasonably attractive. On top of those holdings, the manager regularly sells call options — contracts that give another party the right to buy the Treasury bonds at a predetermined price. The buyer of the option pays a premium, which LTTI keeps and distributes to shareholders. In exchange, LTTI accepts the risk that if Treasury prices rise sharply, the call options will be exercised, and the Treasuries will be sold away at a below-market price. This is the classic trade-off of covered-call writing: higher current income in exchange for capped upside.

The real risk here is opportunity cost. If long-term Treasury yields fall sharply — because the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) cuts rates aggressively or because global demand for U.S. debt surges — Treasury prices will rally, and the [covered calls](/covered-call/) will exercise, forcing the fund to sell its holdings at prices that cap the gains. An unadorned Treasury ETF would participate fully in that rally; LTTI will not. This happened repeatedly in the decade after the 2008 financial crisis, when many covered-call Treasury strategies significantly underperformed plain Treasury indices because yields fell more than the options premiums could compensate for. For investors focused solely on total return, that drag matters. For income-focused investors, it is the intended trade-off.

The distributions LTTI makes are designed to be higher and steadier than a conventional Treasury ETF would provide, combining the yield of the underlying bonds with the call premium. However, the source of the distribution matters. If most of it comes from the coupon, it is a true return and is funded by interest the government is paying. If most comes from call premiums — which represent a sale of future gains — the distribution is partially a return of capital, and it is unsustainable if Treasury prices keep rising indefinitely. This is where the word "target" in the fund's name matters: the distributions are targeted, not guaranteed, and they depend on market conditions remaining within the range the strategy assumes.

For investors considering LTTI, the prospectus is essential reading, particularly the section on options strategies and the fund's performance during periods of falling Treasury yields. A back-test or historical analysis showing how the fund has performed relative to a plain long-Treasury index in periods of Fed easing is invaluable context. Finally, understanding the fund's current option-writing schedule and the [strike prices](/strike-price/) at which calls are sold helps an investor calibrate their expectations for upside cap and the probability that holdings will be called away. LTTI is not a "set it and forget it" fund; it rewards investors who understand the trade-off between current income and future price appreciation, and who are comfortable with that bargain.
