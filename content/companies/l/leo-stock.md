BNY Mellon Strategic Municipals is a [closed-end fund](/wiki/closed-end-fund/) that invests primarily in municipal bonds to provide shareholders with regular distributions of tax-exempt income. Unlike an open-end mutual fund, which can be bought and sold at net asset value daily, a closed-end fund trades on an exchange like a stock, meaning its price can diverge from the underlying value of its holdings. LEO's focus on municipal securities — bonds issued by states, cities, and local authorities to finance infrastructure and services — makes it part of a larger market where [closed-end funds](/wiki/closed-end-fund/) have carved out a distinct role: offering retail investors higher yields and portfolio leverage that would be difficult to replicate through direct bond purchases or simpler open-end muni funds.

## What sets a closed-end fund apart from an open-end muni fund?

The structural difference between a closed-end fund and an open-end mutual fund profoundly shapes how LEO operates and what returns investors can expect. When you buy shares in an open-end municipal fund — such as those offered by Vanguard or Fidelity — you buy at the fund's net asset value (NAV), which equals the total value of holdings divided by the number of shares outstanding. The fund stands ready to issue new shares to new buyers and redeem shares from sellers at that NAV price. As a result, the share price tracks the fund's underlying value closely.

A [closed-end fund](/wiki/closed-end-fund/) like LEO works differently. The fund issues a fixed number of shares upfront and trades them on an exchange. Investors buy and sell existing shares with each other, not with the fund. The share price is determined by supply and demand in the market, not by the fund manager. This means LEO's share price can trade above its NAV (at a premium) or below it (at a discount). A discount is common in municipal [closed-end funds](/wiki/closed-end-fund/), particularly when municipal bond markets are weak, when the yield curve shifts unfavorably, or when buyers have lost confidence in the tax-exempt bond space. A premium typically reflects strong demand for a fund's holdings or its distribution yield.

This structural feature creates both opportunity and risk. An investor who buys LEO shares when the fund trades at a steep discount gets an extra source of return: if the discount narrows, the share price can appreciate even if the underlying bonds themselves remain flat. Conversely, buying at a premium and watching the discount widen can hurt returns. This dynamic—the discount or premium—is invisible in an open-end mutual fund and is one reason why closed-end funds appeal to sophisticated investors seeking higher yields but also why they carry more risk for retail buyers who misunderstand the mechanism.

## How does leverage work in the closed-end fund structure?

One of the central reasons LEO and other closed-end municipal funds can offer distributions higher than their NAV-based yield is leverage. The fund borrows money—often through floating-rate notes or bank lines of credit—and invests the borrowed funds alongside shareholder capital into higher-yielding municipal bonds. This amplified portfolio generates more interest income than the fund would produce from shareholder capital alone, and that excess income can be distributed to shareholders or reinvested.

Leverage is a double-edged tool. In a benign interest-rate environment, where borrowing costs stay low and municipal bonds hold their value, leverage magnifies returns. But when rates rise, the cost of leverage climbs, potentially squeezing net returns. If municipal bond prices fall sharply, the fund's NAV shrinks, and the leverage ratio (the ratio of borrowed funds to shareholder equity) climbs, making the portfolio more fragile. Some closed-end muni funds also faced redemption pressure during the 2008 financial crisis and again during brief episodes of municipal bond market stress, when fear drove discounts wider and floating-rate debt became expensive or harder to access.

Shareholders should understand that the distributions LEO pays include a mix of current interest income, capital gains (if bonds are sold for profit), and in some periods, a return of capital—a return of invested shareholder principal that is not earned income. The fund discloses this breakdown in its annual reports and in distributions notices, but many retail investors overlook it. A high distribution rate that includes significant return of capital is economically unsustainable and erodes the NAV over time.

## What is the asset base and composition?

LEO holds a diversified portfolio of municipal securities across the United States, with allocations typically spanning general obligation bonds (backed by the full taxing power of a municipality), revenue bonds (backed by a specific revenue stream, such as airport or toll-road fees), and other types of munis. The fund aims for investment-grade credit quality, meaning its holdings are concentrated in bonds rated BBB and above by major rating agencies. This conservatism reduces default risk but also constrains yields — the fund sacrifices the extra income available from lower-rated, higher-yielding municipal securities.

The geographic and sectoral diversity of the portfolio matters because state and local government finances vary widely. A fund with heavy exposure to weak-economy states, declining school districts, or water authorities with aging infrastructure faces different risks than one holding bonds from stable, growing metros. The fund's prospectus and annual reports detail the portfolio composition, credit ratings, and sector breakdowns; reviewing these alongside credit reports on major holdings is essential for assessing true risk.

## Why would an investor choose a muni closed-end fund over direct bonds or a muni ETF?

Investors in higher tax brackets benefit from municipal bond tax exemption—the interest is generally exempt from federal income tax and often from state and local tax if the investor is a resident of the state where the bond is issued. For a high-earner in a high-tax state, this can be enormously valuable. Direct bond ownership offers simplicity and certainty: you hold the bond to maturity, you know exactly when you'll get your principal back, and you avoid the discount-to-NAV risk inherent in closed-end funds.

Closed-end muni funds, however, offer several attractions. One is simplicity: a small amount of capital can buy a diversified portfolio of dozens of bonds. Direct bond purchases require larger minimum investments and significant research to assemble a diversified portfolio. A second is the potential for higher yield, achieved through both the fund's active management and leverage. A third is daily liquidity: you can sell your shares whenever the market is open, whereas selling individual bonds can be slow and costly. Some closed-end muni funds also offer monthly distributions, which appeals to retirees or income-focused investors who value a predictable cash flow.

The trade-off is that you accept volatility in the share price. When the bond market is rising and confidence is high, a discount can narrow and amplify returns; when the opposite occurs, you lose. Exchange-traded muni funds (ETFs) have gained market share because they combine the diversification and leverage-like features of closed-end funds with lower expenses and more predictable premiums and discounts. But closed-end funds, especially those with long track records and experienced managers like BNY Mellon, remain popular among institutions and knowledgeable individual investors.

## What risks should buyers understand?

Beyond the discount-to-NAV risk, LEO investors face interest-rate risk: as yields on municipal bonds rise, the market value of the fund's existing holdings falls. This is true of any bond fund, but it can be magnified in closed-end funds because the share price can fall faster than the NAV—a combination of falling bond prices and a widening discount. Leverage also amplifies losses in a rising-rate environment. Credit risk, though mitigated by the focus on investment-grade bonds, is still present: a deterioration in state or local finances, a major issuer default, or a shift in the credit rating of a large holding can hurt performance.

There is also reinvestment risk: if the fund experiences a period of high bond maturities or calls, the capital returned to the portfolio may need to be reinvested at lower yields if the interest-rate environment has shifted. Lastly, there is the risk of distribution cuts. If the fund's income declines sharply due to falling yields, if leverage becomes expensive, or if defaults occur, the distribution may be reduced. A reduction can trigger panic selling by income-focused shareholders, widening the discount further and amplifying losses.

## How to research LEO and similar funds

For potential investors, the [fund prospectus](/wiki/10-k/) (available on the SEC website under CIK 818972) details all fees, risks, and holdings. The fund's annual reports and quarterly fact sheets show NAV, share price, premium or discount, portfolio composition, and distribution details. Financial news and fund-research platforms like Morningstar provide performance histories, peer comparisons, and expense ratios. It is critical to distinguish between the fund's NAV return and its share price return — they can differ significantly. Understanding the distribution breakdown (is it mostly interest income, capital gains, or return of capital?) is essential to assessing sustainability and true yield. And monitoring the discount or premium to NAV helps identify when the market is overpricing or underpricing the fund relative to its holdings, creating opportunities or risks for new buyers.
