*The **iShares iBonds 1-5 Year Corporate Ladder ETF** (LDRC) holds a diversified collection of investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/) arranged in a ladder — equal [tranches](/tranche/) maturing each year from one to five years out — to generate steady income while limiting the price volatility that comes with longer maturities.*

## What is a bond ladder, and why does LDRC build one?

A [bond ladder](/bond-ladder/) is a portfolio structure where [bonds](/bond/) are spaced across different maturity dates. Instead of owning a single bond that matures in five years, or a basket of bonds all maturing at once, LDRC holds roughly one-fifth of its portfolio maturing each year for the next five years. The reason is predictable cash flow: every year a tranche matures, returning principal that can be reinvested at current rates or withdrawn. This staggered approach also smooths out [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/). Longer-dated bonds are more sensitive to rate changes — if rates rise, a ten-year bond falls more than a two-year bond. By capping the longest maturity at five years, LDRC stays responsive to rate shifts without the duration drag of a traditional bond fund holding securities maturing in ten, twenty, or thirty years.

For an investor seeking reliable income rather than total return, or someone uncomfortable with the multi-year price swings a long-dated bond fund experiences, the ladder structure delivers both predictability and a realistic yield without requiring active trading.

## What bonds does LDRC actually hold?

LDRC focuses exclusively on investment-grade corporate debt — bonds issued by public companies with a [credit rating](/credit-rating/) of BBB or higher (in the Standard & Poor's or Moody's scale). These are companies with reasonably strong [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/): a bank, an industrial conglomerate, a utility, a healthcare company. LDRC holds hundreds of such bonds, weighting them by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) — larger issuers like [Apple](/aapl-stock/), [Microsoft](/msft-stock/), or [JPMorgan Chase](/jpmorgan-chase/) account for more of the fund's weight than smaller corporations.

The fund does not cherry-pick the "best" bonds or actively trade based on rate forecasts. It is a passive, rules-based vehicle. The underlying index it tracks — the Bloomberg U.S. 1–5 Year Corporate Bond Index — is a mechanical screen: include investment-grade corporate bonds issued by U.S. entities, exclude any with a maturity longer than five years or shorter than one year, and weight by the size of each bond issue. The fund holds whatever that index holds and rebalances as bonds mature and new ones are issued, so the composition drifts only as the underlying market evolves, not based on manager judgment.

## Why own this instead of individual bonds or a traditional bond fund?

Individual investors typically do not buy corporate bonds directly — they are sold in blocks, transaction costs are high, and assessing [credit risk](/credit-risk/) across hundreds of issuers is a full-time job. A bond fund simplifies that: you own a share of a large, diversified basket, and the fund handles the mechanics of trading and reinvesting [coupon payments](/coupon-payment/).

LDRC's ladder structure offers two advantages over a traditional corporate bond fund (one without a maturity ladder). First, you know roughly when you will get cash back: one-fifth each year for five years. Second, you avoid the duration mismatch: a conventional bond fund holding bonds with an average maturity of seven or eight years will fluctuate in price much more than LDRC, especially if rates rise quickly. LDRC's maximum five-year maturity means it captures most of the yield available in short- and intermediate-term corporates while staying insulated from the wild price swings that plague longer-duration portfolios.

The cost of this predictability is yield. LDRC's weighted-average yield is typically lower than a broader corporate bond fund holding longer maturities, because longer bonds pay more. If rates stay flat or fall, that extra yield from longer-dated bonds compounds into higher total returns. If rates rise, LDRC's shorter duration looks prescient.

## What are the actual risks?

The first risk is credit risk. LDRC owns investment-grade corporate bonds, which by definition carry a meaningful chance of default — not zero, not tiny, but material. During a severe [recession](/recession/), even companies rated BBB may struggle; if the economy contracts sharply, multiple issuers could default and LDRC's principal value would drop. This risk is real and underpriced in years when the economy is humming.

The second is [reinvestment risk](/reinvestment-risk/). When a bond matures and you are returned the principal, you must reinvest it in a new bond. If [interest rates](/interest-rate/) have fallen in the interim, that reinvestment will earn less. Over a five-year ladder, you face this risk in each of five separate years; the ladder does not eliminate it, only spread it out.

Third is [liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/), though it is modest. LDRC itself is highly liquid — you can buy or sell shares in the open market during trading hours. But the underlying corporate bonds are less liquid than government bonds; if the fund needs to sell bonds quickly during a market stress, it may face wider [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/).

Finally, there is [inflation risk](/inflation-risk/). LDRC's coupons are fixed in dollar terms. If [inflation](/inflation/) accelerates, the purchasing power of those coupon payments erodes. For an investor with a long time horizon concerned about rising prices, LDRC is not the answer — a Treasury Inflation-Protected Security fund would be more appropriate.

## How to evaluate and research LDRC

Start with the fund's fact sheet, published by iShares, which lists the fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) (typically very low, around 0.04% or less), the yield, the weighted-average maturity, and the top ten holdings. That sheet tells you most of what you need to know: how much you will pay in fees, roughly how much income you can expect, and which companies dominate the portfolio.

Next, look at the fund's duration — a measure of how sensitive it is to interest-rate changes. A duration of 3, for instance, means a one percentage point rise in rates would cause the fund's price to drop roughly 3%. LDRC's duration should be in the range of 2.5 to 4 — if it drifts much higher, the fund is taking on unexpected long-date risk.

Check the credit quality breakdown. What percentage of LDRC holds AAA-rated bonds (safest), what percentage BBB (riskiest investment-grade)? A fund heavily concentrated in BBB is more vulnerable to a credit crunch. You can find this in the fact sheet or the fund's prospectus.

If you are considering LDRC, you are likely choosing between it and other short-to-intermediate corporate bond funds, or between it and a bond ladder you would build yourself from individual bonds. LDRC's advantage is low cost and [diversification](/diversification/); its disadvantage is that you give up the certainty of maturity dates that individual bonds provide. The trade-off is fair and depends on your preference for simplicity over control. As with any fixed-income investment, understand that if you sell LDRC before the bonds mature, you are subject to market price swings — the ladder structure only helps if you hold to maturity or reinvest continuously. For a buy-and-hold investor comfortable with investment-grade corporate credit, LDRC is a straightforward, low-cost way to generate predictable income from a diversified bond basket.
