## What is tactical equity investing?

Tactical investing means making deliberate, concentrated bets on specific stocks or market segments, rather than holding a broad, diversified portfolio. Tactical approaches aim for higher returns by being selective about which equities to own — accepting that concentration brings both amplified upside and amplified downside. LeaderShares AlphaFactor Tactical Focused ETF (LSAT) is a tactical fund: it holds a deliberately concentrated portfolio of US large-cap stocks, selected by a proprietary algorithm that identifies the most attractive companies according to value, quality, and momentum metrics.

## How concentrated is LSAT, and why?

LSAT typically holds 50 to 75 stocks — far fewer than a broad [index fund](/index-fund/) with its 500-plus holdings and considerably fewer even than many active large-cap funds. This concentration is intentional. The fund manager's thesis is that by identifying the very best candidates according to AlphaFactor's scoring system and holding only those, the fund can deliver returns higher than the broader market or broader value-tilted indices.

The trade-off is volatility. In [bull markets](/bull-market/), particularly those favoring value stocks and quality names, LSAT's concentration amplifies gains. In [bear markets](/bear-market/) or periods when the specific characteristics LSAT favors go out of favour, the fund can underperform significantly. A broad index fund would decline in those periods too, but the damage would be spread across 500 holdings; LSAT feels it concentrated in perhaps 60. This is the fundamental bargain a tactical investor strikes: accept swings in relative performance in exchange for the potential for outperformance.

## The AlphaFactor scoring system

Like its sibling fund LSAF (the core equity version), LSAT relies on LeaderShares' proprietary AlphaFactor methodology to select stocks. The system weighs multiple factors — valuation (price relative to earnings or book value), balance-sheet quality (debt levels, asset coverage), earnings stability and growth, and price momentum. Stocks that score highest on this combined ranking become the largest weights in the portfolio.

The specific weighting assigned to each factor — how much to value cheapness versus quality, for instance — is not disclosed in full, both because it is proprietary and because disclosing it would eliminate any edge. However, the fund's prospectus outlines the general approach. An investor evaluating LSAT should examine that prospectus carefully, then look at the fund's historical holdings to see which types of companies have been favoured (e.g., has it leaned toward banks, consumer stocks, industrial companies) and how those sectors have behaved.

## Tactical tilts and market regimes

The effectiveness of a tactical focused fund depends entirely on whether the factors it selects on — value, quality, momentum — are persisting predictors of returns in the current market environment. Value stocks have not always outperformed; in the 2010s, expensive, high-growth technology stocks dramatically outpaced cheap value names. During that decade, any fund tilted toward value would have lagged. Conversely, in periods when value has reversed and begun outperforming, value-tilted funds have surged ahead.

LSAT's concentrated focus amplifies this regime dependency. If AlphaFactor is currently selecting primarily value-oriented and quality stocks, and value stocks enter a decade of outperformance, LSAT should be a strong performer. If growth stocks regain favour, LSAT will likely lag. This is not a flaw in the fund — tactical approaches by definition take explicit bets on which factors will drive returns — but it is a central risk to understand.

## Fees and liquidity

LSAT trades as an exchange-traded fund on US exchanges, offering the ability to buy and sell throughout the trading day. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is modest, typical of actively managed or factor-tilted equity funds, though higher than passively managed index funds that charge minimal fees. Because the fund is tactical and concentrated, it likely has higher portfolio turnover than a broad index fund, which can generate taxable capital gains — an important consideration for accounts outside a tax shelter.

Liquidity is generally solid given the fund's assets and the focus on large US equities, but trading volume should be checked before committing a very large position, as LSAT is more specialized than a mega-cap index fund.

## Is tactical better than core?

LSAT and LSAF (LeaderShares' core version) are siblings based on the same AlphaFactor methodology, but LSAT is the more aggressive sibling. LSAT is concentrated and tactical; LSAF is broad and core. The choice between them hinges on risk tolerance and philosophy. An investor who believes the AlphaFactor system works and is willing to accept the [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) and the shorter-term underperformance that tactical approaches can suffer might prefer LSAT. An investor who wants the same methodology applied to the same stock universe but wants more [diversification](/diversification/) would prefer LSAF.

Neither is objectively better. LSAT offers a shot at higher returns in years when its specific tilts are rewarded, but that shot comes with higher volatility and the real possibility of underperformance when the market favours the stocks and styles it has chosen to exclude. LSAF offers steadier performance with less drama but potentially lower upside.

## The research question

Before committing meaningful capital to LSAT, an investor should examine the fund's historical performance across different market environments — bull markets, bear markets, value-outperformance periods, and growth-outperformance periods — to see whether the AlphaFactor approach has delivered the promised edge or whether it has merely reflected the performance of the factors it selected on. The prospectus and factsheet provide the raw material; looking at rolling returns over three-, five-, and ten-year windows reveals the consistency of any outperformance.
