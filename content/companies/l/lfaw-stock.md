LFAW targets the year 2060, placing it five years further out than LFAO. It is designed for investors born around 1995 who expect to retire in their early sixties. The fund's construction reflects a belief that retirement income—not growth—is the primary goal, and that a portfolio should deliberately shift its character over time to reflect that.

Today, the fund holds a higher equity allocation than its later-dated siblings. Seventy-five percent stocks allows for nominal growth and [inflation](/inflation/) protection; the gains in stock prices help offset the erosion of purchasing power over decades. But those stocks are not selected for momentum or earnings growth. Instead, the fund's managers screen for companies with the capacity and commitment to pay [dividends](/dividend/) consistently through [market cycles](/market-cycle/)—utilities, mature industrials, established consumer staples, selected financial sectors. The intent is stability: a stock that falls ten percent in a downturn but still pays a five percent dividend offers a cushion that pure-growth stocks do not.

The [bond](/bond/) allocation is split between government securities and investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), emphasizing credit quality over yield. A bond that defaults wipes out the coupon premium and the principal; a fund avoiding junk-rated debt trades modestly lower yield for the reliability that matters in a drawdown-heavy phase of life.

The glide path is the mechanical heart. As 2060 approaches, the fund will gradually reduce equity exposure and increase bond exposure. This happens on a schedule—typically quarterly or semi-annually—without requiring any action from the investor. By 2055, the fund might hold 60 percent stocks and 40 percent bonds. By 2060, perhaps 35 percent stocks and 65 percent bonds. By 2065, even more conservative. This forced rebalancing has a subtle cost: in a prolonged [bull market](/bull-market/), the fund will be selling stocks at higher prices to buy bonds at lower yields, locking in opportunity loss. In a prolonged [bear market](/bear-market/), the fund will be forced to buy falling stocks with capital raised from selling rising bonds, which can feel like adding to losses. But in sideways or cyclical markets—the norm over decades—the glide path smooths the ride and reduces the psychological pressure to panic-sell equities in a downturn.

The active management layer is where performance diverges from peers. The fund's managers must select specific dividend-paying stocks and bonds that will perform well over cycles. This requires conviction about which sectors and geographies will be most resilient in a downturn. In the 2008 financial crisis, managers who overweighted financial stocks saw brutal losses, even if those companies eventually recovered. Managers who tilted toward utilities and consumer staples and avoided leverage fared better. The skill to make these calls consistently is real, but it is also rare, and most active funds underperform their passive equivalents over long periods.

LFAW differs from LFAO primarily in time horizon and expected starting age. Five years is not trivial—a twenty-year-old has a very different investment horizon than a fifteen-year-old—but both funds operate on the same core philosophy: automatic rebalancing toward safety as a specific life moment approaches.

The investor to whom LFAW appeals is someone who has a strong belief that 2060 is their target retirement date, values the hands-off nature of automatic rebalancing, and wants a portfolio that is explicitly designed to generate income, not capital gains. Such a person avoids the need to manually adjust their allocation over time and sidesteps the emotional difficulty of selling stocks after a bear market. The cost is that they must accept whatever outcome the manager's stock-picking delivers and they cannot easily adjust the glide path if their circumstances change.

The key research points are the fund's prospectus (which shows the exact allocation at each date), the fact sheet (which shows current holdings and their [dividend yields](/dividend-yield/)), and the fund sponsor's long-term track record with similar target-date products. A buyer should verify that the 2060 date matches their actual expected retirement year, not rely on the name. Someone retiring at fifty-five needs different math than someone retiring at sixty-five. Once aligned, the real work is assessing whether the manager's stock-picking skill is worth the active fees, or whether a passive [target-date fund](/target-date-fund/) would serve equally well for lower cost.
