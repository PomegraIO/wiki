When LaFayette Acquisition Corp. opened for IPO investors in October 2025, the standard way to buy in was the unit — a package deal bundling one ordinary share and one redeemable warrant into a single security trading under the symbol LAFAU. Units are the SPAC market's most common entry point. They are marketed as a lower-friction way to gain exposure to both the equity and the leverage of a warrant without having to parse two separate trading instruments.

The LAFAU units began trading on Nasdaq on October 24, 2025, priced at $10.00 per unit in the IPO, with 11.5 million units issued. Each warrant embedded in the units can be exercised to purchase one additional share at $11.50 per share. The units themselves are meant to be held only temporarily; investors are meant to eventually separate them into distinct shares and warrants once the company begins secondary trading in those components.

## The unit structure and investor logistics

Understanding how a SPAC unit works requires understanding what happens over time. At IPO, you buy the whole package. Your money sits in a trust account managed by a custodian. The ordinary shares and warrants have already been separated at the company level — the unit just bundles them contractually for trading purposes. At some point, usually within a few months of the IPO, the SPAC announces a date when unit holders can elect to separate their holdings into individual shares and warrants. After that date, the shares trade under one symbol (in LaFayette's case, LAFA) and the warrants trade under another (LAFAR).

This choreography matters for the cyclical story. In a bull market, SPAC units often trade at a modest premium to the net cash in the trust account. Investors are bidding up the embedded warrant, betting that the SPAC will find a target and that a successful merger will send the stock soaring. The warrant becomes a call option on that upside. The spread between the unit price and the cash backing widens. But in a bear market, when growth stocks are out of favor and the fear of being trapped in a worthless SPAC is acute, units often trade below trust value — a sign that investors would rather redeem and take their money back than hold the warrant and the equity uncertainty.

## LaFayette's capitalization and governance

The $115 million raised at the October 2025 IPO flows into the trust account, where it is held pending completion of a qualifying acquisition or merger. The sponsors — the insiders who formed the company — hold founder shares worth nothing until a deal closes and the company completes its merger. The underwriters captured fees on the IPO; the insiders are now betting their founder shares that they can identify, negotiate, and close a compelling target.

The company has 24 months from October 2025 to complete a business combination. That window is firm. If no deal closes by October 2027, the trust account is liquidated and returned to public shareholders in proportion to their holdings, and the company winds down. For unit holders who have not yet separated their holdings, the economics are straightforward: they get the trust value back, and the embedded warrant expires worthless because the company ceases to exist.

## The cyclical extremes

The LAFAU unit embeds a view on whether LaFayette will find a good target in favorable market conditions. When the cycle is in expansion and late-stage capital is scarce, private companies are eager for a public listing path, and targets are abundant and reasonably priced. Sponsors hunting for deals have an easier time finding and closing one. Unit holders who hold through separation and into the merged company can see their warrant appreciation amplified. When the cycle is in contraction, targets are scarce or overvalued, sponsors are less motivated because no SPAC is likely to fund their vision, and even completed mergers become drag-your-feet exercises because the macro is deteriorating.

## What moves the market

The price of LAFAU units before separation is driven by three factors: the net cash in the trust (a floor, roughly), the implicit value the market assigns to the warrant, and momentum and sentiment about SPAC deals as a category. When broad market sentiment on blank-check companies sours — perhaps due to high-profile merger failures or regulatory scrutiny — even a well-sponsored SPAC like LaFayette sees unit prices compressed. When sentiment is hot, the warrant premium can be fat. The trick for unit holders is timing the separation and redemption decision based on market conditions and confidence in the sponsor team.

Once separated, the unit ceases to exist; you own shares and warrants individually. The share behaves like a SPAC share awaiting news of a merger; the warrant is a dated call option with a specific strike and expiration. Warrants on well-known SPACs sometimes trade on their own market, but LaFayette's, given its youth and European roots, may face thinner trading. That illiquidity is itself a cyclical risk: in downturns, warrant liquidity dries up first, leaving holders with positions they cannot easily exit.
