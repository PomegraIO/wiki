XRPI is an inverse exchange-traded fund: it is supposed to go up when XRP goes down. For a smart teenager asking what it does, here's the plain answer. If you think XRP will fall in price and you want to make money when that happens, you would normally have to borrow XRP on a cryptocurrency exchange and sell it — complicated, risky, and not available to ordinary investors with a brokerage account. XRPI lets you do that through a normal buy-and-hold trade in your account. When XRP price drops, the fund's shares go up. When XRP price rises, the fund's shares go down.

## How an inverse fund works

The fund does not hold actual XRP. Instead, it holds derivatives — futures contracts and swap agreements that increase in value when XRP price falls. Every day, the fund re-balances these holdings to maintain its target of moving roughly in the opposite direction to XRP price movements. This daily reset is crucial to understand. If XRP drops 10 percent on day one, XRPI should rise around 10 percent. But then on day two, XRPI's value has moved up from day one, and the fund adjusts its derivatives positions to keep aiming at the same target. This rebalancing creates what mathematicians call decay: if markets whip back and forth without a clear direction, the fund will gradually lose value even if XRP ends the period where it started.

Because inverse funds are designed for short-term moves, not long-term holds, they are best suited to traders betting on a specific decline over days or weeks, not investors planning to hold them for months or years.

## Leverage and daily decay — the real risk

XRPI is technically an inverse fund, but some versions of inverse cryptocurrency funds add leverage: they might aim to move 2 or 3 times as far in the opposite direction as XRP. This magnifies both gains and losses, and it makes decay far worse. If XRPI is leveraged 1x (straight inverse, no multiplier), holding it through a period of sideways or choppy price movement will drain money through the daily rebalancing cost, even if XRP ends exactly where it started. If it is leveraged 2x, that decay accelerates dramatically. A reader should check the fund's prospectus to see the exact leverage ratio, which will be spelled out clearly.

The mechanical risk is not controversial or hidden — the prospectus explains it, and it is baked into the fund's construction by design. It is a cost of using leverage and short exposure, and investors accept it consciously. But it is easy to underestimate in practice. A 2x inverse fund that is held through a period of high volatility can lose half its value or more even if the underlying asset does not move sharply in the wrong direction.

## Custody and pricing

Like other cryptocurrency ETFs, XRPI relies on digital asset custody providers to manage the underlying derivatives. The fund charges annual fees that cover operations, custody, and the cost of rolling over the derivative positions. It trades over-the-counter, so bid-ask spreads will be wider than a large, liquid fund, and trading around major cryptocurrency news or market moves can be difficult.

Pricing is set by the market — buy and sell orders from traders determine the fund's trading price, which may drift away from the per-share value of the underlying derivative holdings, especially in thin trading. This mismatch between the fund's net asset value and its trading price is called a premium or discount.

## Who should own this and how to research

Inverse funds are instruments for active traders with a short-term view, not core portfolio holdings. Someone holding XRPI for more than a few weeks faces an uphill battle against decay, even if they are right about XRP's direction. Someone holding it for months or years is almost guaranteed to lose money due to the daily rebalancing cost, unless XRP falls very sharply.

A reader considering this fund should read its prospectus to understand the exact leverage ratio, the fee structure, and the daily reset mechanics. Then examine recent XRP price history to get a feel for the cryptocurrency's volatility — the more volatile the underlying, the faster decay eats away at an inverse fund's value. Do not rely on recent performance of the fund itself as a guide to the future, because the daily reset mechanics make past returns highly misleading.
