*The **US Treasury 6 Month Bill ETF** (XBIL) is an exchange-traded fund that holds a rolling portfolio of U.S. [Treasury bills](/treasury-bill/) — short-term IOUs issued by the federal government with maturities of six months or less. It is intended as a liquid, low-cost way to own government debt without the volatility of longer-term [bonds](/bond/).*

## What XBIL holds

At any given moment, XBIL's portfolio is a ladder of Treasury bills in their final six months of life. When a bill in the fund matures — which happens frequently, since the bills are so short-dated — the fund reinvests the proceeds in newly issued bills further out the curve. The result is that the fund is continuously rolling forward into fresh debt issuance by the U.S. Treasury, without any meaningful duration drift. The fund's average maturity sits very close to three months, a snapshot that reflects the rolling nature of the underlying securities.

Treasury bills are among the safest securities in the world, backed by the full faith and credit of the United States government. Unlike longer-dated [Treasury bonds](/treasury-bond/), bills do not fluctuate much in price if [interest rates](/interest-rate/) move — their very short maturity means that any given bill will pay off in weeks or months, so the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) (NAV) is highly stable. That stability is the appeal. Investors who want government exposure without the price swings of the bond market, or who are parking cash while deciding where to deploy it, find bill ETFs appealing because the shares trade like a cash position but with the certainty of a government backing.

## The mechanics and structure

XBIL is a straightforward exchange-traded fund; it owns the actual Treasury bills and distributes to shareholders any interest income the bills earn. There is no leverage, no inverse exposure, no exotic derivatives. The fund trades on an exchange during market hours just like a stock, so an investor can buy or sell shares intraday at market prices. The sponsor, BlackRock's iShares division, handles the continuous rebalancing and reinvestment as bills mature.

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is quite low — typical for a government bill fund — because the assets are simply sitting in the safest, most liquid corner of the financial system. There are no complex trading or hedging costs, and no illiquidity premium. Transaction costs for buying and selling shares are typically tight because the fund is large and actively traded.

[Dividends](/dividend/) arrive regularly, usually monthly or quarterly, as the holdings accrue interest. The yield (the annual income per dollar invested) tracks closely to the prevailing Treasury bill rate — the short-term interest rate set by market forces and influenced by the [Federal Reserve's](/federal-reserve/) policy stance. When short rates are high, XBIL's yield is high; when rates fall, so does the fund's income.

## Who owns it and why

XBIL serves a particular niche: investors and financial professionals who need a cash-equivalent position that sits in the securities market. These include money-market funds that reinvest excess reserves, portfolios waiting for a clear entry point into equities or longer bonds, risk-averse savers who find the yield appealing, and institutions managing short-term liabilities. The fund is liquid and tax-efficient compared to holding actual Treasury bills in a brokerage account, and it avoids the minimum purchase sizes and paperwork of direct Treasury ownership.

For ordinary retail investors, XBIL is most useful as a "holding tank" — a place to keep [dry powder](/pe-dry-powder/) without accepting the volatility of stocks or the [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) of bonds, while earning whatever the market is paying on very short-term government debt. The stability of the underlying holdings means the share price is predictable; the risk is not the fund losing value, but the yield being low if the Federal Reserve has cut rates aggressively.

## Duration, interest-rate risk, and the yield curve

Because XBIL holds only bills maturing within six months, the fund has negligible duration — the mathematical measure of how much a bond's price moves when rates change. A 0.5% move in short-term interest rates affects the fund's NAV by roughly 0.02% or less. Over a year, this translates to a fund that behaves almost exactly like cash earning interest, not a bond that swings with the economic cycle.

This is both a strength and a constraint. The strength is certainty: an investor knows that XBIL will not suddenly drop 10% if the Fed raises rates again. The constraint is that the fund offers no capital appreciation if longer-term rates fall — it misses the gains that accrue to investors holding bonds with years of maturity remaining. XBIL is a trade-off for stability, not for opportunity.

## Research and considerations

Anyone considering XBIL should understand what it is: not an investment that offers growth or capital appreciation, but a very-low-risk vehicle for earning government interest rates on cash balances. The fund's performance and yield are driven almost entirely by the Federal Reserve's short-term rate policy and the market's expectations around future policy.

Start by checking the current Treasury bill rate — widely available on the Federal Reserve's website and the U.S. Treasury's site — and comparing it to XBIL's latest yield. The fund's yield should track closely to the prevailing short-rate environment. If it is much lower, there may be trading or expense friction. The fund's prospectus and fact sheet (available from [BlackRock](/blk-stock/)) lay out the holdings in detail, though with such short maturities and frequent turnover, the exact composition changes daily.
