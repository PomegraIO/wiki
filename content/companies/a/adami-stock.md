## What are Adamas Trust's senior notes, and why does the company issue them?

Adamas Trust, through its various debt securities, funds the purchase of mortgage loans and securities that form the core of its business. The 9.125% Senior Notes due 2029 (ticker ADAMI) are unsecured senior obligations of the company, meaning they rank ahead of equity holders in a bankruptcy but behind any secured borrowing. Most of Adamas' funding comes from [repurchase agreements](/repurchase-agreement/) (repo)—short-term borrowing from financial institutions collateralized by mortgage securities. Senior notes provide longer-dated, fixed-rate funding that gives Adamas a stable cost of capital. When Adamas' portfolio is earning a net interest margin above the cost of these notes, the arbitrage is profitable. When the margin compresses (because short-term rates rise faster than long-term rates, or because [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) tighten), the notes become expensive relative to earnings, and the company may reduce leverage or let notes mature without refinancing.

## How do interest-rate cycles affect the value of these notes and their attractiveness to investors?

The 9.125% coupon on ADAMI was set at issuance. The notes trade in the secondary [bond](/bond/) market at prices that fluctuate with credit spreads and general interest-rate movements. If new Adamas notes are issued at lower coupons (because rates have fallen), existing ADAMI notes become more valuable and may trade above par. Conversely, if newly issued notes trade at higher yields (because [credit risk](/credit-risk/) premiums widen or rates rise), ADAMI may trade below par. The callable nature of senior notes matters too: Adamas will refinance or redeem notes when borrowing costs fall, capping the upside for note holders. For Adamas itself, issuing notes in low-rate environments—when long-term funding is cheap—is sensible; issuing in high-rate environments is expensive and a sign that management believes rates will fall or leverage must shrink.

## What risks do holders of ADAMI face?

The central risk is credit risk: if Adamas' mortgage portfolio suffers large losses, [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) shrinks, and the company may lack the [equity cushion](/equity-cushion/) to cover debt payments. This risk is material in a sharp credit downturn (e.g., a [recession](/recession/) where residential defaults spike or multifamily properties falter). Adamas maintains leverage of roughly 4.9x, meaning equity represents only about one-fifth of total capital. A portfolio-wide loss of more than 20% would threaten the full recovery of debt holders. Second, [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) is asymmetric for note holders: if rates fall, the notes' market value may rise slightly, but the issuer (Adamas) may refinance and retire them, capping gains. If rates rise, the market value falls and note holders suffer unless they hold to maturity. Third, [liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/) affects secondary-market trading: when financial stress hits, [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) on [mortgage REIT](/mortgage-reit/) debt can widen sharply, and sellers may face price concessions.

## How cyclical are returns on these notes across boom and bust?

In the early stages of a boom (when the Fed is cutting rates and risk appetite is rising), Adamas notes may trade wider and offer attractive yields, because the market is priced for stress. As the cycle progresses and credit spreads tighten, the notes may appreciate in the [secondary market](/secondary-market/), rewarding early buyers. At the peak of a cycle, spreads are tight, new issuance by Adamas may occur at tighter coupons, and returns are compressed to the coupon alone. When the cycle turns and risk premiums widen again, existing ADAMI notes become attractive on a relative-value basis, and note holders who bought at the trough may face small mark-to-market losses but are compensated by the higher yield. Holders who buy at the peak face the reverse: slight price appreciation but depressed relative value. The key insight is that senior notes issued by a mortgage REIT are not passive fixed-income instruments—they are levered bets on the net interest margin and credit health of the REIT's portfolio, and that exposure is cyclical.

## What does Adamas' balance sheet and leverage tell an investor about note safety?

As of the most recent filings, Adamas carries roughly $10.9 billion in total assets with a recourse [leverage ratio](/leverage-ratio-forex/) around 4.9x. This means the company borrows about $3.90 for every $1.00 of equity. The leverage ratio is not low, but it is manageable and typical for mortgage REITs. Equity-to-assets is roughly 20%, which gives some cushion for loss absorption. However, the quality and duration of the liabilities matter. If Adamas has large short-dated repo funding, a sudden spike in [repo rates](/repo-rate/) would squeeze the margin. If the company has long-dated senior notes like ADAMI, that part of the capital structure is locked in at the [coupon rate](/coupon-rate/). Adamas has been disciplined about staggering debt maturities to avoid [refinancing risk](/refinancing-risk/), though the 2029 maturity of ADAMI is now in the near term (as of 2026) and refinancing conditions will matter. The company does not publish an explicit guarantee on note repayment from parent entities or do any [securitization](/securitization/) of collateral; the notes are backed simply by the net worth and earnings power of the consolidated REIT.

## How should an investor monitor Adamas' credit quality and the performance of ADAMI?

Watch the quarterly filings for trends in net interest margin, realized gains or losses on asset sales, and the composition of the portfolio. A widening margin suggests the REIT is benefiting from the current rate environment. A narrowing margin is a warning that leverage is becoming costly. Realized gains indicate the company is trading assets and crystallizing value; large realized losses signal stress in the portfolio. The leverage ratio should stay stable or decline over time; if it rises, it suggests the company is taking on more risk or facing losses that erode equity. Also track the [default rate](/default-rate/) and [delinquency](/delinquency/) trends in the underlying mortgages—these feed into the 10-K disclosures and are early signals of credit stress. If defaults begin to rise, ADAMI note holders should take note that loss risk is increasing. Finally, watch the stock price and REIT sector movements: when mortgage REIT stocks are selling off, spreads on their bonds often widen, creating mark-to-market pressure on ADAMI. Holders should be prepared for price volatility and should only buy if they can hold to maturity or have a clear exit plan. The 9.125% coupon is attractive in an absolute sense, but only if credit risk is adequately compensated by the yield spread above comparable-duration [risk-free rates](/risk-free-rate/).
