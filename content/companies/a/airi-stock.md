*[AIR INDUSTRIES GROUP](/wiki/airi-stock/) (ticker **AIRI**) is an aerospace and defense parts supplier that manufactures electrical and mechanical systems, assemblies, and components for commercial and military aircraft. The company operates as a contract manufacturer serving major aerospace primes and integrators across production and aftermarket channels.*

## What the company does

AIR INDUSTRIES GROUP manufactures and supplies precision components and assemblies for the aerospace sector. Its product portfolio includes electrical and mechanical systems used in aircraft production, ranging from landing gear components and hydraulic systems to electrical connectors and cable assemblies. The company serves both new aircraft production and aftermarket maintenance and repair segments, supporting commercial airframe manufacturers, defense primes, and military branches.

## Market position and customers

As a mid-tier supplier, the company competes in a fragmented aerospace components market dominated by larger integrated defense contractors. AIR INDUSTRIES depends on prime contracts flowing from major aircraft manufacturers such as Boeing and Airbus, as well as defense budgets. Its customer concentration—with a small number of large OEMs—creates revenue exposure tied to those customers' production schedules and program funding. The company has historically supplied components across multiple aircraft platforms, providing some revenue diversification within the aerospace sector.

## How it makes money

AIR INDUSTRIES generates revenue through contracts for the design, manufacture, and delivery of aerospace components and assemblies. Revenue streams include production volume orders from aircraft manufacturers (pricing often tied to build rates) and spare parts sales for in-service aircraft. Gross margins on aerospace parts typically reflect the precision and regulatory requirements of the sector, though contract pricing and supplier consolidation pressures can compress profitability. The company's cost structure includes labor, materials, tooling, and quality/compliance expenses related to AS9100 and other aerospace standards.

## Capital intensity and operational challenges

The aerospace components business requires significant capital for tooling, quality systems, and facility maintenance to maintain AS9100 certifications and FAA compliance. The company must also manage working capital tied to long payment cycles from major OEMs, which can delay cash collection. Inventory management, procurement of specialized materials, and skilled labor availability represent operational considerations. Dependence on a small set of large customers introduces concentration risk; loss of a major contract or reduction in aircraft production can significantly impact revenue.

## Where it sits in the industry

AIR INDUSTRIES occupies a position as a second-tier or component-level supplier within the aerospace value chain, below the major integrated defense contractors (Lockheed, Raytheon, General Dynamics) and peer companies like Esterline or Ducommun. The company competes with other specialized suppliers and is subject to consolidation pressures in the aerospace supply base. Its survival depends on maintaining quality certifications, cost competitiveness, and relationships with major primes—particularly as consolidation in both defense and commercial aviation continues.

## Cyclicality and market drivers

The aerospace sector is cyclical, tied to commercial aviation demand, aircraft order backlogs, and defense budgets. Production rates for commercial aircraft can swing sharply with economic cycles and airline capital discipline, directly affecting demand for components. Government defense spending and military platform development cycles also influence revenue. Aftermarket demand tends to lag new production by years, creating additional revenue volatility.
