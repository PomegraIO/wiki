**ADT is among the largest professional security monitoring companies in North America**, serving both residential and commercial customers through a network of monitoring centers. The company operates in the fragmented home security market, where it occupies a position built on nearly two centuries of brand recognition and a subscriber base spanning millions of households and businesses.

The core business model relies on recurring revenue from monitoring fees rather than equipment sales. Customers sign contracts typically ranging from two to five years, paying monthly for 24/7 professional monitoring of their alarm systems. This structure creates predictable cash flows, though it also exposes the business to subscriber churn—the rate at which customers cancel their contracts. ADT's customer [acquisition](/acquisition/) strategy centers on direct sales forces, partnerships with telecommunications providers, and digital marketing, balanced against the costs of installation, technician dispatch, and monitoring center operations.

The security monitoring sector has fragmented significantly in recent decades. Where ADT once faced primarily regional competitors, it now competes with national competitors like Vivint and Frontpoint, DIY options like Ring and SimpliSafe that require less human labor, and cellular-based systems that bypass traditional hardwired phone lines. This shift has pressured margins across the industry and forced established players like ADT to modernize their technology stack, reduce operational costs, and experiment with tiered service models that capture price-sensitive customers while maintaining premium service tiers.

ADT's profitability hinges on retention, average revenue per user, and the efficiency of its monitoring infrastructure. The company has pursued cost reduction through automation, remote support capabilities, and consolidation of its monitoring centers. Capital intensity is moderate relative to peers—subscribers generate recurring payments against relatively fixed monitoring center costs once built out, creating potential for operating leverage as the installed base grows or stabilizes.

The business is sensitive to [housing starts](/housing-starts/), consumer confidence, and broadband penetration, since declining landline adoption shifted monitoring toward broadband and cellular pathways. A significant portion of revenue comes from commercial customers, including small businesses and enterprise accounts, which tend to have different churn patterns and contract economics than residential segments. Economic downturns can pressure both new customer acquisition and retention as households defer security upgrades or cancel services.
