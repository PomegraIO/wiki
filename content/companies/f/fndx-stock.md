The Schwab Fundamental U.S. Large Company ETF (FNDX) is a passive, open-ended fund that tracks a fundamentals-weighted index of U.S. large-cap equities. Rather than weighting its holdings by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) — the standard approach in indices like the S&P 500 — FNDX weights its companies according to fundamental metrics: book value, [dividend](/dividend/) income, earnings, and sales. The result is a fund that holds the same universe of large-cap names as a traditional broad-market index but tilts itself toward undervalued, high-dividend, and profitable firms. FNDX trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) under its ticker and functions as a low-cost complement or alternative to cap-weighted large-cap funds.

## Origins and the rise of fundamental indexing

FNDX was born in 2006, in the wake of growing academic interest in alternatives to market-cap weighting. The traditional cap-weighted approach — where the largest companies by market value get the largest weights in an index — had an obvious mathematical consequence: it automatically loaded investors most heavily into whatever stocks the market had recently bid up highest. In the [dot-com bubble](/dot-com-bubble/) of the late 1990s, this had meant spectacularly poor weightings toward overvalued technology and internet firms. Researchers and [index providers](/index-provider/) began exploring whether other weighting schemes might deliver better long-term returns.

The fundamentals approach, championed most publicly by Rob Arnott's Research Affiliates, proposed an inversion: weight companies not by their market price but by their underlying financial strength. A company with high book value, strong earnings, or substantial dividends got a larger slice; expensive, low-earning companies got a smaller slice. Theoretically, this bias toward value could outperform over time. Schwab brought this approach to retail investors with FNDX, building an index that selected the 500 largest U.S. firms by market cap but then reweighted them according to a formula combining book value, cash dividends, earnings, and sales.

## How the index works

The Schwab U.S. Large-Cap [Fundamental Index](/fundamental-index/) begins with the 500 largest U.S. companies by market capitalization — essentially the S&P 500 constituent universe. It then applies a fundamentals-weighting scheme: each company's weight is determined by its combined book value, trailing-twelve-month dividends, trailing-twelve-month earnings, and sales, rebalanced annually. This means a firm like a cheap, profitable, high-dividend bank might have a much larger weight in the index than its market cap would suggest, while a low-earning, high-valuation software company might have less.

The result is a portfolio that typically holds 400 to 450 positions (fewer than the full 500, because some firms fail to meet the weighting criteria or have been assigned zero weight). The fund holds ordinary stocks — no leverage, no shorting, no derivatives — making it a straightforward long-only ETF. Holdings are rebalanced once per year, usually in August or September, which keeps trading costs and tax consequences modest.

Because the index selects only from the largest 500 companies by market cap, FNDX offers broad [diversification](/diversification/) across sectors and names. Unlike focused strategies or individual sector bets, it is exposed to technology, financials, industrials, consumer staples, healthcare, and energy. Compared to a cap-weighted large-cap fund, FNDX tilts toward sectors and stocks favored by its fundamentals weighting — notably banks, energy, and consumer staples — and away from the most expensive growth and technology names.

## Cost and liquidity

FNDX has an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) near the bottom of the industry's cost spectrum — among the cheapest passive large-cap U.S. equity funds available. Schwab's retail ownership structure and its integration with the broader Schwab platform (brokerage, advisory) have allowed it to maintain unusually low fees, a strategic priority for the firm. The fund is large and liquid, trading millions of shares daily with tight [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/), so retail and institutional investors can buy or sell quickly without moving the price.

## The fundamentals-weighting trade-off

The distinguishing feature of FNDX is also its greatest risk: the fundamentals tilt means it is not truly index-neutral. In years when valuation-insensitive growth stocks outperform, FNDX underperforms its cap-weighted peers because it holds less of the winning momentum names. Conversely, in years when value and dividends dominate, FNDX tends to outperform. The academic case for [fundamental weighting](/fundamental-weighting/) rests on the expectation that value and quality outperform growth over very long periods — but that is a directional bet, not a passive bet on the market as a whole.

This is a feature for investors who believe in fundamentals-based value and seek a diversified way to gain that tilt without picking individual stocks. It is a bug for investors who want pure market exposure. In the 2010s, a period when large-cap growth stocks dominated, FNDX trailed a standard cap-weighted index. The fund's performance relative to the S&P 500 swings considerably based on the investment environment.

## Who uses FNDX and how to research it

FNDX appeals to investors seeking low-cost large-cap exposure with a deliberate tilt toward value, dividends, and fundamental metrics. It is neither a passive bet on the market nor an active stock-picker's fund, but rather a passive vehicle for a specific investment philosophy. Individuals building a diversified portfolio might use FNDX as a large-cap core holding, or pair it with a cap-weighted counterpart to diversify their style exposure. Some advisors use it to reduce the heavy weight to the largest mega-cap technology stocks that dominates cap-weighted indices.

To understand FNDX and evaluate whether it fits a portfolio, start with Schwab's fund fact sheet and prospectus, available on schwab.com. The prospectus details the index methodology, the selection and weighting rules, and the fund's expenses. The fund's [annual report](/10-k/) breaks down holdings by sector and shows the actual composition; tracking the most recent report reveals FNDX's current style tilt relative to the broader market. Investors should compare FNDX's performance over rolling multi-year periods against the S&P 500 or other cap-weighted large-cap funds to see whether the fundamentals tilt has added or subtracted value in recent years. Because the fund's outperformance or underperformance depends on whether value and dividends are in or out of favor, a long time horizon and a clear understanding of style cyclicality are important to avoiding regret.
