**Fund snapshot:** FFLC targets U.S. large-cap equities with an active fundamental approach, 0.38% [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/), benchmarked to the S&P 500. Inception: June 2020. Portfolio: ~104 holdings, $964.6 million under management.

---

FFLC invests at least 80% of assets in equity securities of U.S. companies with large market capitalisations—essentially the same universe as the S&P 500. The fund does not track that index passively. Instead, it uses fundamental analysis to select roughly 100 stocks from the 500 available, concentrating on companies that Fidelity's managers believe offer the best risk-adjusted returns at current valuations.

**The active overlay.** Holding 104 names versus the S&P 500's 500 is a deliberate choice. The subset approach means FFLC will lag in environments where the excluded names outperform (tech mega-caps, for instance, during AI-driven rallies), and outperform when the market rewards disciplined stock-picking over cap-weighted exposure. This [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) is the flip side of conviction: by leaving out stocks they view as overvalued, managers accept the possibility of underperformance when those stocks rally anyway.

**Valuation discipline matters here.** The fund benchmarks to the S&P 500, so long-term tracking of FFLC's returns against that index tells the whole story. From inception in June 2020 through 2025, active management's success varies: some periods saw outperformance; others saw FFLC trail as value factors faded or as mega-cap technology dominated. The 0.38% expense ratio, below the average for active U.S. equity funds, means outperformance does not need to be heroic for the fund to justify its existence—even a small annual edge over the benchmark pays for the fee. But because FFLC is concentrated (relatively few holdings), single-stock volatility can be higher than a fully diversified approach.

**Portfolio composition tilts.** With 104 holdings against the S&P 500's 500, the fund must avoid some names. If managers are underweight financials and overweight industrials, that allocation gap shapes returns independently of stock-picking skill. Sectors like utilities and consumer staples sometimes appear overweight in FFLC, suggesting the managers see value there; technology often appears underweight, signalling caution toward expensive mega-caps. These sector tilts are a natural outcome of fundamental selection but deserve scrutiny—are they deliberate bets, or side effects of the stock-picking process?

**Fee structure and opportunity cost.** At 0.38%, FFLC costs less than most active managers (who charge 0.6% to 1%+) but more than a passive S&P 500 tracker (which costs 0.03% to 0.05%). For the fee to be worthwhile over a 20-year hold, FFLC must deliver at least 0.38% of annual outperformance before fees to match a tracker's returns after all costs. Fidelity's track record with fundamental active management is strong across its business, but past performance is no guarantee of future results, especially as passive competition has grown and markets have become more efficient.

**How to evaluate FFLC.** Investors should track the fund's rolling one-, three-, and five-year returns versus the S&P 500 to see whether active management is paying off. Check the portfolio's sector and stock-specific positioning—is the fund concentrated in a few names or diversified across its 104? Look at the fund's price-to-earnings, price-to-book, and [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) relative to the S&P 500 to understand the fund's valuation stance. Read the fund's prospectus and fact sheet for details on the fundamental stock-picking process and the managers' investment philosophy. Because FFLC is not a passive [index fund](/index-fund/), understanding what the managers believe they are adding is as important as watching whether they actually add it.
