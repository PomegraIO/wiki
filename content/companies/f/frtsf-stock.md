Fortis Inc. is a utility company that operates the infrastructure delivering electricity and [natural gas](/natural-gas/) to millions of customers across Canada and the United States. The company owns and runs the poles, wires, and distribution networks that carry power from generation facilities to homes and businesses. It also operates some power plants and participates in energy trading and distribution. The business model rests on a simple principle: in most markets, one utility is designated the local monopoly provider, granted the right to be the only seller in exchange for allowing regulators to set its rates and review its spending decisions.

This regulated monopoly model is foundational to understanding Fortis. Unlike a manufacturing company that competes on price and innovation, Fortis cannot be undercut by a rival; it faces no competition. But it also cannot charge arbitrary prices. Instead, regulators approve rates based on a formula that typically allows the utility a specific [return on invested capital](/return-on-invested-capital/)—usually 8–10 percent. This means Fortis cannot earn spectacular returns, but it also cannot be bankrupted by losing market share. The returns are stable and predictable, which is why utility stocks appeal to investors seeking steady [dividends](/dividend/) rather than capital appreciation.

Fortis grew from a small regional utility in Newfoundland in 1987 into a large holding company operating subsidiaries across ten provinces and states. Each subsidiary—Newfoundland Light & Power, FortisBC, Fortis Alberta, MidAmerican Energy, and others—operates as a quasi-independent business within its regulatory jurisdiction. This geographic and jurisdictional diversity was deliberate: by spreading across many regulatory regimes, Fortis reduced its dependence on any single regulator or geography. A harsh regulator in Ontario cannot damage the company if its British Columbia and Arizona utilities are performing well.

The business generates revenue from two sources: electric and natural gas distribution. Customers pay a per-unit charge for each kilowatt-hour consumed and a fixed monthly fee for access to the network. The utility's cost of service—the wages of lineworkers, the maintenance of poles and transformers, the cost of capital to build new infrastructure—are largely fixed. This means that each new customer added, or each percentage increase in consumption, flows almost entirely to profit. Conversely, when consumption falls, the company's costs fall only slowly, putting pressure on returns.

The company also owns generation assets—power plants that produce electricity—which earn returns in two ways. Some are operated under long-term contracts that guarantee a fixed return, treating them much like distribution assets. Others compete in wholesale electricity markets where the utility sells power at whatever price the market sets in that hour. These competitive generation assets are more volatile: they earn high returns when demand is strong and electricity prices spike, but suffer when supply is abundant and prices are low.

The energy sector is undergoing a structural transition that directly affects Fortis. Regulators across North America are pushing utilities toward renewable energy and away from fossil fuels. This creates both opportunity and risk. Fortis earns investment opportunities when it builds wind farms or solar installations, which regulators typically allow it to recover through rate increases. But the shift also threatens the core gas distribution business: as homes and businesses switch from natural gas heating to electric heat pumps, gas consumption falls, and the gas utility's revenue declines. Regulators are increasingly skeptical of approving investments to maintain or grow gas distribution systems if the long-term trend is clear decline.

Recent regulatory developments have added uncertainty to the business. Regulators in British Columbia, California, and other jurisdictions have become more aggressive in scrutinizing utility earnings and sometimes denying requested rate increases. Some have implemented performance-based regulation, where utilities must demonstrate efficiency improvements or face penalties. Others have cut the allowed [return on equity](/return-on-equity/), forcing utilities to operate at tighter margins. This regulatory tightening is gradually eroding the predictability that made utilities attractive as dividend stocks.

Fortis has tried to position itself for the transition by investing in renewable generation, electric-vehicle charging networks, and energy-efficiency programs. But the pace is slow, constrained by the capital-intensive nature of utilities and the lag between investment and regulatory approval. Meanwhile, the company still generates substantial cash from its core gas distribution business, which is profitable today even if it may be obsolete in thirty years. This creates a strategic tension: the company must transition to survive the long term, but its current cash flows depend on the legacy business.

The company's financial profile reflects this balancing act. Fortis carries substantial debt—necessary to finance its large capital programs—but maintains investment-grade [credit ratings](/credit-rating/) because regulated utilities are considered low-risk. The dividend is maintained and usually grows slowly, appealing to retirees and income-focused investors. Capital expenditure remains in the billions annually, directed toward grid modernization, renewable integration, and system maintenance. The company's profitability is stable but modest—not the kind of business that produces outsized returns, but one that provides dependable income streams insulated from competitive pressure.

For investors, the question around Fortis is whether the regulated utility model can persist in a world demanding a faster transition to clean energy. Will regulators allow utilities time to gradually shift their portfolios, or will they accelerate the transition and force utilities to accept lower returns on legacy assets? And can utilities like Fortis find enough investable opportunities in renewables and grid modernization to maintain their dividend-growth profile even as their traditional business shrinks? The answers depend on regulatory decisions that lie years ahead, which is why utility stocks, despite their historical stability, now carry political and policy risk.
