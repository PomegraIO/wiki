Fat Brands, Inc., trading over-the-counter under the ticker FATPQ, is a restaurant company that owns and franchises a portfolio of casual-dining and quick-service brands. The company operates as both a franchisor (licensing its brand and operational systems to independent franchisees) and in some cases a direct operator of company-owned restaurants. The business model centers on brand ownership and franchise operations rather than capital-intensive company-owned store networks.

## Fatburger — the flagship brand

Fatburger is a heritage fast-casual burger chain with origins in Southern California, known for customizable chargrilled burgers and a focus on premium ingredients. The brand operates hundreds of locations globally across company-owned and franchised formats. For Fat Brands, Fatburger generates revenue through franchise fees, royalties on franchisee sales, and operations of any company-owned locations. The brand's value depends on its ability to attract franchisees willing to invest capital in opening new locations and on consistent customer demand at existing restaurants.

Fatburger's supply chain mirrors that of typical quick-service restaurants: sourcing beef, buns, and condiments from suppliers; managing inventory; and ensuring operational consistency across the franchise network. The brand's positioning as a premium burger concept carries both benefits (higher average unit volume potential) and risks (vulnerability to economic downturns, sensitivity to beef prices, competition from well-capitalized chains).

## Johnny Rockets — casual dining brand

Johnny Rockets is a casual-dining chain emphasizing burgers, milkshakes, and a retro American diner aesthetic. The format is higher-touch than Fatburger, with sit-down service and a fuller menu that includes salads, sandwiches, and entrées. Johnny Rockets locations are typically larger and require greater investment from franchisees than quick-service formats do. The brand operates domestically and internationally, with significant exposure to Asia-Pacific markets.

Casual dining as a category has faced structural headwinds over the past decade — rising labor costs, changing consumer preferences toward fast-casual and delivery, and increased competition from both independent restaurants and national chains. Johnny Rockets' profitability depends on the franchisees' ability to achieve acceptable unit economics in this challenging environment and on the brand's continued appeal to its target customer demographic.

## Other brands and portfolio

Fat Brands has acquired or developed additional brands in the portfolio, diversifying revenue beyond Fatburger and Johnny Rockets. These may include regional or emerging concepts that the company believes can scale through franchising. The portfolio approach spreads risk: if one brand faces headwinds, others may offset the decline.

## Franchising as a business model

Fat Brands' revenue model is fundamentally different from an operating company with company-owned stores. Franchising generates upfront franchise fees when new locations open, ongoing royalties (typically 5–7% of franchisee sales), and sometimes fees for advertising, technology, or training. The advantage is that franchisees, not Fat Brands, bear the capital expenditure, labor costs, and operational risk of running restaurants. The disadvantage is that the company's growth depends on franchisees' willingness and ability to invest and operate, and the company has limited direct control over customer experience and brand consistency.

During economic downturns, franchisee profitability declines, discouraging new unit growth and risking closure of marginal locations. Fat Brands' revenue becomes volatile and sensitive to macroeconomic conditions, even though the company itself carries minimal operating expense.

## Supply chain and dependencies

Fat Brands depends upstream on its franchisees' capital and operational competence. A franchisee must be able to source real estate, construct or renovate the restaurant, hire and train staff, and sustain operations through the inevitable ramp-up period before consistent profitability. The company provides the brand, the operational playbook, and support services, but execution falls to independent operators.

Downstream, the company depends on consumer demand for burgers, casual dining experiences, and the specific positioning of each brand. Beef prices, labor costs, and real-estate availability all affect franchisee unit economics and hence the attractiveness of opening new locations.

## International expansion and risks

Fat Brands has pursued international growth, particularly in Asia and the Middle East, where American-style casual dining carries novelty appeal and premium positioning. International expansion requires adaptation of menus, careful selection of local franchisees, and navigation of different regulatory and consumer preference landscapes. Foreign exchange fluctuations can affect the company's reported earnings if it consolidates foreign currency revenue.

## Financial profile and capital

As a small-cap franchisor, Fat Brands operates with modest balance-sheet strength. The company has typically carried debt from acquisitions or expansion initiatives and depends on franchisee growth to service that debt and generate shareholder returns. The over-the-counter listing reflects the company's small size relative to major restaurant franchisors and indicates modest analyst coverage and investor attention.

## What a reader should monitor

Investors tracking Fat Brands should focus on new-unit growth (the number of franchised locations opened in each period), same-store sales trends for company-owned locations or reported by franchisees, franchisee sentiment regarding unit economics, and the company's debt and leverage ratios. The 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001705012) details revenue by brand, lease structures, and any material disputes with franchisees.

The sustainability of the franchisor model in casual dining depends on whether franchisees can achieve acceptable returns in an environment of rising costs and shifting consumer preferences. If franchisee profitability deteriorates persistently, the brand's ability to attract investment capital weakens, and growth stalls.
