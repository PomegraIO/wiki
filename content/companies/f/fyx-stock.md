FYX is simple: it buys small-cap stocks and screens them for financial strength. It does not care whether a stock looks cheap or expensive by valuation metrics. It just wants companies with solid [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/), reliable cash flow, good debt management, and steady profitability. Small-cap companies are those worth roughly $300 million to $2 billion. The fund holds about 250 of them, refreshes its list every quarter, and trades on the NYSE under the ticker FYX.

## The core logic

The fund's index takes every small-cap stock available and scores each one on the same financial metrics: [return on assets](/return-on-assets/), quality of operating cash flow, debt levels, and working-capital management. It does not split the world into growth versus value or favour stocks with low prices versus high prices. It just ranks companies on financial strength and holds the 250 that score highest. An equal-weight structure means each of the 250 holdings gets roughly the same portfolio weight, which tilts the fund slightly toward the tiniest companies in the universe. That adds volatility, but it also reaches stocks that large institutions ignore.

First Trust developed this approach in the 2000s as a response to a real problem in [value investing](/value-investing/). Buying only cheap stocks works in some years and falters in others, partly because being cheap is not always rational — sometimes a stock is cheap because the business is deteriorating. The original AlphaDEX insight added quality screens on top of value. FYX went further: why add quality to value, when you could apply quality screens to the entire market and let valuation take care of itself? The fund launched in 2007, just before the financial crisis, survived that stress, and has become a middle-ground choice for investors seeking small-cap exposure without betting on a single style.

## Costs and liquidity

FYX trades during market hours on the NYSE at prices set by supply and demand. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is approximately 0.50% per year. That is higher than a broad passive fund (which costs 0.05–0.15%) but standard for a systematic quality-screening approach. The fund's expenses cover the cost of accessing the data, running the screens, and doing the quarterly rebalancing. Trading volume is moderate—fine for retail investors but thinner than large-cap funds, which can matter if you are trading large positions.

## The risks that matter

Quality screens sound sensible, but they cannot guarantee returns. From 2015 through 2020, growth stocks dominated and investors moved away from traditional quality metrics. FYX held companies with clean balance sheets and steady earnings, yet the fund lagged the broader market during that stretch. This is not a failure of the screening methodology. It is a property of [factor investing](/factor-investing/) itself: any factor you bet on goes through cycles of favour and disfavour, and you cannot predict when those will switch.

Small-cap stocks are inherently more volatile than large caps. A sudden tightening of credit, a [recession](/recession/), or a sector-wide downturn hits small companies harder because they have thinner [operating margins](/operating-margin/) and less access to emergency financing. An equal-weight structure compounds this: holding 250 small positions means higher costs to buy and sell, and wider [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/).

The quarterly rebalancing also creates drag. The fund sells stocks that have fallen out of the top 250 quality scores and buys new ones to replace them. Those trades incur costs and, for taxable investors, can trigger capital-gains taxes. Even mechanical, low-cost rebalancing still costs money over time.

The deepest risk is that financial strength, while important, does not prevent all business failures. A company can have a healthy balance sheet and still face disruption, lose major customers, or suffer from poor management decisions. FYX's screens catch balance-sheet weakness but not every form of business risk.

## How to research it

Start with the prospectus and fact sheet from First Trust, which document the current holdings and the exact screening methodology. Scan the top 10 holdings—you will probably not recognize most of the names, which is expected. Compare FYX's three- and five-year rolling returns to the Russell 2000, the broad small-cap benchmark, to see whether the quality screen has added or subtracted value. Check the fund's annual turnover rate—higher turnover means more trading friction eating into returns over time.

Small-cap funds are inherently volatile. If a 20 percent decline in a single year would make you uncomfortable, FYX is not appropriate. If you have a multi-year horizon, can tolerate large drawdowns, and believe that financial strength matters even in small companies, the fund is worth deeper study.
