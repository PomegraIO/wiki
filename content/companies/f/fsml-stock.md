*The **Franklin Small Cap Enhanced ETF** (FSML) is an exchange-traded fund that builds on the [Russell 2000 index](/russell-2000/)—the benchmark for U.S. small-cap stocks—but layers an active overlay designed to tilt the portfolio toward companies showing value and quality characteristics. Unlike a pure [index fund](/index-fund/) that simply holds all Russell 2000 constituents equally weighted or market-weighted, FSML gives portfolio managers latitude to re-weight holdings and filter for specific traits believed to boost returns over long [holding periods](/holding-period/).*

## The small-cap landscape

Small-cap stocks—those with [market capitalizations](/market-capitalization/) roughly between $300 million and $2 billion—occupy an unusual niche in equity markets. They are large enough to file with the [Securities and Exchange Commission](/securities-and-exchange-commission/) and trade on major exchanges, but small enough that Wall Street coverage is sparse and information asymmetry between insiders and public investors remains substantial. The Russell 2000 index captures roughly two thousand of these names and is the standard benchmark for the category.

Small caps are often portrayed as a distinct asset class, and in some ways they are. Historically, they have offered returns that exceed large-cap stocks over long periods, though with higher volatility and wider [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/). This return premium is sometimes called the "small-cap effect" and has been documented across decades, though its magnitude fluctuates with market regimes. During periods of economic confidence and rising leverage, small caps outperform; in [recessions](/recession/) or flights to safety, they lag.

## Why Franklin enhanced Russell 2000 exposure

Rather than offer a straight Russell 2000 index fund, [Franklin Templeton](/fgdl-stock/) designed FSML as an actively managed overlay on small-cap territory. The philosophy is that while small-cap stocks in aggregate may offer a return premium, selective tilting toward those with stronger [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/), lower leverage, and cheaper valuations can improve that premium further.

The fund implements this by weighting its portfolio differently from the Russell 2000. A pure index fund would hold each of two thousand names in proportion to its market capitalization; FSML's managers adjust these weights based on proprietary models. Companies that score high on value metrics—low price-to-earnings multiples, high [dividend yields](/dividend-yield/), strong [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) relative to price—receive higher allocations. Similarly, firms displaying quality characteristics—lower debt, higher profit margins, more consistent earnings—get tilted higher. Conversely, expensive or highly leveraged names get reduced or eliminated.

This active tilting comes with a cost: the fund's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is higher than a passive Russell 2000 tracker would be, reflecting the labor of portfolio managers and analysts. That cost must be overcome by alpha—the excess returns the active management generates. In [bull markets](/bull-market/) for small-cap value, that bar is easier to clear; in years when growth and momentum dominate small-cap returns, it is harder.

## The value-versus-growth tension in small caps

Small-cap markets have been swept in recent years by the same growth-versus-value dynamics that have affected the broader equity market. For much of the 2010s and into 2020, technology-driven small caps with high growth expectations but low or zero earnings commanded lofty multiples. Traditional industrial, financial, and energy small caps languished at deep discounts. FSML's value tilt meant it was structurally underweight the outperformers during that era, a headwind on relative performance.

From 2022 onward, the pendulum swung: rate increases and recession fears hammered growth stocks and favored value. Small-cap value surged. FSML was positioned to benefit from this regime. The fund's track record depends substantially on which regime we are in—a simple mechanical insight, but one that frames the risk. Value tilts shine when the market recognizes cheap assets; they languish when the market ignores them.

## Costs and liquidity

FSML trades like any ETF on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) during regular market hours, so investors can buy and sell shares intra-day at market prices without redemption windows or trading delays. The expense ratio is the annual drag on returns and represents the cost of active management plus the ETF infrastructure; it is deducted automatically from fund returns.

The underlying securities—small-cap stocks—are generally less liquid than large caps. Bid-ask spreads are wider, and a large order to buy or sell can move prices. However, FSML aggregates demand for two thousand different small caps into a single ETF share, which smooths individual stock liquidity challenges and makes the fund itself reasonably liquid.

## Real risks and structural headwinds

Small-cap stocks are inherently more volatile than large-cap counterparts. Earnings can swings sharply, debt defaults happen more often, and business models are more fragile. A company with $500 million in market cap and $50 million in revenue is far more exposed to a single lost customer, a failed product launch, or a supply-chain disruption than a megacap with diversified operations.

FSML amplifies this by concentrating holdings in names the manager believes are undervalued and solid—a [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) that cuts both ways. In the fund's favor, conviction positions can drive outperformance in good environments. Against it, concentrated bets in a volatile asset class can amplify drawdowns.

There is also the structural risk that active management underperforms. Over many years, especially in efficient large-cap markets, passive indexing has beaten most active managers after fees. Small caps are more mispriced and less heavily covered by analysts, which theoretically offers active managers more opportunity. But there is no guarantee FSML will capture that opportunity consistently. In years when the manager's filters and models are misaligned with what the market rewards, the fund will lag its benchmark while charging higher fees.

## How a reader would research FSML

Start with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, available on Franklin Templeton's website, which detail the current holdings, the index being tracked, and the expense ratio. Compare FSML's performance against the Russell 2000 index over rolling periods of one, three, five, and ten years. The net excess returns (after fees) reveal whether the active management has added value. Review the fund's turnover—how frequently the portfolio turns over—which can signal tax drag in taxable accounts.

Track the fund's style exposures. Because FSML tilts toward value and quality, it will naturally have higher exposures to energy, financials, and industrials and lower exposure to information technology and consumer discretionary sectors than the broad Russell 2000. Understanding these tilts helps explain the fund's performance relative to the benchmark in different market environments.

Finally, monitor the small-cap market backdrop. Watch whether small-cap value is expensive or cheap relative to small-cap growth using metrics like the Russell 2000 Value versus Russell 2000 Growth relative multiples. When value is deeply beaten down, FSML's tilt offers opportunity; when value has already surged, upside may be limited. The broader economic outlook also matters: small caps thrive when [credit spreads](/credit-spread/) are tight and corporate defaults are low; they struggle in recessions.
