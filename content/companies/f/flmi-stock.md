The Franklin Dynamic [Municipal Bond](/municipal-bond/) ETF is an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) that invests in municipal securities—the [tax-exempt bonds](/tax-exempt-bond/) issued by U.S. states, cities, and other municipalities to finance public works. Unlike most exchange-traded products, FLMI is not benchmarked to a fixed index. Instead, it employs a dynamic strategy in which the fund's managers assess economic conditions, interest-rate outlook, and relative value across the municipal [bond](/bond/) market and adjust holdings accordingly. For investors in higher tax brackets, municipal bonds offer a distinctive advantage: the interest income is free from federal income tax and, if the issuer is in the investor's state of residence, from state and local tax as well. That tax-exempt status is what gives municipal bonds their allure and what constrains their yields relative to comparable taxable bonds.

## The municipals market and its peculiarity

The municipal bond market is one of the largest and least well-known credit markets in the United States. It is fragmented—each city, county, school district, and special authority issues its own securities—which makes it less efficient than the Treasury market and less liquid than the [corporate bond](/corporate-bond/) market. That inefficiency creates opportunities for skilled managers. Active funds like FLMI can hunt through thousands of individual municipal issuers for the ones offering the best risk-adjusted return at a given moment, playing both credit quality and interest-rate movements simultaneously.

Municipal bonds themselves come in two main flavors. [General obligation bonds](/general-obligation-bond/) are backed by the full taxing power of the issuer. [Revenue bonds](/revenue-bond/) pledge a specific revenue stream—tolls from a bridge, fees from a water system—and depend on the health of that particular enterprise. Revenue bonds can offer higher yields to compensate for the extra [credit risk](/credit-risk/), but they also require more homework to understand whether the underlying revenue is stable or fragile.

## Strategy and positioning

FLMI's approach is to construct a portfolio that responds to the current landscape rather than mechanically tracking a static benchmark. When [interest rates](/interest-rate/) are expected to fall, the fund might extend duration—buying longer-dated bonds to capture capital gains as prices rise. When rates are rising, it might shorten duration or rotate toward shorter maturities. The fund can also shift between general obligation bonds and revenue bonds, or adjust its exposure to different geographies and sectors within the muni universe based on the manager's outlook on state and local fiscal health.

This active approach carries a cost. [Expense ratios](/expense-ratio/) for managed municipal [bond ETFs](/bond-etf/) are typically in the range of 0.4–0.6% annually—meaningfully higher than the cost of passively tracking a muni index, which might run 0.2% or less. The fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) and market price can diverge, especially for municipal bonds, which trade less frequently than stocks, creating opportunities for the informed buyer but also [execution risk](/execution-risk/) for those trading without care.

## The tax-advantage trap

The principal allure of municipal bonds is the tax break. For an investor in the 37% federal tax bracket, a 4% tax-exempt yield is economically equivalent to a 6.35% taxable yield—a substantial edge. Yet that same tax advantage makes municipal bonds a mistake for tax-sheltered accounts. An individual retirement account (IRA) or a 401(k) already shields investment returns from tax, so buying a tax-exempt bond inside one sacrifices the tax exemption without benefit. The ideal owner of a municipal bond ETF is a high-earner in a high federal (and state) tax bracket with significant taxable wealth to deploy.

## Liquidity and volatility

The [secondary market](/secondary-market/) for municipal bonds is thinner than many equity investors expect, which matters for an ETF that must price fairly and allow trading at reasonable spreads. Most trading in municipal bonds happens between institutions, and retail access has traditionally been limited. An ETF structure makes munis more accessible by allowing daily trading at the fund's net asset value, but liquidity can still be strained during market dislocations. The fund's holdings are also sensitive to interest-rate moves. When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) tightens, bond prices fall—a muni ETF will too. Conversely, falling rates generate capital gains. Over a full [market cycle](/market-cycle/), that interest-rate sensitivity is the primary driver of returns.

## Risks and structural points

The largest risk in any [municipal bond fund](/municipal-bond-fund/) is credit deterioration. If an issuer's finances collapse and it defaults, bondholders take losses. FLMI's active management is intended to anticipate and avoid such situations, but it cannot eliminate credit risk entirely. The fund must also contend with [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/)—the longer the duration of the portfolio, the greater the loss if rates rise. Municipal defaults are historically rare, but they do happen, and FLMI's individual holdings in thousands of issuers mean the fund carries some idiosyncratic issuer risk.

A second risk is [liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/) during periods of market stress. If redemptions spike, the fund must sell bonds, potentially at unfavorable prices if the market is dislocated. The active-fund structure also introduces the risk that the manager's directional bets—duration calls, [sector rotations](/sector-rotation/)—may be wrong.

## How to research FLMI

Begin with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, available on Franklin's website, which detail the exact strategy, fees, and holdings. The fund reports a distribution yield, which indicates the current income the holdings are generating. Watch the expense ratio closely and compare it to passive alternatives to ensure you are comfortable paying for the active management. For a municipal bond fund, understanding the tax situation is crucial—calculate what that tax-free yield means in after-tax dollars given your marginal tax rate. Monitor the fund's duration, sector allocations, and credit-quality distribution, all of which shift with market conditions and the manager's outlook. Finally, understand that a muni ETF is not a stable-value instrument; it is a bond fund that moves with interest rates, and over short periods it can deliver meaningful losses or gains.
