The Franklin FTSE Mexico ETF is a straightforward index tracker—it holds a basket of the largest publicly traded Mexican companies, weighted by their [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/), and aims to replicate the returns of the FTSE Mexico Index before fees. For a U.S. investor seeking exposure to Mexico's equity market, FLMX offers direct access without the need to trade directly on Mexico's exchange, through a daily-tradeable fund in [U.S. dollars](/us-dollar/) (though the underlying holdings are priced in [Mexican pesos](/mexian-peso/)).

## What FLMX holds

The fund's largest holdings are typically the same companies that dominate Mexico's [stock market](/stock-market/): América Móvil (the dominant wireless carrier), Grupo Femsa (a large beverage and retail conglomerate), [Banco Santander](/san-stock/) México, and Grupo México (mining and rail). The top ten holdings typically account for a high proportion of the fund's assets—often 50% or more—which is a reflection of Mexico's equity market structure. The Mexican [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) is not as broad or deep as the U.S. market; it is concentrated in a handful of very large, often family-controlled businesses that operate across multiple economic sectors.

This concentration is both a feature and a liability. It ensures that FLMX's holdings are liquid and easy to trade, but it also means that the fund's performance is heavily dependent on the fortunes of a few names. If Grupo Femsa stumbles, it ripples through the entire fund. Unlike a diversified U.S. equity fund that owns thousands of companies, FLMX's fate is bound to the destinies of perhaps 30 or 40 major Mexican firms.

## Currency and macro exposure

An investor in FLMX is taking on two kinds of risk: the stock-price risk of the companies themselves, and the exchange-rate risk of the Mexican peso. When the peso strengthens against the U.S. dollar, a U.S. investor who owns the fund gains not only from any stock-price appreciation but also from the currency move. When the peso weakens, that currency headwind cuts into returns even if the underlying companies do fine. Over long periods, currency movements can be as important to total returns as stock selection.

The fund is unhedged, meaning it does not use [currency futures](/currency-futures/) or forwards to neutralize peso exposure. A U.S. investor in FLMX is explicitly betting that the peso will hold its value or strengthen relative to the dollar. For some investors, that currency exposure is desirable—they want to diversify into Mexican assets and accept the peso as part of the package. For others, it is an unwanted complication, and they may look for a hedged variant (if available) or limit FLMX to a small sleeve of their portfolio.

## The Mexican economy and market dynamics

Mexico's equity market is driven by the performance of its largest multinationals, many of which earn a significant portion of their revenue outside Mexico itself. The economy is tightly integrated with the United States through trade, investment, and manufacturing supply chains. A U.S. [recession](/recession/) or slowdown ripples quickly into Mexico. Conversely, strong U.S. growth can lift Mexican exports and corporate profits.

Domestic [political risk](/political-risk/), [inflation](/inflation/), and the strength of the [central bank's](/central-bank/) [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) are also relevant for Mexican equity investors. The Mexican peso tends to weaken when inflation rises or when U.S. [interest rates](/interest-rate/) climb faster than Mexican rates, both of which reduce the appeal of holding peso assets. Commodity price movements—particularly oil, which Mexico both exports and refines—also matter, especially for companies like Petróleos Mexicanos and Grupo México.

## Sector composition and balance

The largest sectors in FLMX are typically industrials (manufacturing, telecom, and mining), consumer discretionary (retail and beverages), and financials (banks and diversified companies). This mix reflects Mexico's role as a manufacturing and commodity economy and the dominance of family-owned conglomerates that span multiple sectors. There is limited exposure to technology relative to a U.S. index, and telecommunications is a larger slice of the market.

## Costs and liquidity

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) for FLMX is typically in the low-cost range—generally below 0.5%—reflecting its passive indexing approach. The fund's trading volume is generally moderate; it is not the most liquid ETF, but it is liquid enough for most retail investors. The [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) (the difference between the price at which you can sell and the price at which you can buy) is often a few cents wide, which translates to a fraction of a percent cost for transactions.

## The concentration risk

The overriding risk in FLMX is concentration. Holding a handful of mega-cap stocks means the fund is not truly diversified in the way a U.S. equity fund is. A severe decline in one of the top five holdings can meaningfully hurt performance. Investors must be comfortable with that risk profile to own the fund.

## How to research FLMX

Start with the fund's fact sheet from [Franklin Templeton](/fgdl-stock/), which lists the exact top holdings, sectors, and geographic breakdown. Compare FTSE Mexico Index performance to the fund's returns to understand tracking error. Monitor the fund's year-to-date and trailing returns, but remember that past performance is not predictive. Watch the Mexican peso's strength relative to the dollar—this currency component is as important as stock performance for a U.S.-based investor. Follow headlines on major Mexican companies and macroeconomic data (inflation, growth, central bank policy) to understand headwinds or tailwinds. Finally, consider how FLMX fits into your broader portfolio: it is a concentrated emerging-markets bet, not a substitute for broad [diversification](/diversification/), and should be sized accordingly.
