*Sprott Focus Trust is a closed-end mutual fund that pools investor capital to purchase and hold a concentrated portfolio of [common stocks](/common-stock/), selected through active management rather than index replication. The fund offers investors a way to gain equity exposure through a professionally managed vehicle rather than buying individual shares directly.*

## What the fund holds

The fund invests primarily in equity securities — that is, shares of common stock — selected by Sprott's investment team. Unlike an open-end mutual fund, which continuously buys and sells shares from investors on demand and adjusts its asset base accordingly, a [closed-end fund](/closed-end-fund/) issues a fixed number of shares at inception. Those shares then trade on an exchange at whatever price buyers and sellers agree to, much like any other security. The price on the exchange may trade at a premium (above) or a discount (below) the underlying value of the fund's holdings, creating opportunities and risks that exchange-traded funds and open-end [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) do not present.

Sprott Focus Trust's holdings typically span multiple sectors and geographies, though the specific allocation and concentration vary according to the fund manager's judgment and market conditions. The "focus" in the fund's name reflects its approach: a concentrated portfolio of a smaller number of securities rather than a broadly diversified or index-tracking approach. This concentration means that the fund's returns are more heavily influenced by the performance of a few large holdings, making it more volatile than a diversified index fund but offering the potential for outperformance if those securities are chosen well.

## How the fund generates returns

The fund earns returns for shareholders through two primary channels. First, the securities held in the portfolio appreciate in value as investors' appetite for those companies increases or as the companies themselves grow more profitable. Shareholders participate in that capital appreciation. Second, many equity holdings pay [dividends](/dividend/), and the fund collects those dividends and passes them on to shareholders either as regular distributions or reinvests them back into the fund. The mix depends on whether the shareholder has elected dividend reinvestment or prefers to receive cash.

The fund itself charges a [management fee](/management-fee/) to cover the cost of Sprott's investment team, research, and administration, as well as other operating expenses. These fees are expressed as an annual [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) — a percentage of assets under management — and vary based on the fund's size and Sprott's pricing. The fund is self-liquidating, meaning it must eventually wind down and return capital to shareholders.

## The case for active equity funds

The closed-end structure and the active-management approach reflect a specific bet: that a skilled team, given a concentrated mandate and the ability to move quickly without handling constant inflows and outflows of investor capital, can select stocks that outperform the overall market. This bet is contested. Decades of empirical research show that most [actively managed funds](/actively-managed-fund/) do not beat their corresponding index benchmarks after fees, especially over long periods. The concentration here — holding fewer securities — amplifies both potential outperformance and the risk of underperformance if the selected holdings decline.

The closed-end structure itself offers one advantage that [open-end funds](/open-end-fund/) and [index funds](/index-fund/) do not: stable asset capital. The manager does not face redemption pressure (shareholders who want to exit sell shares to other investors on the exchange rather than asking the fund to liquidate holdings). That stability can allow for a longer time horizon and less frequent trading. However, it also means the fund's discount or premium to [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) can create additional volatility that an index fund investor does not face.

## How to research the fund as an investment

Any investor considering Sprott Focus Trust should begin with the fund's latest prospectus and [annual report](/10-k/), both available through the SEC (CIK 0000825202) and Sprott's website. The prospectus lays out the fund's strategy, permitted investments, risks, and fee structure. The annual report shows the current portfolio holdings, their weights, and the fund's performance over trailing periods. Pay close attention to the expense ratio and the trading discount or premium; a fund trading at a deep discount may offer value if the holdings are solid, or it may signal that investors lack confidence in management's stock-picking ability. Watch the turnover rate, which shows how frequently the manager trades — high turnover can erode returns through transaction costs, while very low turnover might suggest the manager is too passive or overweighting a few core positions. Compare the fund's one-year, three-year, and five-year returns against a broad equity index to assess whether the active management has justified its fees.
