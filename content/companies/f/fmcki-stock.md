Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation, trading under the ticker FMCKI for its 6.55% Non-Cumulative Perpetual Preferred Stock, is a government-sponsored enterprise that sits at the centre of the American housing finance system. FMCKI represents a specific class of preferred shares in Freddie Mac, issued during the company's extensive reorganisation after emerging from financial conservatorship. These preferred shares rank ahead of common equity but below debt in Freddie Mac's capital structure, making them a hybrid instrument that blends characteristics of bonds and stock.

## What exactly is Freddie Mac, and what role does it play?

Freddie Mac is a government-sponsored enterprise chartered by Congress in 1970 to expand the secondary mortgage market. It does not originate mortgages; instead, it purchases loans from banks, credit unions, and other lenders, bundles them into mortgage-backed securities, and sells those securities to institutional investors with an implicit federal guarantee attached. This mechanism replenishes lenders' capital so they can continue making new mortgages. By operating the secondary market for mortgages, Freddie Mac and its peer Fannie Mae collectively channel capital to roughly 70 percent of American mortgage originations, making them indispensable infrastructure in housing finance.

## Why does Freddie Mac issue preferred stock, and what makes FMCKI different from common shares?

When Freddie Mac emerged from federal conservatorship in 2019—a restructuring that wiped out its previous preferred classes—the company needed to rebuild its capital base. The new preferred stock classes, including FMCKI, serve as a way for the enterprise to raise equity capital while maintaining a clear priority structure. FMCKI is non-cumulative, meaning if Freddie Mac cuts or suspends its dividend (which it is allowed to do under terms of its conservatorship agreement), holders do not accumulate the missed payments as a claim to recover later. In contrast to common shareholders, preferred holders receive fixed dividend payments and have some protection against dilution, though their upside is capped at the stated rate. The tradeoff is illiquidity: FMCKI trades over-the-counter in very light volume, not on a major exchange.

## What is the central risk to holding FMCKI?

The principal risk is regulatory and structural rather than operational. Freddie Mac remains in conservatorship—a form of government control—under the Federal Housing Finance Agency. The company's earnings are subject to a "profit cap" that limits its retained earnings, meaning most gains flow to the Treasury rather than being retained for creditors or shareholders. Should Congress or regulators decide to wind down Freddie Mac, restructure its role, or materially alter the terms of conservatorship, the preferred shares could see sharp losses. Preferred shareholders have no contractual right to demand redemption; they are perpetual unless the company chooses to call them. If interest rates fall significantly and Freddie Mac's mortgage portfolio becomes more valuable, the company could theoretically redeem the shares at par, capping upside. Conversely, if regulators tighten capital requirements, preferred shares might absorb losses before the company fails any capital test.

A secondary risk is the interest rate environment. Freddie Mac's profitability depends in part on the spread between what it pays to borrow and what it earns on mortgages. A sharp inversion—such as a sustained period when short-term rates remain well above long-term rates—can compress Freddie Mac's net interest margin. This affects common earnings first, but severe compression could ultimately threaten the preferred dividend, especially if conservatorship restrictions prevent the company from accumulating capital.

## How does Freddie Mac generate revenue and sustain operations?

Freddie Mac earns revenue from several sources. The primary source is the guarantee fee it charges to investors in mortgage-backed securities—a small percentage of the principal that investors pay for the explicit guarantee of timely payment of principal and interest, even if borrowers default. Freddie Mac also earns net interest income from mortgages it holds directly, and it collects servicing and other ancillary fees. Operating expenses are substantial: the company maintains loan-servicing operations, deal-making staff, technology infrastructure, and credit analysis teams. Credit losses on non-agency mortgages and non-performing loans also reduce earnings. Because most mortgages are guaranteed by the government (through Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or Ginnie Mae), the actual default risk is minimal; Freddie Mac's role is to absorb timing mismatches and operational risks, not catastrophic credit events.

## What should investors watch to assess risk?

The most important metric to track is whether Freddie Mac remains in conservatorship and on what terms. Any announcement of a change in FHFA policy, the capital release agreement, or the profit cap is material. Second, monitor housing market conditions and mortgage origination volume. When originations fall sharply (as they do when mortgage rates spike), Freddie Mac's guarantee fees and transaction volumes contract, squeezing short-term earnings. Third, watch the interest rate environment closely, particularly the shape of the yield curve. A steep inversion sustained for years erodes net interest margin. Fourth, observe Congressional and regulatory commentary about the long-term future of the GSEs. The company's preferred stock trades on the assumption that Freddie Mac will continue to operate under its current structure; any credible signal of fundamental restructuring could trigger sharp price moves. Finally, review Freddie Mac's quarterly earnings releases and investor presentations for commentary on delinquency rates, prepayment speeds, and capital adequacy—these are the operational metrics that reveal whether the business is under stress.

## Why would an investor hold a security with capped upside and perpetual structure?

FMCKI trades at a yield that reflects both the safety of the government-sponsored enterprise and the constraints of preferred equity. For income-focused investors seeking higher yields than Treasury bonds, the perpetual structure and the implicit backing of the federal government make it attractive relative to lower-yielding fixed-income alternatives. The non-cumulative dividend also appeals to investors who believe conservatorship will eventually end and the company's capital structure will normalise. However, this is a market-dependent, event-driven holding rather than a pure cash-flow business investment. The principal driver of returns is the price movement—whether regulators signal an exit from conservatorship, whether housing policy shifts, and whether the market reprices the risk.
