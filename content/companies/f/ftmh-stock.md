*The **Franklin Municipal High Yield ETF** (FTMH) is an exchange-traded fund that tracks a diversified portfolio of [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/) below investment grade — typically in the BB to B rating range — offering higher yields than [bonds](/bond/) from creditworthy municipalities, with the tax advantages of municipal bond ownership.*

## What this fund holds and where it sits

Franklin Municipal High Yield ETF entered the market as part of Franklin Templeton's municipals family during the growth of exchange-traded products in the 2000s. The fund targets the subset of municipal bond issuers whose credit quality is weaker than the A- and above ratings that dominate most municipal [index funds](/index-fund/). Cities, states, and special districts with stretched finances — those paying debt service on aging infrastructure, facing declining tax bases, or recovering from economic shocks — issue bonds at lower [credit ratings](/credit-rating/). Those bonds trade at higher yields to compensate investors for the added risk of default. FTMH captures that yield premium by holding a broad basket of such securities, letting individual investors access a riskier slice of the municipal market without researching individual credits.

The fund typically holds bonds rated between BB and B — that is, [speculative grade](/speculative-grade/), well below the A-rating sweet spot that many municipal funds target. This positioning makes FTMH distinct from its investment-grade sibling funds in the Franklin municipal lineup. The fund is permissive on geography and bond type: it may hold [general obligation bonds](/general-obligation-bond/) (backed by the full faith and credit of an issuer), [revenue bonds](/revenue-bond/) (repaid from a specific revenue stream like bridge tolls or water fees), and [special assessment bonds](/special-assessment-bond/). The geographic [diversification](/diversification/) spreads [credit risk](/credit-risk/); no single state, city, or sector dominates.

## Yield, tax treatment, and who this fund is for

Municipal bonds, including those held in FTMH, produce interest income that is exempt from U.S. federal income tax — and often exempt from state and local income tax as well, depending on residency and the issuer's domicile. Because of that tax-advantaged treatment, municipal bond yields are lower than comparable [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/). A BB-rated municipal bond might yield 4–5%, whereas a similarly rated corporate bond yields 7–8%. For investors in high federal and state tax brackets, the after-tax return from that lower nominal yield can exceed the after-tax return of a higher-yielding corporate bond.

FTMH is designed for taxable accounts, not tax-deferred accounts like IRAs, where the tax advantage is meaningless. Its primary audience is a high-income investor with a large federal and state tax liability seeking tax-exempt income. The higher yield of the below-investment-grade slice makes the fund attractive to those hunting for current income rather than principal appreciation alone. The trade-off is clear: higher yield comes with higher default risk.

## The core risks

The defining risk of FTMH is credit risk — the possibility that an issuer will be unable or unwilling to pay interest or principal on schedule. BB and B rated bonds default far more often than A and AA rated bonds, especially during economic downturns. A single issuer's financial distress or a [recession](/recession/) can concentrate losses. Though the fund holds many bonds to reduce the impact of any one default, a significant default in a major holding can still impair returns in a way it would not for an investment-grade fund.

A second risk is duration risk — the sensitivity of bond prices to interest-rate movements. When [interest rates](/interest-rate/) rise, existing bond prices fall, particularly for longer-duration bonds. If an investor needs to sell before maturity, a rise in rates means a loss. FTMH typically holds intermediate-duration bonds, so it is not immune to that risk, though it is less exposed than longer-duration funds.

[Liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/) is quieter but real. Muni bonds trade over the counter in a less transparent market than stocks or investment-grade corporates. Spreads can widen sharply in a market stress or a sudden [flight to quality](/flight-to-quality/). The ETF structure itself provides daily liquidity — an investor can sell at the closing price — but the underlying bonds may be harder to sell quickly without a price concession if the fund needs to liquidate a large position.

## How municipal bonds found their way into an ETF

For most of the 20th century, municipal bonds were primarily the domain of individual investors buying and holding to maturity, and of buy-and-hold bond funds that charged high fees. The rise of ETFs lowered the cost of entry and made it easier for an investor to hold a diversified basket of munis without doing individual credit research. [Franklin Templeton](/fgdl-stock/), long a major player in municipal fund management, built out a family of municipal ETFs tracking different segments of the market — investment-grade, high-yield, short-term, and state-specific.

FTMH's niche was deliberate: the fund offered a lower-cost way to capture the higher yields of below-investment-grade munis while benefiting from the diversification that an ETF provides. As with all municipal ETFs, the tax-exempt character of the underlying bonds is preserved for the ETF holder.

## How to research and track this fund

The fund's prospectus and fact sheet appear on the Franklin Templeton website and should be the first stop for any potential investor. The prospectus details the fund's strategy, its benchmark (if any), the risks, and the fees. The fact sheet gives a snapshot of the [current yield](/current-yield/), duration, and composition.

To understand the credit quality of the fund's holdings, review the credit-quality distribution — the percentage in each rating category — and the distribution of yields. Compare the fund's yield against plain-vanilla investment-grade municipal ETFs to confirm that the extra yield exists and is material. Watch for changes in the fund's average credit quality over time; a steady decline could signal heightened credit risk.

During periods of economic uncertainty, FTMH may experience larger price swings than investment-grade funds. Tracking the fund's price and yield over a full [market cycle](/market-cycle/), including a recessionary period if available, gives the best sense of how it behaves in stress. For a long-term holder, the periodic 10-Q and [annual reports](/10-k/) filed with the SEC detail portfolio composition and performance in depth.
