Horizon Flexible [Income ETF](/income-etf/) offers a straightforward idea: combine stocks that pay [dividends](/dividend/) with [bonds](/bond/) that pay coupons, and let the manager tilt the mix as conditions change. FLXN is the vehicle for investors seeking a monthly or quarterly income stream without the complexity of managing separate stock and bond holdings.

The fund's equity sleeve typically draws from U.S. large-cap stocks across utilities, consumer staples, financials, and [real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/) — sectors and companies with a history of steady dividend payments. These are not high-growth enterprises; they are mature, cash-generative businesses that prioritize returning money to shareholders. Real estate investment trusts (REITs) often feature prominently, since REITs are required by law to distribute most of their taxable income as dividends, making them natural income vehicles.

The fixed-income portion is anchored in investment-grade corporate and government bonds, typically with maturities spanning two to ten years. This intermediate duration provides some yield without the extreme interest-rate sensitivity of longer bonds. The bonds anchor the portfolio's stability — when stocks stumble, bonds often provide a cushion.

What distinguishes FLXN is the flexibility. Rather than committing to a fixed 60-40 or 50-50 mix, Horizon's management team can adjust the equity-to-bond ratio based on their assessment of valuation and risk. In environments where stocks appear attractively priced, they may shift toward equities. When equity valuations seem stretched or economic risks rise, they tilt toward bonds. This tactical flexibility is meant to smooth returns and optimize the income stream, but it introduces manager-selection risk: good timing adds value, poor timing subtracts it.

For income investors, the monthly distribution is the main draw. Dividends from the stock holdings and [coupon payments](/coupon-payment/) from bonds are pooled and paid out regularly, so the holder receives a steady flow of cash without having to sell shares. This is tax-efficient compared to selling a portion of the fund each month, though distributions themselves are taxable in a non-retirement account. The actual dollar amount varies with market conditions: when yields are elevated, distributions are higher, and when yields compress (typically in a falling-rate environment), they decline.

The fund's risk profile sits between an all-stock fund and an all-bond fund. Equity exposure means downside in [bear markets](/bear-market/), though the bond cushion softens the blow. [Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) on the bond side means the fund's value falls if the Fed raises rates sharply. But neither risk is as severe as a 100-percent-equity portfolio would face. This makes FLXN appropriate for retirees or near-retirees who need income now, can tolerate some volatility, and are comfortable holding through a [market cycle](/market-cycle/).

Invesco operates a similar fund (FLXI), and there are other blended-income funds in the market, so comparison is straightforward. Look at Horizon's fact sheet to see the current allocation, top holdings, sector exposure, and credit-quality breakdown of the bond portion. The prospectus explains the fund's mandate, fee structure, and the limits on how much the manager can shift between stocks and bonds. Review performance over a three-year and five-year period against a 60-40 stock-bond benchmark to assess whether the flexibility has been deployed well. Check the distribution history to understand the trend in payouts — rising, flat, or declining — which signals the fund's yield environment and income prospects going forward.
