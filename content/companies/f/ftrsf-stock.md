> A customer must buy electricity. A regulator will allow the utility to charge whatever rate is necessary to recover costs and earn an approved return. The rest is cash flow and dividend mathematics.

Fortis Inc. operates at the intersection of these three forces. The company owns and manages ten regulated utility subsidiaries across Canada, the United States, and the Caribbean. Each subsidiary holds a geographic monopoly in its service area — no other company can legally distribute electricity or [natural gas](/natural-gas/) to homes and businesses within its territory. In exchange for that monopoly, the subsidiary accepts rate [regulation: a](/regulation-a/) regulator sets the rates customers pay and the return the company is permitted to earn on its invested capital. This structure has governed North American utilities for more than a century, and Fortis operates entirely within it.

## The stability of essential demand

The electricity and natural gas that Fortis delivers are not discretionary. A customer might defer buying a new car or cutting back on restaurant meals during a [recession](/recession/), but they cannot defer paying their electric bill. This creates a uniquely stable revenue stream for the company. Fortis's customers will pay for power and gas in good years and bad. This essential-service characteristic is why utilities attract investors seeking income and capital preservation rather than capital appreciation.

Fortis's three million customers are spread across sixteen regulatory jurisdictions, which provides natural [diversification](/diversification/). A severe recession in Arizona does not cascade across the Caribbean or British Columbia operations. A regulatory decision in New York affects only Central Hudson's earnings. This portfolio approach buffers the company from regional or sector-specific shocks and makes earnings more predictable across the [business cycle](/business-cycle/).

## The regulator as partner and constraint

The customer is certain to demand the product. The regulator is certain to scrutinize the company's costs and allow recovery. What is not certain is the rate of return the regulator will permit. Some regulators approve 8 percent; others approve 10 percent or more. This two-percent difference in allowed return sounds small but translates to significant earnings swings across a multi-billion-dollar asset base. When a regulator increases the allowed return or accelerates capital cost recovery, Fortis's earnings expand. When a regulator cuts the return or denies a rate increase, earnings contract.

Management must therefore work as carefully on regulatory relationships as on operational efficiency. The company's track record of winning cooperative regulatory treatment in multiple jurisdictions — reflected in fifty consecutive years of [dividend](/dividend/) increases — shows that Fortis has built trust with policymakers. That trust is earned and can be lost. A history of mismanagement, poor service, or hostile relationships with regulators would eventually show up in lower approved returns and weaker earnings.

## Capital deployment: reinvestment and return

Fortis generates substantial operating cash because utilities have relatively low variable costs once the infrastructure is built. The company's chief expenses are [depreciation](/depreciation/) of aging assets, employee compensation, and ongoing maintenance and compliance. The difference between revenue and these operating costs flows to the bottom line. The company then deploys that cash into two channels: capital reinvestment and shareholder returns.

Capital reinvestment funds the replacement of aging pipes and wires, the expansion of service to new areas, and the modernization of equipment — tasks necessary to maintain the utility franchise and position the company for growth. The company has outlined a multibillion-dollar capital plan for the coming years. Funding this plan requires growing debt alongside operating cash flow. As long as the company's debt levels remain within conservative bounds and the regulator allows the company to recover that investment in rates, the capital spending supports future earnings growth and justifies future dividend increases.

The dividend is the mechanism by which Fortis returns excess cash to shareholders. The company has raised the annual dividend every year for five decades, signaling management's confidence in future earnings and the regulator's willingness to support that confidence. For a company in a mature, low-growth industry, this consistency is remarkable and explains why Fortis is held by pension funds and individual retirees seeking predictable income.

## Risks in a changing energy landscape

Natural gas distribution exposes Fortis to long-term transition risk. A sustained shift away from fossil fuels toward electric heating and transportation would reduce gas demand and pressure those utility segments. However, natural gas remains essential for many industrial customers and for power generation, and regulatory policy in Canada and the US has not yet pushed gas utilities toward rapid decline. The company's electricity utilities are better positioned, as electrification is likely to increase power demand.

Regulatory risk is the other material threat. A regulator can deny a rate increase, reduce the allowed return, or impose new costly requirements. A major adverse regulatory decision would ripple through the company's cash flow and dividend. Catastrophic weather events — hurricanes in the Caribbean, ice storms in Canada — can damage infrastructure and drive one-time charges, though these are typically infrequent.

## How investors research Fortis

The starting point is the annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001666175), which details operations by subsidiary and jurisdiction, breaks down revenue by utility and geography, and discusses regulatory and [operational risks](/operational-risk/). Quarterly results reveal trends in customer growth, rates, and [operating margins](/operating-margin/). Earnings calls provide management color on regulatory filings, rate case outcomes, and capital spending.

Key metrics: the dividend history, which is the most transparent measure of whether the company's earnings are genuinely growing or merely treading water; the [debt-to-capital ratio](/debt-to-capital-ratio/), which indicates financial leverage and [refinancing risk](/refinancing-risk/); and the [weighted average cost of capital](/weighted-average-cost-of-capital/), which signals the regulatory environment's willingness to support the company's growth. Regulatory developments in major jurisdictions — particularly rate-case outcomes in New York, Arizona, and British Columbia — directly affect future earnings. Customer growth trends show whether the company's service territories are expanding or stagnating. Finally, any changes to allowed rates of return or regulatory terms in key jurisdictions are leading indicators of earnings trajectories for years to come.
