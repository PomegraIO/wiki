*The **First Trust Small Cap BuyWrite [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** (FTKI) is a hybrid instrument: it holds small-cap US stocks but simultaneously sells [call options](/call-option/) against them each month, converting those holdings into an income machine at the cost of capping potential gains. Investors receive higher yield than small-cap equities alone deliver, but they forfeit the chance to participate fully in dramatic rallies.*

## The mechanics

FTKI holds stocks from the [Russell 2000 Index](/russell-2000/) — roughly 2,000 small-cap and micro-cap US companies. At the same time, the fund's managers sell one-month call options on those same stocks, pocketing the premium. That premium flows to shareholders as an extra boost to income on top of the [dividends](/dividend/) the underlying stocks pay. The options expire monthly, and the process repeats.

The trade-off is built-in: when a stock rallies sharply, the call option caps the fund's upside. If FTKI holds a small-cap stock that jumps 20% in a month, but the sold call is struck at a 10% higher price, the gain is capped at that 10% strike. The fund sells the stock away and rebalances. That sold premium feels good in sideways or down markets — it cushions the decline and pays steady income. In roaring [bull markets](/bull-market/), it feels like a handbrake.

## The small-cap disadvantage

Small-cap stocks are inherently more volatile than large caps. They are riskier, less liquid, and more sensitive to economic downturns. Their upside in bull markets can be spectacular. A buywrite strategy layered on top of small-cap exposure is particularly restrictive because small-cap investors are often betting on explosive growth — exactly the scenario where a capped upside hurts most.

The fund's yield is attractive — substantially higher than small-cap or broad-market [equity ETFs](/equity-etf/) — but that yield is neither magic nor free. It is extracted from the opportunity cost of forgone gains. An investor who buys FTKI is implicitly betting that consistent income and capital stability matter more than the occasional home-run gain that small-cap stocks can deliver.

## The volatility floor and risk

The covered-call strategy does provide a measure of downside cushioning: the [option premiums](/option-premium/) collected serve as a partial buffer in down months. That is real but limited — a 30% crash in small-cap stocks will still hurt, even with call premiums helping to soften the blow.

The deeper risk is opportunity cost. If small-cap stocks enter a sustained bull run — driven by economic acceleration or a shift in investor risk appetite — FTKI will lag meaningfully. An investor holding the fund during such a period will watch broad small-cap indices zoom higher while FTKI's upside stays capped. Over long periods, that drag accumulates.

The fund is also exposed to all the normal risks of small-cap investing: lower liquidity, higher volatility, less disclosure, greater susceptibility to economic slowdowns, and [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) if a few large positions dominate the portfolio.

## Who owns this, and how to evaluate it

FTKI appeals to income-focused investors who own small-cap exposure but want to reduce volatility and extract yield from it. It is common in retirement accounts where steady income matters more than capital appreciation. It is less suitable for growth-focused investors or those in accumulation phases of their investing life.

To evaluate the fund, compare its yields against plain [small-cap ETFs](/small-cap-etf/): the extra income should be meaningful enough to justify the capped-upside constraint. Look at how often the fund's call options go in-the-money (that is, end up capping the fund) — high frequency means you are sacrificing gains often. Examine the fund's performance in strongly rallying markets: does the underperformance align with the option structure as expected? Check the prospectus for the option-writing strategy details and any discretion the manager has in strike selection. Finally, stress-test: in a scenario where small-cap stocks surge, would the trade-off feel acceptable, or would you regret the constraint?
