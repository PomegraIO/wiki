Freddie Mac is one of two major institutions (along with Fannie Mae) that operate the machinery of the American residential mortgage system. It does not lend directly to homeowners; instead, it buys mortgages from banks and other originators, pools them into securities backed by those mortgages, and sells those securities to investors around the world. This role as middleman between the originating lender and the final investor is what defines the company's business, and it is why Freddie Mac matters so much to the availability and affordability of home loans across the United States.

## Core business: buying mortgages and selling securities

Freddie Mac operates in a defined niche. Primary lenders — banks, credit unions, mortgage brokers — originate loans to homeowners. Freddie Mac then purchases those mortgages in bulk, which frees up the originator's capital so it can make more loans. Freddie Mac then pools hundreds or thousands of mortgages together and issues securities backed by the cash flows those mortgages generate. These mortgage-backed securities are sold to institutional investors, pension funds, insurance companies, and foreign governments. The investor bears the credit risk (the risk that a homeowner stops paying); Freddie Mac earns a guarantee fee on each security it issues, plus servicing income and other fees.

This structure does two things: it provides originators with a steady buyer for their loans and a source of capital, and it distributes the risk of mortgage defaults across a wide pool of investors instead of concentrating it on the lender. Without a functioning secondary mortgage market, the originating lender would have to hold every mortgage to maturity and tie up enormous capital. The whole system would shrink.

## Geographic shape and business volatility

Freddie Mac's mortgage purchases span all 50 states, but the composition and health of its portfolio shifts with regional housing markets, interest rates, and economic conditions. A housing downturn in a single region — or nationally — can raise delinquency rates across the portfolio, increase the pace of defaults on the guarantees Freddie Mac has issued, and trigger losses. Conversely, in a healthy market with rising home values and stable employment, delinquency rates fall and Freddie Mac benefits from the fees it earns on a large, performing portfolio.

The company is not insulated from its geographic exposure. In 2008 and the years that followed, when housing prices collapsed and mortgage delinquencies soared, Freddie Mac suffered enormous losses as homeowners walked away from underwater mortgages. The company had to be taken into government conservatorship, an arrangement that remains in effect. That history underscores how much a company in the secondary mortgage market is buffeted by the health of the underlying asset — the American home and the borrowers who live in it.

## Revenue streams and profitability drivers

Freddie Mac's income comes primarily from guarantee fees (a small percentage of mortgage balances), servicing revenues, and investment income on its mortgage portfolio. During periods of rising interest rates, the company can also benefit from price appreciation on the mortgages it holds. However, the company's costs are heavily weighted to its credit losses — the mortgages it guarantees that ultimately default. During normal housing markets these losses are manageable; during downturns they become the dominant fact of the profit statement.

The company also operates under a statutory dividend cap on common equity that limits how much profit can be retained or returned to shareholders, which constrains its accumulation of capital. As a government-sponsored enterprise, Freddie Mac operates under a regulatory charter that defines its mission, its permissible activities, and the limits on the mortgages it can purchase.

## Scale and market position

Freddie Mac has historically held a roughly 50–50 market share with Fannie Mae across the secondary mortgage market. The combined portfolio of both enterprises typically encompasses between 40 and 50 percent of the mortgages in America, making them systemic to the housing system. The remainder is held by banks, sold into private-label mortgage-backed securities pools, or kept by community lenders.

This concentrated position in a critical market means Freddie Mac and its counterpart are carefully regulated and politically sensitive. Their financial health is monitored continuously by the Federal Housing Finance Agency. Changes to their business model, their capital requirements, or the mortgages they are permitted to purchase ripple through the entire housing market.

## Key considerations for investors and researchers

Anyone researching Freddie Mac as an investment faces the reality that it is a government-sponsored enterprise operating under conservatorship. Its future shape — whether it will remain in government hands, be privatized, be recapitalized differently, or be restructured entirely — is a policy question as much as a financial one. The company's 10-K filing details its exposure to interest-rate risk, credit risk, and refinancing risk. Investors should monitor the composition of its mortgage portfolio, the trajectory of delinquency and default rates, and any regulatory changes to its mission or capital constraints. The company's earnings are highly dependent on the mortgage rate environment and the health of housing markets, which makes it a cyclical play rather than a defensive one.
