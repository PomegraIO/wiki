FTSL is a [closed-end fund](/closed-end-fund/) holding senior secured bank loans to corporations. These are not [bonds](/bond/) or debt securities traded on public markets; they are actual lending agreements originated by banks, syndicated among institutional investors, and held to maturity or until paid off. FTSL purchases [tranches](/tranche/) of these loans, holds them, and distributes the monthly interest ([coupon) payments](/coupon-payment/) to shareholders, along with any principal that is paid ahead of schedule or at maturity.

The two defining features are seniority and floating-rate mechanics. Seniority means FTSL's loans sit at the very top of a borrower's capital structure. If a company stumbles, the senior loan holders get paid first from asset sales before a single dollar goes to bondholders or equity holders. This prioritisation dramatically reduces credit losses in default scenarios — senior lenders typically recover sixty to eighty cents on the dollar; subordinated lenders often recover nothing. Floating-rate mechanics mean the coupon resets periodically (typically every quarter) based on a reference rate like [SOFR](/sofr/) plus a [credit spread](/credit-spread/). When the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) raises benchmark rates, SOFR rises, and FTSL's coupon payments rise proportionally. When rates fall, coupons fall. This automatic adjustment is the inverse of traditional fixed-rate bonds, where coupon is locked in. In a rising-rate environment, FTSL benefits from higher income just as bond prices would be falling — a useful hedge profile for certain market regimes.

FTSL's structure as a closed-end fund matters significantly. Unlike open-end [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) or ETFs, where new shares are created and redeemed continuously at [net asset value](/net-asset-value/), closed-end funds issue a fixed number of shares. Investors buy and sell existing shares on an exchange. As a consequence, the fund's share price can diverge from its net asset value — the true value of its underlying holdings. It might trade at a ten percent discount to NAV if sentiment sours, or a five percent premium if the fund is in favour. That discount or premium is a separate market phenomenon from underlying loan performance and represents an additional source of return or loss for shareholders.

The portfolio typically consists of fifty to one hundred loans spread across corporate borrowers. The borrowers are usually leveraged companies funded by private equity sponsors or large corporations using bank loans alongside bond issuance. A typical borrower might be a healthcare services company backed by a PE firm, a specialty industrial manufacturer, a consumer products company, or a business services firm. The loans are usually medium-term, with remaining maturities of three to seven years at origination. FTSL's manager evaluates each loan on credit quality — how likely is the borrower to repay on time — and collateral value (what assets back the loan and their realisation value). The fund typically holds loans in the middle of the credit spectrum: not investment-grade bank credits, but not distressed troubled debt either. The prospectus details concentration, industry breakdowns, and credit distribution.

Leverage amplifies returns and risks. Closed-end funds often borrow money at a fixed or floating rate to fund additional loan purchases beyond what equity shareholders have invested. If FTSL's equity base is one hundred million and it borrows fifty million at a low rate to purchase more loans, the returns to equity holders rise — the loan spread is typically higher than the borrowing cost. But if credit conditions deteriorate and the loan portfolio falls in value, that decline is magnified in the equity holder's loss because the borrowing must still be repaid. The prospectus and monthly reports disclose the exact [leverage ratio](/leverage-ratio-forex/) and composition (fixed versus floating borrowing).

Interest-rate sensitivity is complicated. The floating-rate loans benefit from rising rates through higher coupons. But if FTSL has used fixed-rate borrowing to lever, rising rates do not increase the cost of that leverage — so the economic benefit is clear. If it has used floating-rate leverage, rising rates increase both coupon income and financing costs, with the spread potentially compressed. Additionally, rising rates sometimes portend economic slowdown, which harms borrower credit profiles. FTSL is not a pure interest-rate bet; it is a floating-income vehicle with [credit risk](/credit-risk/) as the dominant driver.

Credit and default risk are the core concerns. In a strong economy with low unemployment and stable to rising corporate profitability, defaults are rare and FTSL collects robust income. In a [recession](/recession/), defaults spike, recoveries are lower (even senior lenders may recover only fifty cents), and distributions shrink. The fund distributes its actual monthly coupon income; if losses mount due to defaults, the distribution can be cut. Shareholders should not expect stable distributions through all credit cycles; they will vary with economic conditions.

Who owns FTSL? Income-focused investors seeking yield higher than [investment-grade bonds](/investment-grade-bond/) but comfortable with credit risk. Those with a view that credit spreads offer [fair value](/fair-value/) and that economic conditions will not deteriorate sharply. Investors with long time horizons who can tolerate periodic distribution cuts and share-price volatility. Not appropriate for conservative income seekers, capital-preservation mandates, or those expecting imminent recession. The fund's [annual report](/10-k/) and monthly fact sheets disclose the loan portfolio, credit metrics, and leverage. Track the trailing twelve-month [default rate](/default-rate/) and recovery rates in the leveraged-loan market to anticipate whether conditions are favouring loan performance or threatening it.
