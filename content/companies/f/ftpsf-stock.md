## What exactly does Fortis do?

Fortis owns and operates the utility infrastructure—the poles, wires, and pipes—that deliver electricity and [natural gas](/natural-gas/) to homes and businesses across Canada and the United States. It doesn't compete in an open market; instead, it holds exclusive licenses to serve specific geographic regions as a regulated monopoly. Customers cannot switch to a rival utility; Fortis is the only option. In exchange for this monopoly, the company cannot charge whatever it wants—regulators set rates using a formula designed to allow the utility a fair but not excessive return on its investment.

## How did Fortis grow from a small Newfoundland utility to this size?

Fortis started in 1987 as a holding company controlling Newfoundland Light & Power, a regional utility serving Newfoundland and Labrador. Over the following decades, the company pursued [acquisition](/acquisition/) strategy, buying utilities in Atlantic Canada, then expanding westward across the provinces. It also entered the United States, acquiring Fortis Alberta, MidAmerican Energy (operating in multiple U.S. states), and several other smaller utilities. Each acquisition added a new regulated service territory, reducing the company's reliance on any single regulator or geography. This [diversification](/diversification/) meant that if one province tightened its regulatory stance, the impact on Fortis overall would be modest because the company earned profits from utilities in many other places.

## What is the basic financial model?

The company generates revenue by charging customers for electricity and gas delivered through its distribution networks. Customers pay a per-unit rate (cents per kilowatt-hour or per cubic meter of gas) plus a fixed monthly connection fee. The company's costs are partly variable—fuel cost if it operates generation plants, contract payments to suppliers—but are mostly fixed. Maintaining poles and wires, paying administrative staff, and servicing debt happen regardless of how much power is delivered. This cost structure means profit scales with volume: add more customers or raise consumption, and most of the additional revenue flows to profit.

The company raises capital by issuing debt ([bonds](/bond/)) and equity to fund the massive infrastructure investments required to maintain and expand its networks. Regulators typically allow it to recover the cost of this capital through rates. If Fortis invests a billion dollars in new infrastructure, it earns a regulator-approved return (say 8.5 percent) on that investment, which gets paid for by customers through higher utility bills over many years. This predictable cost recovery is why utilities can sustain high debt levels that would be dangerous for other businesses.

## What are the main business segments?

Fortis operates electric and gas utilities, with electric generation and some competitive energy trading as secondary operations. The electric business dominates in terms of the installed base (most properties use electricity), but natural gas is often more profitable per unit of consumption because gas utilities typically operate at tighter margins than electric in booming markets but maintain better margins in slumps. The company also owns some generation capacity—power plants that produce electricity for the grid. Some of this generation is regulated (paid a fixed return for being available) and some competes in wholesale markets (paid market prices for power sold). Competitive generation is volatile: prices spike when demand is high and supply is tight, producing great profits, but plummet when supply is abundant, producing losses.

The geographic mix matters. MidAmerican Energy, the largest U.S. subsidiary, operates in multiple states with different regulatory regimes. FortisBC operates in British Columbia, where energy regulation has become more stringent in recent years. Fortis Alberta benefits from a large installed base in a growing province. Each territory has its own regulatory environment and growth rates, so spreading across many reduces [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/).

## What pressures is the company facing cyclically?

Like most utilities, Fortis benefits in economic booms: industrial customers increase production and use more electricity, consumption rises, and the company can sometimes raise rates. In [recessions](/recession/), industrial demand falls sharply, but residential demand holds up because people still need lights and heat. This provides some recession-resistance. However, the company's debt service is fixed regardless of economic conditions, so a deep recession that cuts consumption by 5–10 percent can meaningfully reduce profit.

Longer-term, the biggest pressure is the structural shift away from fossil fuels. Natural gas demand is declining as customers install heat pumps instead of gas furnaces, and regulators are increasingly reluctant to approve investments in gas infrastructure that may be obsolete in a decade. Fortis generates substantial current cash from its gas business, but that business is in secular decline. The electric business is more stable, but electricity consumption growth has slowed in mature markets.

Energy transition creates a deeper problem: Fortis is being pushed to invest heavily in renewables and grid modernization, which require capital, but regulators are simultaneously becoming more stingy about allowing cost recovery. This squeeze—high capital requirements, tighter regulatory approval—threatens the [dividend](/dividend/) growth that has been the main attraction of utility stocks.

## How resilient is Fortis to boom-and-bust cycles?

Utilities are traditionally considered defensive investments—they weather recessions better than most companies because electricity and gas are essential services. Fortis has multiple leverage points that work in its favor. First, residential customers use electricity and gas regardless of economic conditions; booms and busts don't eliminate these needs. Second, the regulated model protects profitability: even if consumption falls, regulators typically allow rate increases to maintain the utility's return on investment. Third, diversification across many jurisdictions means a local recession in one state doesn't devastate the company.

The downsides are real, though. Industrial customers are discretionary users; a severe recession that cuts factory production also cuts electricity demand sharply. If recession is paired with regulatory hostility—a regulator refusing rate increases—the company's profitability can compress. The company's high debt load means that in a stress scenario with falling consumption, rising [interest rates](/interest-rate/), and restrictive regulators, Fortis could be forced to cut its dividend to preserve cash. This happened during the 2008 financial crisis and is possible again if conditions align.

## How should an investor approach Fortis stock?

Start by reviewing the 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001666175). Examine which segments are growing and which are shrinking. Is the company shifting capital toward renewables? Is it maintaining gas distribution capacity or unwinding it? Look at capital spending and debt levels—is the leverage manageable if consumption declines?

Watch regulatory decisions carefully. Rate decisions in British Columbia, Ontario, and key U.S. states affect profitability. If a regulator cuts the allowed [return on equity](/return-on-equity/) or denies a rate increase, it signals tighter oversight ahead. Fortis's strategy depends on regulators allowing it to recover the cost of capital investments; if that stops, the dividend becomes vulnerable.

Check the dividend and whether the company is raising it. Traditionally, utilities grow dividends 5–6 percent annually because regulated returns compound. If that growth slows or stops, it signals concerns about future profitability. Also monitor whether Fortis is issuing equity to fund investments (diluting shareholders) or using debt (increasing financial risk). In transition periods, utilities often resort to equity issuance, which pressures stock prices.

Finally, assess the company's position on the energy transition. Is management aggressively investing in renewables? Is it divesting gas assets or defending them? The answer reveals whether the company is adapting to a decarbonizing world or banking on regulatory protection for a legacy business. That strategic choice will determine whether Fortis's dividend remains sustainable over the next decade.
