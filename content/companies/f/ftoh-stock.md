*The **Franklin Ohio Municipal [Income ETF](/income-etf/)** — FTOH on NYSE — holds investment-grade [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/) issued by Ohio cities, counties, utilities, and public authorities. Like its peer Franklin municipal funds, FTOH delivers interest income that is exempt from federal income tax and, for Ohio residents, exempt from Ohio's income tax as well. It is one of several state-specific muni ETFs managed by [Franklin Templeton](/fgdl-stock/), a leader in municipal [bond](/bond/) management.*

## The thesis: tax-shield for Ohio taxpayers

Ohio's top state income tax rate sits around 3.9%, below the national peak but non-trivial for high earners. For a top-bracket federal taxpayer in Ohio, that 3.9% state rate compounds the federal exemption. A bond yielding 4% is worth roughly 5.3% after all state and federal taxes avoided — a meaningful boost to after-tax return. That advantage is why state-specific muni funds exist: they bundle bonds from one state so that in-state buyers receive full double exemption while out-of-state buyers at least retain federal exemption.

Ohio's municipal bond market is substantial. The state has large, creditworthy issuers — Columbus, Cincinnati, Cleveland, and the Cuyahoga County transit system are well-known names — alongside smaller towns, school districts, and utilities. FTOH's mandate is typically to hold the top tier of Ohio credits, filtering for single-A or better, so the portfolio skews toward large, stable borrowers. That conservative stance lowers [credit risk](/credit-risk/) but also dampens yield relative to a fund that dipped into BBB or A- territory.

## Portfolio shape and duration

FTOH is built for intermediate- to long-term holding. Most positions mature in 15 to 30 years, creating significant interest-rate sensitivity. In a rising-rate environment, the fund's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) will decline — a 3% jump in yields could mean a 15% to 25% drawdown in share price, depending on the exact maturity profile. That risk is inherent to the space; you are being paid through tax exemption and a higher coupon, and the price swings are the flip side of that arrangement.

The portfolio excludes bonds rated below single-A (or equivalent), which rules out some of the highest-yielding Ohio munis but eliminates the credit disasters that occasionally blow up muni funds. Ohio school districts, for example, sometimes face funding crises; FTOH would not own the distressed bonds of such a district unless and until it climbed back to single-A.

Concentration in the portfolio is modest. While large issuers like Columbus, Cincinnati, and regional utilities may appear prominently, the fund typically spreads holdings across 50+ different bond issues, so no single issuer dominates. That diversity is a selling point and a practical necessity — Ohio's bond market is large but not infinite, and over-concentrating in a few names would push the fund into illiquid paper.

## The costs and the tax trade-off

FTOH's announced [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is attractive — under 0.5% annually in most cases. But the true cost is captured in the [bid-ask spread](/bid-ask-spread/) when you buy or sell shares on NYSE, and in the opportunity cost of owning long-duration bonds when rates are rising. The tax exemption is automatic and embedded in the coupon; you do not pay a separate fee for it, though you give up the upside of taxable bonds if tax rates fall sharply.

For Ohio residents in high tax brackets, the math works. For non-residents, the fund is less compelling because they only get the federal exemption; a national muni fund or a taxable bond fund might offer better yield on a pre-tax basis. For Ohio residents in low tax brackets (10% or lower effective rate), the exemption is less valuable, and a diversified taxable bond fund might offer a better risk-adjusted return.

## Credit and interest-rate risk

Ohio is not California or Illinois — it does not have a history of severe structural budget crises — but it is not a backwater either. The state's main pressures are demographic aging (declining school-age population in some regions), the decline of industrial manufacturing, and the long-tail cost of public-sector pensions. Most of FTOH's holdings are backed by large, diversified municipalities and utilities that have weathered previous downturns, so the credit risk is moderate for the space. The fund's historical [default rate](/default-rate/) is very low.

[Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) dominates. If the Fed's overnight rate rises 2% over two years, FTOH's share price will likely fall by 20% or more, all else equal, because the fund holds long-duration bonds. This is not unique to FTOH; it is baked into any bond fund with significant maturity. The hedge is that if you hold FTOH to maturity (which you cannot directly, but you can hold shares long-term and let positions mature into cash), you recover [par value](/par-value/) regardless of the interim price moves.

## How to research the fund

Start with Franklin Templeton's prospectus and fund fact sheet for FTOH, which detail the credit quality requirements, maturity targets, and fee structure. The prospectus will also disclose which Ohio issuers are held and in what proportion.

Track Ohio's state fiscal health via the State Auditor's office and legislative budget reports. Unusual fiscal stress at the state level (like a revenue shortfall or a spike in [pension obligations](/pension-obligation/)) can eventually ripple down to local issuers that depend on state aid.

For the broader muni picture, follow the Bloomberg Muni Bond Index and the ICE Ohio Muni Index if it exists (or equivalent regional trackers). When Ohio munis widen relative to other states, it might signal that Ohio-specific credit concerns are emerging. Conversely, when they tighten, it could mean relative value is fading.

Finally, check the fund's trading premium or discount to NAV. If FTOH persistently trades at a premium to its NAV, it suggests that Ohio resident demand is outpacing supply — they are willing to overpay for the state-tax exemption. A persistent discount might indicate that the market is skeptical of Ohio credit or that the exemption advantage is not enough to offset the fund's fees and the underlying bonds' maturity risk.

---

FTOH serves a clear purpose: it bundles Ohio munis into an easy-to-trade vehicle for residents who benefit from the state tax break. For that constituency, it is a sensible core holding in a fixed-income allocation. For non-Ohio residents or those in low tax brackets, other strategies — national muni funds, taxable bond funds, or direct Treasury ownership — may be more efficient.
