## Starting with the 10-K, Not the Name

The company name includes "AI," a descriptor that has become heavily freighted with investor speculation and marketing claims. Begin your analysis by setting the name aside and reading Item 1 (Business) of the latest 10-K filing. This section describes what Fabric.AI actually does, what customers it serves, and how it generates revenue. Many software companies with AI in their name are still predominantly selling traditional software licenses or services; others have meaningful machine-learning components. The 10-K will clarify the distinction. Pay attention to how management describes its core offering: is it a subscription model, a license model, professional services, or a mixture? The answer shapes everything that follows in your analysis.

## Revenue Recognition and Contract Structures

Once you understand the basic business, the next critical entry point is the revenue-recognition policy in the accounting notes (usually Note 1 or the revenue note in Item 8). Technology companies recognize revenue in varied ways: upfront for perpetual licenses, over time for SaaS subscriptions, or as services are delivered. Fabric.AI's specific policy tells you when cash actually arrives relative to when revenue is recorded and whether the company's growth is driven by new customer acquisition or expansion within existing accounts. Look specifically for subscription-contract terms: what is the typical contract length, what are renewal rates, and how much revenue is deferred (unearned) at the balance-sheet date? Deferred revenue that is growing faster than reported revenue suggests strong future earnings power; deferred revenue that is flat or shrinking suggests customer churn or slowing deal flow.

## Customer Concentration and Dependency

In Item 1A (Risk Factors), Fabric.AI will likely disclose whether it has significant customer concentration. Many enterprise software companies rely on a handful of large customers for a disproportionate share of revenue. The 10-K should disclose the percentage of revenue from the largest customer and the top five customers. If a single customer represents more than 15% of revenue, that dependency is material: loss of that contract could force a substantial earnings restatement. Cross-check the customer-concentration disclosure with the accounts-receivable aging in the balance-sheet notes. Are receivables concentrated among the same large accounts? Are days sales outstanding (DSO) reasonable for the business model, or are days stretching (suggesting collection challenges)?

## Sales and Marketing Efficiency

For a technology company, the ratio of sales and marketing expense to revenue is a proxy for customer-acquisition cost and go-to-market efficiency. Compare Fabric.AI's S&M spending to gross margin. A company with a 75% gross margin can afford higher S&M spend than one with 40%. Look at the trend: is S&M spending growing or shrinking as a percentage of revenue? Growing S&M as a percent of sales can signal either accelerating growth investments or slowing sales productivity. Research-desk analysts often calculate the Magic Number (net new ACV / S&M spend) for SaaS companies; while this requires customer concentration and ACV data that the 10-K may not fully disclose, the filing will give enough detail to estimate the rough efficiency of sales investments.

## Product Development and Engineering Cost

The 10-K will report research and development (R&D) or product-development expense as a separate line or lumped into operating expense. For a software company, R&D is capital-like: it builds competitive advantage and durable products. Understand what Fabric.AI is building. If R&D is high relative to revenue (say, 30% or more), the company may be in heavy investment mode or may be burning cash to maintain a deteriorating competitive position. The risk factors section should clarify the strategic intent: is the company investing in the next generation of its product, or is it struggling to compete? Talk of product launches, new features, or platform expansions suggests the former; language about market pressures or competitive threats suggests the latter.

## Profitability and Path to Cash Flow

Most early-stage or growth-stage software companies are not profitable. The 10-K will report operating losses and often negative cash flow from operations. For Fabric.AI, determine how the company is burning cash and whether management has articulated a path to profitability. Check the cash-flow statement (Item 8): is the company burning cash, is it cash-flow positive, or is it somewhere in between? A company can report operating losses while generating positive cash flow (via deferred revenue or changes in payables) or be cash-flow negative despite reported profitability. The operating cash flow trend is more reliable than reported earnings for a software company. If operating cash flow is negative and deteriorating, understand why: is the company investing in growth (and does that investment show traction in new revenue), or is it burning cash on a stalled business?

## Balance Sheet and Liquidity

Review the balance sheet for cash position, debt obligations, and any off-balance-sheet commitments. Software companies often have minimal debt but should have substantial cash. If Fabric.AI is burning cash, how long is the runway? A company with $5 million in quarterly operating losses and $20 million in cash has about one year of runway; beyond that, it must either reach profitability, raise new capital, or face a crisis. Check whether the company has any debt covenants, convertible notes, or preferred-stock agreements that might restrict its ability to raise capital or might dilute common shareholders. These details often live in the balance-sheet notes and in Item 5 (Liquidity and Capital Resources).

## Forward-Looking Statements and Guidance

The MD&A section (Item 7) often includes forward-looking statements about expected growth, new products, or market opportunities. Read these critically. Software companies frequently make bullish claims that don't materialize; the analyst's role is to identify which claims are backed by disclosed trends (e.g., billings growth, customer wins) and which are aspirational. If management claims new products will drive growth, check the backlog or pipeline disclosures and look for customer wins already announced.
