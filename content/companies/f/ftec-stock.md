*The **Fidelity MSCI Information Technology Index ETF (FTEC)** is a passive, index-tracking fund that owns the largest U.S. technology and software companies — a low-cost way to gain exposure to the sector that has driven U.S. market returns for the past two decades.*

## The index and what it holds

FTEC tracks the MSCI USA Information Technology Index, a stock list compiled by [Morgan Stanley](/morgan-stanley/) Capital International to represent U.S. companies in the technology sector. MSCI defines that sector to include software companies, semiconductor designers and manufacturers, computer hardware makers, IT consulting firms, data-centre operators, semiconductor-equipment companies, and internet-related businesses. The index includes roughly one hundred to one hundred fifty of the largest such companies by [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/).

The names are familiar to any investor: [Microsoft](/msft-stock/), [Apple](/aapl-stock/), [Nvidia](/nvda-stock/), [Intel](/intc-stock/), [Broadcom](/avgo-stock/), Qualcomm, Meta, Google ([Alphabet](/googl-stock/)), Amazon, [Tesla](/tsla-stock/), [ServiceNow](/now-stock/), [Salesforce](/crm-stock/), [Adobe](/adbe-stock/), and dozens of mid-size firms. All of these companies appear in the index, weighted by their market capitalisation, meaning Apple and Microsoft — the two largest — account for a hefty slice of the fund, while smaller members contribute a smaller weight.

## Passive indexing and why FTEC is low-cost

FTEC is a passive fund, meaning Fidelity simply tracks the index — buying and holding the same stocks in the same weights, rebalancing as needed when the index changes. There is no portfolio manager trying to pick winners or time the market. The fund's holdings are transparent and published daily; they mirror the index exactly, minus a tiny tracking error.

Because there is no active research or frequent trading involved, the costs are minimal. FTEC's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically below 0.10 per cent annually, meaning an investor with USD 10,000 in the fund pays roughly USD 10 per year in fees. That compares to active technology funds that might charge 0.50 to 1.00 per cent or more. Over decades, the fee savings compound into meaningful extra returns.

## The technology sector and its role in U.S. equities

The technology sector has been the dominant profit engine of the U.S. [stock market](/stock-market/) for the past twenty-five years. The rise of the personal computer, the internet, cloud computing, and artificial intelligence has created an enormous moat for U.S. companies, and the largest tech firms have become the largest companies on Earth by market value.

FTEC provides sector exposure — a way to own a slice of technology without owning individual company stock. But it is important to understand what that means. A [sector ETF](/sector-etf/) is far more concentrated than a broad market index like the S&P 500. The S&P 500 includes all industries — financials, healthcare, energy, consumer goods, industrials — and technology is roughly a quarter to a third of the index. FTEC is one hundred per cent technology, meaning all of the fund's performance is driven by that single sector. When technology stocks surge, FTEC outperforms the broad market. When technology stumbles, FTEC underperforms.

## Concentration and the megacap bias

FTEC is weighted by market capitalization, which means the very largest companies — Microsoft, Apple, Nvidia, Alphabet — make up a large slice of the portfolio. The ten largest holdings often account for forty to fifty per cent of the fund's value. This concentration reflects the enormous scale and profitability of those firms, but it also means that FTEC's performance is heavily dependent on how a handful of mega-cap stocks perform.

In the 2010s and early 2020s, when mega-cap technology stocks were the dominant leaders, this concentration worked in FTEC's favour and the fund outperformed the broad market. If market leadership rotates to mid-size or smaller-cap tech companies, or if mega-cap growth slows relative to other sectors, the concentration can work against FTEC.

The fund does include smaller-cap technology companies, but they represent a small slice of the total, so the fund is less diversified than it might appear.

## Interest rates and valuation risk

Technology companies tend to be among the most interest-rate-sensitive stocks in the market. When [interest rates](/interest-rate/) are low, investors place a high value on the future earnings of tech companies, since those future profits are worth more in present-value terms. When rates rise, the opposite happens: future profits are discounted more heavily, and tech valuations compress. This dynamic means FTEC tends to outperform in low-rate environments and underperform when rates are rising or expected to rise.

The valuations of many technology stocks also embed optimistic growth assumptions. Companies trade at high multiples of current earnings based on expectations of rapid future growth. If those expectations disappoint, or if the broader market loses appetite for growth stocks and favours value or dividend-paying stocks instead, FTEC can struggle.

## Competition and cyclical risks

The technology sector is highly competitive, and it is subject to disruption. A shift in computing architecture — the rise of AI chips, the adoption of quantum computing — could render some technology companies less competitive or obsolete. Regulatory scrutiny of large tech firms, particularly around antitrust concerns, data privacy, and content moderation, poses an ongoing risk to profitability.

Geopolitical tension, especially around semiconductors and U.S.-China relations, creates supply-chain and competitive risks for chip designers and manufacturers that FTEC holds. A deterioration in U.S.-China relations or a restriction on chip exports would directly affect companies like Nvidia, Qualcomm, and Intel.

## How to research and use FTEC

FTEC is appropriate for investors who believe the technology sector will outperform other sectors over the long term and are willing to accept the [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) and interest-rate sensitivity that come with it. It is a simple, low-cost way to gain broad exposure to U.S. technology without owning individual company stock.

Review the fund's top ten holdings and sector breakdown on Fidelity's website or through any [broker](/broker/) that carries the fund. Compare FTEC's performance to the broad S&P 500 and to other technology-focused funds to understand how much sector-specific risk you are taking on. Be aware that FTEC will significantly outperform the broad market in periods when technology is leading and significantly underperform in periods when technology is lagging — that is the nature of a concentrated sector bet. Finally, consider whether your overall portfolio already has exposure to mega-cap technology through broad [index funds](/index-fund/); if so, adding FTEC adds meaningful overlap and concentration risk.
