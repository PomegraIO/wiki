*The Schwab Fundamental International [Equity ETF](/equity-etf/) (FNDF) is a passively managed exchange-traded fund that holds equities from developed markets outside the United States — including Europe, Japan, Canada, Australia, and other advanced economies — weighted according to fundamental metrics of profitability, cash generation, and balance-sheet strength rather than [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/).*

## Geographic and market-cap composition

FNDF provides exposure to the developed world outside the United States through a single, diversified portfolio. The fund holds approximately 1,500–2,000 stocks, which means it includes large-cap anchors (such as the largest European banks, Japanese automotive manufacturers, and London-listed multinational mining companies), but also substantial allocations to mid-cap and small-cap companies that would otherwise receive little attention in a market-cap-weighted index. The largest country weights typically include the United Kingdom, Canada, Japan, Germany, France, Australia, and Switzerland, though the actual allocations shift based on the fundamental-index methodology and market movements.

By holding companies across all market capitalizations, FNDF captures the small-cap premium that exists in developed markets outside the U.S. — the historical tendency for smaller stocks to deliver higher returns over long periods, though with higher volatility. This breadth also means the fund is less dependent on the fortunes of any single mega-cap stock or country. In contrast, a market-cap-weighted international index is often heavily concentrated in a handful of the largest European or Japanese names.

## Fundamental weighting and sector implications

The fundamental-indexing methodology weights each holding by its underlying profitability and cash generation, not by its current stock-market value. This creates structural tilts in the fund's sector and style positioning. FNDF is permanently overweight to financials (banks, insurers, and asset managers), materials (mining and chemicals), energy, and industrials — all sectors heavy in developed-market economies and all represented by profitable, dividend-paying companies. The fund is lighter on consumer discretionary and significantly lighter on technology than a market-cap-weighted index would be.

These sector tilts are not speculative bets on management's view of economic cycles; they emerge mechanically from the fundamental-weighting rule. During periods when value and cyclical sectors outperform, FNDF leads. During periods when growth and technology dominate (as they have in some recent years), FNDF lags the market. Investors should understand this structural positioning before committing capital.

## Currency exposure and hedging

FNDF is unhedged to foreign currencies, meaning a U.S. investor faces the performance of both the underlying stocks and the movement of dozens of foreign currencies — the pound, euro, yen, [Canadian dollar](/canadian-dollar/), [Australian dollar](/australian-dollar/), and others. When developed-market currencies strengthen against the dollar, this magnifies returns. When they weaken, it dampens returns. Over decades, currency movements have averaged out, but in any given year or [market cycle](/market-cycle/), currency can be the dominant driver of international fund returns.

Some investors prefer unhedged exposure because they believe currency [diversification](/diversification/) provides a real hedge against dollar weakness and [inflation](/inflation/). Others find it adds unnecessary complexity and volatility and prefer hedged international funds that eliminate the currency bet. FNDF offers no currency-hedged alternative within Schwab's suite, so investors who want that protection must look elsewhere.

## Costs and rebalancing

FNDF's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically around 0.18–0.22% annually, among the lowest for international equity [index funds](/index-fund/). The modest cost reflects Schwab's scale and the passive nature of the fundamental-indexing strategy. For a $100,000 position, this translates to roughly $180–220 per year in fees. The fund rebalances once annually, usually in September, when weightings are reset to align with the updated fundamental scores. Annual turnover is typically 10–15%, higher than a static market-cap index but lower than an actively managed international fund.

## Liquidity and execution

FNDF trades on the [Nasdaq](/nasdaq/) with solid daily volumes, providing efficient entry and exit for most investors. However, the underlying stocks trade on exchanges across Europe, Asia, and the Pacific, often in relatively [thin markets](/thin-market/), particularly for smaller holdings. Large fund inflows or outflows can create execution costs and trading friction that are higher than for a purely domestic fund. The asynchronous trading hours between North American and other global markets also mean that prices incorporate news and events from overseas sessions that are already hours old by the time the U.S. market opens.

## Diversification and inter-market dynamics

FNDF's breadth — 1,500–2,000 stocks across dozens of countries and sectors — provides genuine diversification from U.S. markets. Periods of U.S. outperformance and periods of international outperformance alternate; no developed-market region wins consistently. Holding FNDF as part of a broader portfolio that includes U.S. equities reduces [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/) and ensures some exposure to international [business cycles](/business-cycle/), commodity cycles, and geopolitical events that affect developed markets differently than the U.S.

The correlation between U.S. and international developed-market returns is high during severe market shocks (nearly all equities sell off together), but in normal times the correlations are moderate, meaning international exposure provides meaningful diversification benefits.

## Suitability and research considerations

FNDF is suitable for investors with a global outlook who view developed-market diversity as a core portfolio component and who believe that value and profitability metrics offer a durable edge. The fund is best held as a core international allocation within a broader diversified portfolio, not as a timing or trading position. It is particularly suitable for buy-and-hold investors comfortable with multi-year underperformance periods if U.S. or growth-focused markets perform better.

Before investing, review the prospectus and fact sheet from Schwab to understand the current country and sector weightings, examine historical performance across different market cycles (particularly periods of dollar strength and weakness), and assess the tracking error — how closely the fund tracks its underlying index. Compare FNDF against both market-cap-weighted international funds and other fundamental-weighted international offerings to understand the performance difference. Finally, determine what size international allocation makes sense within your overall portfolio based on your goals, risk tolerance, and time horizon.
