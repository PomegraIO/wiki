*The **First Trust Dorsey Wright Dynamic Focus 5 ETF** (FVC) is built on a core insight: the best-performing stocks often share detectable characteristics, and a disciplined process can identify them before the crowd does.*

The "Dynamic" element of FVC sets it apart from simpler concentration strategies. Unlike a static five-stock portfolio held unchanged for years, FVC rebalances regularly, using Dorsey Wright's proprietary relative-strength and fundamental screens to identify which companies merit inclusion at any given time. Holdings change as the market evolves and as companies' positions shift relative to their peers.

## How the Dynamic Dorsey Wright process works

The Dorsey Wright methodology combines technical relative-strength measures (how a stock is performing compared to its peers and the broader market) with fundamental criteria such as earnings trends, balance-sheet health, and valuation. The output is a ranked list of candidates, from which the fund's managers select up to five for inclusion in the portfolio.

The emphasis on relative strength — how a stock is performing in the context of the market and its sector — is the distinctive angle. Stocks that are outperforming their peers tend to continue outperforming in the near term, a pattern that decades of research has documented across asset classes and geographies. FVC seeks to own those stocks while they have momentum, then rotate out when their relative strength fades and other candidates emerge.

This dynamic approach has a trade-off. Regular turnover (buying and selling as new stocks enter and old ones exit) incurs trading costs and can trigger taxable gains in taxable accounts. The rotation discipline also means the fund may exit a winner prematurely — selling a stock whose underlying business is sound simply because another candidate has developed better near-term momentum. Over long periods, a strategy that always chases relative strength can lag buy-and-hold investors who own stable quality stocks.

## Active management in an ETF wrapper

FVC is an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) structured as an exchange-traded product, which means shares trade on an exchange during market hours at stock-like prices, but the portfolio itself reflects active management decisions. This is different from a passive index ETF, where the holdings follow a fixed rule.

The trade-off is that the fund charges an [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) that typically exceeds that of a passive [index fund](/index-fund/), to cover the cost of ongoing research and portfolio management. Investors receive the benefit of active decision-making; they pay for it through higher fees.

## Concentration risk and reward

Like the original Focus 5 product, FVC holds only up to five positions at any time. This concentration can amplify returns when the selected stocks outperform the broad market and can deepen drawdowns when they underperform. A single holding that encounters trouble causes meaningful damage to fund performance.

The dynamic rebalancing approach is intended to mitigate this risk: if one of the five companies begins to deteriorate in relative strength, it will be replaced by a stronger candidate in the next rebalancing cycle. In theory, this prevents the fund from being trapped in a deteriorating position. In practice, execution timing matters. If a stock falls sharply between rebalancing dates, the fund suffers the loss before the manager can rotate out.

## Who is this for?

FVC appeals to investors who believe active management can outperform passive indexing and who are comfortable with the volatility of a concentrated portfolio. It is not appropriate for risk-averse investors, those with short time horizons, or those who need predictable portfolio composition from month to month.

The fund's frequent rebalancing makes it less tax-efficient in taxable accounts than a buy-and-hold approach; tax-advantaged accounts are often a better home for actively managed funds that trade frequently.

## Evaluating the approach

Assess FVC by comparing its returns to a broad large-cap index (such as the [S&P 500 index](/sp-500-index/)) over multiple market cycles — ideally 5, 10, and 15 years where available. Has the dynamic selection process delivered returns that justify its higher expense ratio and [concentration risk](/concentration-risk/)? Watch the fund's turnover rate and the overlap between its current holdings and those of a few months prior; understand how frequently the strategy rotates.

Research the five current holdings directly to assess whether they genuinely have the relative-strength and fundamental characteristics the methodology claims to identify. The strength of an active strategy ultimately depends on whether the manager's edge is real and durable.
