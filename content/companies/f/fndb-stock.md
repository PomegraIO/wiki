*The Schwab Fundamental U.S. Broad Market ETF (FNDB) holds a diversified, fundamental-weighted portfolio of U.S. equities spanning large-cap, mid-cap, and small-cap companies — selected and weighted according to profitability, cash flow, and balance-sheet metrics rather than [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/) alone.*

## The fund and its index

FNDB tracks the [Fundamental Index](/fundamental-index/) Investable Universe U.S. Large Company and Small Cap Index, a broad measure of U.S. equity market value constructed by Research Affiliates. The fund holds roughly 1,200–1,500 companies, making it a comprehensive snapshot of the investable U.S. stock universe. Unlike a traditional total-market index, which weights each company by its market cap and therefore gives outsized weight to the largest, most popular firms, FNDB reweights its holdings based on fundamental measures: earnings, cash flow, book value of assets, and [dividend](/dividend/) payouts.

The size distribution skews heavily toward large-cap names — they remain the majority of holdings by value — but the weighting methodology ensures that mid-cap and small-cap companies receive more weight than they would in a market-cap index of the same universe. This creates a value tilt relative to pure market-cap indexing, a structural bias that shows up in the fund's performance over multi-year periods.

## Mechanical rebalancing and turnover

The fund rebalances annually, typically once per year. Each year, positions are reweighted so that the largest holdings are those with the strongest fundamental scores, not those with the highest share prices. This means FNDB systematically buys companies whose earnings or cash flows have improved (pushing up their fundamental scores) and sells those where fundamentals have deteriorated or valuations have soared. The annual rebalancing creates a mild mean-reversion tilt — holding companies that have underperformed in price and underweighting those that have outperformed — which over time can capture some gains from cyclical reversions in the market.

Turnover is modest by active-management standards, typically 10–15% annually, which reflects the systematic nature of the rebalancing and the fund's passive mandate. This level of turnover is higher than a static market-cap index (which only turns over when companies enter or leave the investable universe), but lower than an [actively managed fund](/actively-managed-fund/) that adjusts holdings in response to changing economic forecasts.

## Costs and on-exchange trading

The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) sits around 0.16–0.20% per year, making FNDB one of the lower-cost equity [index funds](/index-fund/) in the market. For a typical investor holding $100,000 in the fund, that translates to roughly $160–200 annually. The low cost reflects Schwab's scale, the systematic nature of the indexing methodology, and the lack of human analysts trying to pick winners.

FNDB trades on the [Nasdaq](/nasdaq/) with solid liquidity — typical daily volumes are in the hundreds of thousands of shares — so entry and exit are efficient even for larger investors. [Bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) are usually under 0.01%, negligible compared to the fund's expenses.

## What the fundamental tilt actually achieves

Proponents of fundamental indexing argue that market-cap weighting mechanically overweights expensive companies and underweights cheap ones, creating a structural performance drag. By reweighting to fundamentals, the methodology corrects this bias and captures a modest valuation-driven advantage. Empirical research shows that fundamental-weighted indexes have historically outperformed market-cap indexes over long periods, though the outperformance is not consistent year to year and sometimes appears in certain market environments but not others.

The stronger theoretical argument is [diversification](/diversification/): FNDB's weighting puts less emphasis on whatever handful of mega-cap companies the market currently favours. During periods when those mega-caps do very well, FNDB trails the market. During broader market rallies that include smaller and mid-cap companies, FNDB tends to keep pace or lead. The fund is not attempting to time markets or pick stocks — it is simply holding the market differently.

## Sector positioning and market-regime dependence

Because of its fundamental-weighting approach, FNDB is permanently overweight to industrials, financials, energy, and materials — sectors with strong cash generation and tangible assets — and underweight to technology and consumer discretionary. This sector positioning is not a bet on management's view of the economic cycle; it emerges naturally from the methodology. During periods when the overweighted sectors perform well, the fund outperforms. When tech leadership dominates (as it has in some recent years), FNDB lags.

This is not a flaw, but it is a structural bias that investors should understand before committing capital. FNDB is not a market-neutral or 100% diversified broad-market proxy; it is a broad-market fund with a consistent value and profitability tilt.

## Who should hold FNDB and how to evaluate it

FNDB suits investors who believe that fundamental quality and value metrics offer a durable edge and who want a core, low-cost equity holding that captures broad U.S. market exposure with a value slant. It is not for market-timing or performance-chasing; it is a buy-and-hold core position.

Evaluate FNDB by comparing its long-term returns (five years or longer) against both a traditional market-cap-weighted total U.S. market fund and your own performance needs. Review its sector allocation to understand its structural tilts. Read Schwab's prospectus and most recent [annual report](/10-k/) for the index methodology and current holdings. If you favour growth or mega-cap momentum, FNDB may underperform during those regimes; if you believe value and small-cap quality are durable edges, it aligns with that view.
