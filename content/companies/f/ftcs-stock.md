*The First Trust Capital Strength ETF is a rules-based fund that holds large and mid-sized US companies selected for financial strength and the efficient deployment of capital. Rather than owning every company in a broad index, FTCS applies a proprietary screening process to identify firms that generate strong returns on capital and maintain fortress-like [balance sheets](/balance-sheet/).*

## Capital strength screening

At the heart of FTCS lies the First Trust Capital Strength Score, a quantitative assessment of how efficiently a company deploys its capital and the robustness of its financial position. The fund screens the Russell 1000 universe for stocks that score highly on metrics including [return on equity](/return-on-equity/), [return on assets](/return-on-assets/), profit margins, and the ratio of earnings to debt. The philosophy is straightforward: a company that generates high returns on the capital it has raised, and that finances itself conservatively, is more likely to weather downturns and compound shareholder value over time.

The screening process is transparent and rules-based. Every company in the Russell 1000 receives a score based on its reported financials; the fund holds the stocks with the highest scores. This approach eliminates any human discretion in the selection process and makes the fund's holdings deterministic — if you know the financial reporting data, you can predict which stocks will be in the portfolio.

## Portfolio composition and sector lean

FTCS typically holds between 180 and 220 stocks, a notable narrowing from the full Russell 1000's thousand-plus constituents. The concentration toward capital-strength quality means the portfolio over-represents economically durable industries. Financials, healthcare, consumer staples, and industrials generally have stronger representation in FTCS than in the Russell 1000 itself, while the fund is underweight to speculative or capital-light technology firms and unprofitable high-growth names.

This quality bias is deliberate: the fund's creators believe that rewarding companies with strong capital efficiency will produce better risk-adjusted returns over long periods. The concentrated portfolio also means individual positions carry more weight than they would in a cap-weighted index, which affects both volatility and the specific stock risk an investor must accept.

## Expense ratio and tradability

FTCS trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) with reasonable daily volume, making it accessible to both small retail investors and larger institutions. The [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) sits in the economical range for a systematic, actively managed ETF. The fund's steady focus on balance-sheet strength and return-on-capital metrics has attracted institutional interest, which has contributed to adequate liquidity and asset growth.

The fund is most suitable for investors who believe that capital-efficient, financially strong businesses will outperform over a market cycle, or those seeking to tilt a broad US equity exposure toward quality. It also appeals to conservative allocators who want to avoid the most leveraged or speculative corners of the [stock market](/stock-market/) while maintaining large-cap equity exposure.

## Risks and limitations

The quality screens that define FTCS mean the fund is never entirely market-representative. In periods when low-quality, high-leverage, or unprofitable growth stocks lead the market — as happened in certain technology booms — FTCS will lag. The fund is also geographically concentrated in the United States and carries all the sector and company-specific risks that any equity portfolio does. A major [recession](/recession/) or credit crisis could expose the limitations of even the strongest balance sheets.

Additionally, the systematic methodology locks in any blind spots in the screen design itself. If the Capital Strength Score undervalues certain types of competitive advantages or overvalues metrics that correlate with cyclical peaks, the fund will inherit those biases.

## How to evaluate FTCS

Investors considering FTCS should request or download the fund's prospectus and fact sheet to understand the exact metrics that drive the Capital Strength Score. Comparing the fund's rolling returns to the Russell 1000 or to a simple broad-market fund like an S&P 500 ETF over multiple market cycles — growth markets, downturns, and flat periods — will reveal whether the quality screen adds value after fees. The fund's current sector and geographic allocation can be reviewed on the sponsor's website or fund information pages.
