Fortis Inc. is a holding company that owns and operates ten regulated electric and natural gas utilities across three geographic regions: Canada, the United States, and the Caribbean. The company does not generate its own power or pump its own gas; it buys those commodities wholesale and distributes them over networks its subsidiaries own and maintain. This is the core of the North American utility business — a low-growth, high-certainty enterprise structured for customers who need power and investors who crave stability. Fortis serves more than three million customers and operates across sixteen regulatory jurisdictions, and its business is 100 percent regulated.

## How Fortis operates across borders and business lines

The company's Canadian operations span three provinces. FortisBC Inc. delivers electricity in British Columbia; FortisBC Energy Inc. distributes natural gas, also in British Columbia; FortisAlberta serves both electricity and natural gas customers in Alberta; and Newfoundland Power delivers electricity to the island. These subsidiaries operate under regulatory regimes that set the rates they charge and the returns they can earn — a crucial detail because it eliminates one source of uncertainty that would otherwise define the business. If Fortis provides reliable service and maintains its infrastructure efficiently, the regulator will allow it to charge whatever rate is necessary to cover its reasonable costs plus an approved return on its invested capital. This is not a market the company has to win, but a formula it has to satisfy.

South of the border, Fortis owns ITC Holdings Corp., a transmission company that operates high-voltage lines in the Great Lakes region and the upper Midwest; UNS Energy Corporation, which serves Arizona; and Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corporation in New York State. These American utilities operate under similar regulatory frameworks, though the details vary by state.

The Caribbean portfolio spans three major utilities: Caribbean Utilities Company in the Cayman Islands, Fortis Turks and Caicos in the Turks and Caicos Islands, and Belize Electricity Limited in Belize. These serve smaller customer bases but operate under regulated frameworks comparable to the North American utilities.

## Revenue and earnings stability

Fortis is fundamentally a cash-generation machine for a specific type of investor. The company receives a steady, predictable stream of revenue from customers who must buy electricity and gas regardless of economic conditions. Operating costs are largely known — wages, maintenance, depreciation of known asset lives — and the regulator ensures the company can recover them. The result is that earnings are highly visible years in advance. This visibility is why utilities are owned by pension funds, insurance companies, and individual retirees seeking income. Fortis has raised its annual dividend for fifty consecutive years, one of only two Canadian companies to have done so. That track record reflects not explosive profit growth, but a reliable machine earning enough to raise the payout every year.

## Capital intensity and the growth constraint

Utilities are capital-intensive businesses. Maintaining aging electricity grids and gas pipelines, expanding service to new neighborhoods, upgrading to modern technology, and complying with evolving environmental and safety standards all require continuous reinvestment. Fortis has outlined a multibillion-dollar capital plan for the coming years, funded primarily from operating cash flow and debt. This capital spending drives growth, but the growth is steady, not explosive — it follows population growth and electrification trends, not entrepreneurial breakthroughs.

The company's non-regulated operations include long-term generation assets in British Columbia and Belize, plus the Aitken Creek natural gas storage facility. These contribute less to profit than the regulated utilities but serve strategic roles, either as physical assets that feed into regulated utility networks or as inflation hedges for long-term power contracts.

## Competition and the moat

Fortis faces no direct price competition. Its subsidiaries have geographic monopolies within their service territories, granted by regulators in exchange for a commitment to serve all customers at approved rates. The real competition is for capital and for regulatory approval — Fortis must convince the regulator that its investment plans make sense and its operational performance warrants its requested return on equity.

The lasting advantage lies in the regulatory franchise itself and the utility's track record. Regulators are reluctant to disrupt established utilities because disruption harms customers. Once a utility has proven it can deliver reliable service efficiently, switching it out for a competitor is not on the menu. The cost of regulatory abandonment is the company's main genuine risk.

## Risks to the business model

Regulatory change poses the clearest threat. Regulators can reduce approved returns, deny rate increases, impose new environmental requirements, or in rare cases, revoke a utility's license. Energy transition away from fossil fuels may eventually alter the economics of natural gas distribution. Catastrophic events — hurricanes in the Caribbean, ice storms in Canada — can damage infrastructure and drive one-time charges. A prolonged recession could depress demand for electricity and gas, though essential services are typically resilient.

The company also carries significant debt, which is standard for utilities but not without consequence if interest rates remain elevated and refinancing becomes expensive.

## How to research Fortis as an investment

Start with the company's annual 10-K filing (SEC CIK 0001666175), which details operations by subsidiary and jurisdiction. The quarterly results reveal trends in customer growth, operating margins, and capital spending. Watch the quarterly earnings calls for updates on regulatory filings, approval decisions, and any material changes to the company's utility franchises.

Key metrics to track: the dividend history and payout ratio, which show how much excess cash the company retains for growth versus returning to shareholders; the debt-to-capital ratio, which indicates financial leverage; and the weighted average cost of capital, which shows what the regulatory environment allows the company to earn. Tracking regulatory developments in major jurisdictions — particularly any changes to allowed returns in New York, Arizona, or British Columbia — signals whether Fortis's cost of capital is rising or falling, which ripples through every subsidiary's profitability. The company's annual dividend increase is the most closely watched metric by its core shareholder base, because it has become a signal of the company's health and the regulator's willingness to let it earn a reasonable return.
