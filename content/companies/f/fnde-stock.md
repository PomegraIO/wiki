*The Schwab Fundamental Emerging Markets [Equity ETF](/equity-etf/) (FNDE) holds a diversified portfolio of equities from emerging-market countries across Asia, Latin America, Eastern Europe, and Africa, weighted by fundamental measures of profitability and quality rather than [market capitalization](/market-capitalization/).*

> Emerging markets offer faster economic growth and lower valuations than developed markets, but with meaningfully higher volatility, political risk, and currency exposure — FNDE is not a diversifying sleepy position, but a concentrated bet on developing-world opportunity.

## What emerging markets are and why FNDE exists

Emerging markets comprise the developing and middle-income economies of the world — nations growing faster than the U.S. or Western Europe, where rising incomes, urbanisation, and industrialisation are accelerating. The major constituents of emerging-market indexes are China, India, Taiwan, Brazil, Mexico, Russia, South Korea, Indonesia, and Thailand, among others. These economies represent the majority of global economic growth and population, yet their [stock markets](/stock-market/) often trade at cheaper valuations than developed markets because investors demand a risk premium for currency instability, geopolitical uncertainty, and less transparent corporate governance.

FNDE tracks the [Fundamental Index](/fundamental-index/) Investable Universe Emerging Markets Index, which selects a broad universe of emerging-market equities and weights them by fundamentals — earnings, [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/), book value, and [dividends](/dividend/) — rather than by market cap. This approach aims to capture exposure to faster growth at reasonable valuations while avoiding the concentration in the largest, most-favoured emerging-market names. The fund holds roughly 800–1,000 stocks, providing substantial [diversification](/diversification/) across multiple countries, sectors, and company sizes.

## Currency exposure and country composition

Unlike a fund focused on a single foreign country, FNDE is exposed to the currency movements of dozens of developing nations simultaneously. The [Brazilian real](/brazilian-real/), [Indian rupee](/indian-rupee/), Chinese yuan, [Mexican peso](/mexian-peso/), Thai baht, and others all affect returns for a dollar-based investor. When emerging-market currencies weaken against the dollar (a common occurrence during global risk-off periods), FNDE's returns are dampened even if the underlying stocks hold their value. Conversely, when emerging-market currencies appreciate, returns are magnified. This [currency volatility](/currency-volatility/) is a structural feature of emerging-market investing and cannot be avoided without hedging costs.

The country composition shifts over time as economies grow and shrink in importance. China and India typically represent the largest allocations, followed by smaller weights in dozens of other markets. Some holdings operate in geopolitical gray zones — Taiwan, for example, has significant tech exposure but faces political uncertainty. Brazil and Mexico have experienced periods of [currency crisis](/currency-crisis/) and [capital flight](/capital-flight-sovereign/). Russia has faced severe sanctions and equity-market freezes at various points. These risks are inherent to emerging-market exposure and can materialise quickly and dramatically.

## Structural advantages and limitations of fundamental weighting

The fundamental-indexing approach is particularly valuable in emerging markets because information asymmetry and limited analyst coverage are more pronounced than in developed markets. A small-cap pharmaceutical company in India or a manufacturing concern in Mexico might trade at a steep discount to its underlying profitability simply because few Western investors and analysts follow it. By weighting to fundamentals, FNDE captures some of that overlooked value.

However, fundamental quality in emerging markets often correlates with state ownership, oligopoly control, or family dominance — structures that can shift suddenly due to political change. A profitable state-owned enterprise in one country might face expropriation or forced restructuring; a family-controlled conglomerate might face governance challenges. The fund's focus on profitability does not eliminate these risks; it only tilts toward companies that have demonstrated recent strength.

## Costs, liquidity, and entry-exit dynamics

FNDE's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) is typically around 0.30–0.35% annually, reflecting the costs of trading in less-liquid emerging-market [stock exchanges](/stock-exchange/) and the complexity of tracking a broad, fundamental-weighted international index. For a $100,000 investment, this means roughly $300–350 per year in fees. The fund trades on the [Nasdaq](/nasdaq/) during U.S. hours with reasonable daily volumes, but liquidity in the underlying stocks in emerging markets can evaporate during stress periods, making it difficult for the fund manager to execute large rebalancing trades without market impact.

Investors should expect that entering or exiting a significant FNDE position can take time and may face execution costs. A large redemption of fund shares can force the manager to sell emerging-market stocks in thin trading conditions, and conversely, a large inflow can limit the manager's ability to deploy capital without moving markets.

## Performance, volatility, and the long-term case

Emerging markets are inherently more volatile than developed markets. A combination of factors — currency swings, political uncertainty, commodity-price dependence, and less-mature financial systems — creates larger drawdowns during market panics and faster recoveries during risk-on periods. FNDE is not a steady, predictable holding; it is a concentrated bet on global growth, and it will underperform during periods of developed-market strength or global risk-aversion.

That said, the long-term historical case for some emerging-market exposure is strong: developing economies grow faster than mature ones, populations are younger, and valuations are often cheaper. The fundamental-weighting approach, by focusing on profitability and quality, offers a more disciplined entry point than chasing the most popular names in a herd-driven market. The key is understanding that FNDE is a satellite position, not a core holding, and sizing it appropriately based on risk tolerance and time horizon. Investors with a five-year-plus horizon and comfort with volatile swings have historically been rewarded by emerging-market exposure; shorter-term or risk-averse investors often find the swings unmanageable. Before investing, study the prospectus, review the country and sector weightings, and honestly assess your ability to hold through multi-year drawdowns.
