The First Trust [Nasdaq](/nasdaq/) Pharmaceuticals ETF (FTXH) is a sector-focused equity fund that tracks the pharmaceutical and life-sciences companies within the Nasdaq-100 index. Managed by First Trust Advisors, FTXH offers shareholders a liquid, diversified exposure to the drug manufacturing, biotech development, and pharmaceutical supply industries without requiring individual stock selection. For decades, pharmaceutical equities have been among the most actively traded securities, and FTXH channels that activity into a single basket designed to move in lockstep with the industry as a whole.

## The emergence of pharma sector ETFs

The first broad pharmaceutical [sector funds](/sector-fund/) emerged in the early 2000s as exchange-traded funds began to proliferate across sectors. Investors had long recognized the stable, recurring-revenue characteristics of large pharmaceutical companies — their products enjoy patent protection, recurring prescription demand, and often government-mandated reimbursement — yet individual pharma stocks required specialist knowledge to evaluate. Drug pipelines, regulatory timelines, patent cliffs, and clinical trial results demanded research depth that many individual investors lacked. A pharma-focused ETF let investors access that entire industry without picking single companies or timing drug approvals.

FTXH arrived as Nasdaq-specific indexing grew more granular. Rather than encompass all Nasdaq-listed drug makers, the fund targets those that meet the Nasdaq-100's liquidity and market-cap criteria, creating a concentrated basket of the largest and most actively traded pharmaceutical businesses. This design ensures adequate trading volume and lower [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) than a broader pharma fund might offer.

## How pharma companies operate and why they cluster on Nasdaq

Pharmaceutical firms range from multinational integrated drug manufacturers (which develop, produce, and market medicines) to smaller research-stage biotechs that license compounds or pursue novel targets. Nasdaq has historically attracted the more innovative and higher-growth names, particularly younger biotech firms pursuing novel therapies. The index captures a mix of revenue-generating incumbents and earlier-stage companies with promising pipelines but not yet profitable.

The pharmaceutical business model revolves around patent protection. A new drug discovered by a firm enjoys exclusivity for a set period (typically 20 years from patent filing), during which the company captures large margins on sales. Once that patent expires, generic competitors flood the market and prices collapse, forcing pharmaceutical firms to continually discover and commercialize new medicines to offset declining revenues from aging products. This creates a high-stakes R&D cycle: a company that fails to fill its pipeline with successful new drugs faces revenue cliffs when blockbuster products lose exclusivity.

## The composition and strategy of FTXH

FTXH does not attempt to weight holdings by whether a drug is likely to succeed or fail. Instead, it replicates its benchmark index, holding stocks of pharmaceutical and biotech companies in proportion to their weight in the Nasdaq-100. Larger companies with higher [market capitalizations](/market-capitalization/) receive larger allocations. This passive approach means FTXH makes no judgment about whether a given firm's pipeline is promising or a given patent cliff is surmountable; it simply owns what the index holds.

The fund includes integrated pharmaceutical manufacturers (large, profitable, dividend-paying companies with diversified drug portfolios), pure-play biotechs (often unprofitable but high-growth), and companies that bridge these categories. Rebalancing happens when the Nasdaq-100 index is adjusted, which occurs infrequently, reducing trading costs relative to funds that rebalance more actively.

## Regulatory headwinds and drug pricing

Pharmaceutical companies face unique regulatory pressures. Drug approvals depend on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which can delay or reject even well-developed compounds. Pricing is increasingly scrutinized by governments and payers; the United States permits drug manufacturers to set prices freely, but many other countries negotiate or impose price controls, constraining margins. Patent challenges from generics manufacturers and compulsory licensing disputes add further uncertainty.

These regulatory risks affect all holdings in FTXH simultaneously during periods of political pressure on drug pricing or patent policy. When Congress debates drug-price legislation or the FDA tightens approval standards, the entire fund can move sharply regardless of individual company prospects. This sector-wide regulatory exposure is an inherent risk of concentrated pharma ownership.

## Diversification limits and sector concentration

Unlike a total-market [index fund](/index-fund/), FTXH places all capital in a single industry. If pharmaceutical and biotech stocks fall as a group — due to regulatory headwinds, patent cliff disappointments, or contagion from a major company scandal — there is no offsetting strength in technology, finance, or healthcare services to cushion the blow. The fund is also concentrated by construction; the largest holdings can represent significant portions of assets, so individual company crises ripple through the fund more than they would in a broader pharmaceutical index.

[Dividend](/dividend/) income varies: large established pharmaceutical firms typically pay substantial dividends as part of their capital-allocation strategies, while smaller biotechs often retain earnings to fund R&D. FTXH shareholders receive the aggregate [dividend yield](/dividend-yield/) of the basket, which tends to be moderate compared to consumer staples but higher than pure-growth indices.

## Researching FTXH and pharmaceutical markets

Investors interested in FTXH should begin with the fund's prospectus and fact sheet, available from First Trust, which detail holdings, the benchmark index methodology, and [expense ratios](/expense-ratio/). The Nasdaq-100 index composition and changes are published by Nasdaq, enabling investors to understand what companies are included and how their weights shift over time.

Tracking pharmaceutical markets requires following regulatory and clinical calendars: FDA approval timelines, patent expirations, and trial readouts shape individual company outlooks and sector sentiment. The fund is most useful for investors who believe in the long-term growth of drug demand (driven by aging populations, chronic disease prevalence, and medical innovation) but lack the expertise to handicap individual drug pipelines. For those willing to research specific companies or bet against pharmaceutical price pressures, direct stock ownership or sector-specific active funds may be more suitable.
