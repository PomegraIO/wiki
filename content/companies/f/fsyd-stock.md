*The **Fidelity Sustainable High Yield ETF** (FSYD) marries two trends that once seemed contradictory: chasing yield in below-investment-grade [corporate bonds](/corporate-bond/), and restricting holdings to companies with credible environmental, social, and governance standards. It is an attempt to answer a question many yield-hungry investors ask: can I buy [junk bonds](/junk-bond/) without the guilt?*

[High-yield bonds](/high-yield-bond/)—securities rated below investment grade (typically BB and lower)—have been for decades the wild-card market where risk-seekers hunt for above-market income. FSYD takes that familiar playbook and layers on screening. The fund's managers exclude companies involved in thermal coal, controversial weapons, and severe labor violations, and favor issuers with stronger governance and environmental practices. The result is a high-yield portfolio that claims to carry less reputational and ESG-related risk while still offering junk-bond income.

The math is straightforward. Investment-grade corporate [bonds](/bond/)—those rated BBB and above—yield comparatively little because default risk is low. High-yield bonds, issued by shakier firms, pay substantially more to compensate investors for the higher chance of default. A typical high-yield bond might pay 6–8% or higher, while an [investment-grade bond](/investment-grade-bond/) might pay 4–5%. That 2–3 percentage-point spread is the income premium for taking on [credit risk](/credit-risk/). FSYD offers that premium but tries to improve the risk profile by weighting toward the more stable, better-governed issuers within the junk-bond universe.

The ESG screen is not a guarantee of safety. A company that passes environmental and social filters can still default if its business deteriorates or if credit markets turn hostile. The screening is a tilt, not a shield. But for investors who care about not funding companies in industries like thermal coal or weapons, FSYD offers a compromise: still get the junk-bond yield, just from companies deemed "better."

Active management underlies the fund's decisions. Fidelity's fixed-income team selects which high-yield bonds to hold, applies the ESG screens, and re-weights the portfolio based on credit conditions and market outlook. This is different from a passive junk-bond [index fund](/index-fund/), which would simply hold the index constituents equally. FSYD's managers have discretion to avoid bonds they view as especially risky and to concentrate in issuers they believe will survive a potential downturn.

The fund's holdings span industries. Issuers range from cable and telecom companies (historically heavy borrowers) to healthcare, consumer, and energy firms. Because high-yield bonds are issued by companies at higher leverage, the portfolio is naturally exposed to sectors where debt is common and default risk is real. A [recession](/recession/) that cuts revenue for an overleveraged cable or retail company can quickly threaten default.

Costs matter. FSYD's [expense ratio](/expense-ratio/) covers Fidelity's active management, research, and the ETF infrastructure. This annual drag is deducted from returns and represents the price of the ESG screening and active oversight. An investor must believe Fidelity's risk management and security selection will overcome that fee drag to justify owning FSYD over a cheaper passive high-yield index [fund.

Liquidity](/fund-liquidity/) in FSYD itself is solid; the fund trades on the [NASDAQ](/nasdaq/) with tight [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) given Fidelity's resources. But the underlying bonds—high-yield corporate bonds trading in the [over-the-counter market](/over-the-counter-market/)—are less liquid than large-cap stocks or Treasuries. In a credit panic, bid-ask spreads on high-yield bonds can widen dramatically, and FSYD's [net asset value](/net-asset-value/) can disconnect temporarily from the true market value of its holdings.

[Interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) is present. As rates rise, the value of existing bonds with fixed coupons falls (because new issuance will come with higher coupons). High-yield bonds are more sensitive to credit conditions than to rate moves, but they are not immune. A sharp rate increase coupled with slowing growth can trigger defaults in the junk-bond space and push FSYD down sharply.

Duration—the fund's sensitivity to rate moves—is modest compared to investment-grade bond funds because high-yield bonds have shorter average maturities. But the real risk is not rates; it is [credit spread](/credit-spread/) widening. When investors become worried about defaults, high-yield spreads widen, and prices fall. In the February-to-March 2020 period, for example, high-yield spreads blew out to historic levels, and FSYD units fell sharply as investors fled risk.

For a reader considering FSYD, the key is to understand the trade-off clearly. You are taking credit risk—the chance that issuers default and you lose principal—in exchange for income above investment-grade yields. The ESG screens reduce the risk of reputational damage and focus the portfolio on companies with better-run businesses, but they do not eliminate credit risk. FSYD is appropriate for investors who understand junk bonds, can tolerate volatility, and believe the ESG tilt aligns with their values. For conservative income seekers, investment-grade alternatives carry lower risk of loss. For risk-tolerant investors seeking maximum yield, an unscreened junk-bond index fund offers more holdings and slightly lower fees, though at the cost of ESG compromises.

Start with the prospectus and fact sheet to review current holdings, the ESG methodology, and the expense ratio. Track the fund's duration and [default rate](/default-rate/) of its holdings relative to the broader high-yield market. Monitor credit spreads: when high-yield spreads are tight, FSYD is priced for stability; when spreads are wide, it offers opportunity (at higher risk). Finally, watch the economic environment. Recessions and credit crunches are the enemy of junk-bond funds; strong, stable growth is the friend.
