**What it makes:** FormFactor manufactures specialized probe systems and handlers — precision equipment that semiconductor makers use to test and validate wafers during manufacturing. When a chip fab produces a batch of silicon wafers, workers need to confirm that each die on each wafer meets electrical specifications before the wafers proceed to packaging. FormFactor's systems interface with the wafer to probe its electrical contacts, measure performance, and identify defects. The company also makes test handlers, the robotics systems that move wafers between testing stations at high speed without damage. Neither probe systems nor handlers are glamorous products, but they are essential infrastructure in the chip-manufacturing process, and they are expensive — a single probe card can cost tens of thousands of dollars.

**Origins and positioning:** FormFactor was founded in 1993 as semiconductor testing accelerated and probe-card design became specialized enough that a dedicated supplier made sense. The company has refined that focus ever since, building deep expertise in probe technology, thermal management, electrical measurement, and the mechanical precision required to touch a wafer thousands of times without introducing yield loss. That narrow expertise gives FormFactor genuine defensibility; a competitor must match not just the mechanical design but years of application knowledge across hundreds of chip architectures and test conditions.

**The economics of test equipment:** The chip industry is capital-intensive. Fabs are expensive to build (tens of billions of dollars each), and they operate with narrow margins and fierce competition on cost per wafer. Test and measurement are not a huge cost in the overall manufacturing flow, but they are critical — a yield loss of one percentage point in a large-volume fab can amount to tens of millions of dollars per year. FormFactor's systems help fabs avoid that loss by identifying defects early, before costly downstream processing steps. That economic value — the cost avoidance — is what justifies the price of probe cards and handlers.

FormFactor sells mostly to large foundries and integrated-device manufacturers (Samsung, TSMC, [Intel](/intc-stock/), and others). These are long-term relationships built on technical trust and proven reliability. When a fab qualifies a FormFactor probe system for a particular chip process, switching to a competitor means retesting and re-validation — a burden that creates stickiness. That stickiness is the foundation of the business model.

**Revenue streams:** FormFactor's revenue is primarily from selling probe systems and handlers. Once installed, these systems generate aftermarket revenue through consumables (replacement probes, wear parts) and service contracts. The consumables margin is typically higher than the system margin, so a long-installed base of systems generates valuable recurring revenue. The company also derives revenue from software and data analytics — as wafer testing produces enormous amounts of data, fabs are increasingly interested in analytics tools to predict yield or process drift. That shift from one-time hardware sales toward recurring software and analytics is a multi-year trend in the company's business model.

**Exposure to chip-industry cycles:** FormFactor's profitability swings with chip-industry capital spending. When demand for chips is strong, fabs expand capacity, buy new equipment, and invest in yield optimization. When chip demand softens, fabs defer capital spending, and equipment suppliers see order declines and inventory build-ups. The company is leveraged to two distinct cycles: the broad semiconductor demand cycle (smartphone demand, server spending, automotive electronics) and the equipment-refresh cycle within fabs themselves. The company cannot control either.

**Process technology transitions:** The shift from traditional silicon to advanced nodes (smaller transistor sizes, higher densities, new materials) introduces opportunity and risk for FormFactor. More advanced nodes require more sophisticated testing — higher frequencies, more-precise measurements, advanced probe designs — which can support premium pricing for FormFactor's systems. But the transition also raises the bar for technical performance. A probe system that works for 5-nanometer processes is not compatible with 3-nanometer or smaller, so the company must continuously innovate to stay relevant as the industry advances. That R&D burden is substantial and non-negotiable.

**Geographic and customer concentration:** FormFactor derives substantial revenue from Asian foundries, especially in Taiwan and South Korea, which are hubs for advanced chip manufacturing. That concentration gives it exposure to geopolitical risk and to the fortunes of specific fabs. A slowdown at TSMC or Samsung impacts FormFactor. The company also operates in a highly consolidated customer base — perhaps a dozen major customers represent the bulk of revenue. Loss of a large customer or a major product pause at a key account can significantly impact quarterly revenue.

**Capital intensity and [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/):** FormFactor is not a capital-intensive manufacturer (it outsources production), but it is R&D-intensive. It maintains a healthy balance sheet and generates solid [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/), which it has historically deployed toward [dividends](/dividend/), [share buybacks](/share-buyback/), and occasional [acquisitions](/acquisition/) to broaden its product portfolio. The company does not require heavy external financing to operate, which insulates it from interest-rate pressure better than heavily leveraged peers.

**Research points:** An analyst studying FormFactor should examine the company's quarterly revenue by geography and end-market to understand concentration and the health of major customers. Track capital-equipment orders and commentary from quarterly earnings calls on fab spending trends. Look at the company's R&D spending and product roadmap — high R&D is necessary but must translate into customer adoption of new systems. Review the backlog and order-book trends; strong orders indicate customer confidence, while order weakening can precede revenue declines. Compare FormFactor's margins to peers like Xcerra or LTX-Credence to understand relative operational efficiency. Finally, monitor announcements of new process-node transitions by TSMC, Samsung, and Intel, as these events often trigger renewed equipment spending and represent inflection points for the test-equipment industry.
