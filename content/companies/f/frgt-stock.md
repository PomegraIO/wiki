*The modern freight market is caught between declining transport margins and rising shipper demand for real-time visibility and price efficiency. **Freight Technologies, Inc.** (FRGT) sits at that junction—a digital platform that connects shippers and freight carriers, extracting value by reducing the friction and information asymmetry that has plagued trucking and freight logistics for decades. Every transaction that flows through the platform carries embedded unit economics: a fee structure that must sustain carrier recruitment, shipper acquisition, and technology infrastructure while remaining thin enough that both sides keep using it.*

## The Dollar Per Load: Unit Economics at the Core

Freight brokers—middlemen who match shippers needing cargo moved with carriers who have truck capacity—have historically operated on razor-thin margins measured as a percentage of the freight cost. When a shipper pays $1,500 to move a load across state lines, a traditional broker typically earns $150–$250 on that transaction after paying the carrier. At Freight Technologies, the platform economics shift the bottleneck: the company does not own trucks or assume cargo liability. Instead, it earns a commission (or transaction fee) on each matched load. The math becomes clearer when unit economics are front-and-center: if the platform makes $50 per load and serves 100,000 loads per quarter, that is $5 million in quarterly transaction revenue—but only if customer acquisition cost (CAC) and payment processing costs do not exceed what the platform margin can support.

This is the hard constraint that shapes everything Freight Technologies must do. Every dollar earned per transaction competes directly against the cost of attracting and retaining both shippers and carriers. If the company spends $200 in marketing to acquire a shipper who sends 10 loads before switching to a competitor, the unit economics are negative. The inverse applies to the supply side: recruiting and keeping carriers on the network requires reliability, fair dispatch, and competitive rates. The platform cannot tilt too far toward either side without hollowing out the other.

## How the Platform Earns and What It Costs

The revenue model is categorical: per-transaction fees or a percentage take of the freight cost. Some platforms charge shippers a pickup or booking fee; others collect a percentage of the lane cost. Some impose carrier fees for access; others rely purely on shipper-side revenue. The composition of revenue—who pays what portion and how much per transaction—directly determines the unit-economic viability of the whole business.

Costs, in turn, split into a few predictable buckets. Technology infrastructure (servers, data pipelines, customer dashboards, matching algorithms) scales with traffic but has a fixed baseline. Customer support costs—handling disputes, booking issues, payment exceptions—vary with network size and platform stability. Carrier recruitment and shipper onboarding are customer-acquisition-driven; these costs are highest when the platform is new or entering a region. And there are payment-processing costs: when money flows between shipper and carrier through the platform, the company may absorb or pass along credit-card fees and fraud losses.

The tension in Freight Technologies' unit economics is structural: a high fee per transaction kills volume (shippers and carriers route around expensive platforms), while a low fee per transaction requires enormous scale to hit profitability. Most venture-backed logistics platforms have spent years subsidizing transactions to grow their networks, hoping to become sticky and valuable enough to eventually raise prices. The path from customer acquisition to profitability in a two-sided logistics network is long and uncertain.

## Carrier Stickiness and Network Density

On the supply side, carriers—trucking companies from owner-operators to small fleets—choose platforms based on how many loads they can find and how quickly they get paid. A platform with 1,000 available loads per day in a carrier's region becomes part of their daily hustle; one with 50 loads becomes an afterthought. Building network density per geography is expensive (more shipper salespeople, more carrier support, more marketing) but essential to unit economics. A thick network in Dallas or Atlanta is worth more per transaction than a thin network spread across 30 states.

Retention of carriers (and shippers) compounds the acquisition cost. If a carrier acquired for $500 in incentives stays on the platform for two years and generates $5,000 in commission, that is a 10x return on acquisition spend and radically changes unit profitability. If they stay six months, the return is negative. Freight Technologies' ability to keep both sides satisfied—carriers with steady, fair-paying loads; shippers with reliable pickup and delivery—determines whether the cost of growth ever converts to sustainable margin.

## Margin Compression in a Mature Market

The broader freight market is competitive and mature. Established brokers, 3PLs (third-party logistics firms), and even carrier networks operate at modest margins. As digital platforms proliferate and traditional brokers adopt their own apps, the transaction fees that platforms can command are under pressure. A shipper with negotiating leverage may demand lower commissions or shop between platforms. A carrier, seeing multiple apps on their phone all offering loads, will pursue the one with the best effective rates after fees.

For Freight Technologies, this means unit economics must improve—not through higher fees (which invite competition), but through operational efficiency. Smarter matching (fewer unmatched loads, faster fulfillment) reduces cost per transaction. Automation of customer support (chatbots, self-service exception handling) shrinks per-transaction support costs. And network effects matter: a dense, active platform generates more transaction data, which makes matching algorithms better, which increases velocity and reduces idle time, which improves margins. This is the long-term play—outrun competitors not by charging more, but by operating cheaper at the same volume.

## The Churn and Stickiness Problem

Platform businesses are subject to churn risk. A shipper using Freight Technologies for cost savings will abandon it if a competitor offers savings plus better service, or if they can negotiate directly with carriers they met through the platform. A carrier, once trained on the platform, may replicate the intelligence themselves or sign exclusive relationships with 3PLs. Churn directly translates to rising customer-acquisition costs: the company must spend more per year to maintain its base.

This is where unit economics become existential. If monthly churn of shippers is 5% and carriers is 3%, the company must acquire new customers just to hold steady. If churn is 10% on both sides, growth becomes impossible without spending proportionally more. Understanding the true churn rate—and the lifetime value of a customer after churn is factored in—is central to whether Freight Technologies' unit economics support a profitable, growing business. Public disclosures, 10-K filings, and quarterly earnings reports are the primary windows into whether the company is achieving the scale and efficiency necessary to escape the treadmill of high acquisition costs.
