*A **Managed Futures** strategy is a professional approach to trading [futures](/futures-contract/) and [forward contracts](/forward-contract/) across a broad range of asset classes—stocks, bonds, commodities, currencies—using either systematic trend-following rules or active [manager](/commodity-trading-advisor/) discretion. The strategy is typically employed by [CTAs](/commodity-trading-advisor/) and aims to capture [alpha](/alpha/) independent of traditional equity and bond markets.*

## The trend-following core

The most common managed futures approach is trend-following: identify an asset class (say, crude oil or the [S&P 500](/sp-500-index/)) that has been rising consistently, buy [futures](/futures-contract/), ride the trend upward, then exit when the trend reverses. Conversely, sell short when prices fall steadily. This simple mechanic requires no view on intrinsic value—only that momentum will persist in the near term.

Trend-following is appealing because it works across all markets. A single algorithm can trade stock indices, agricultural commodities, currencies, and bond yields simultaneously. When equities trend up, the strategy is long. When oil trends down, it's short. The [diversification](/diversification/) across these uncorrelated assets reduces the impact of any single market dislocation.

Historically, trend-following performed well during the 2008 financial crisis, when equities collapsed but commodity trends remained strong, allowing managed futures funds to offset losses. This defensive characteristic—profiting during equity bear markets—justifies the premium fees charged by active managers.

## Systematic versus discretionary execution

A **systematic** managed futures program relies on computer models. Pre-defined rules specify when to enter and exit trades based on price, volume, or other quantitative signals. The advantage is consistency—no emotion, no human bias, no Monday-morning second-guessing. The disadvantage is vulnerability to structural regime changes. If a strategy was built on 20 years of trending markets and suddenly enters a choppy, range-bound environment, the model may whipsaw: buying near tops, selling near bottoms.

A **discretionary** manager uses human judgment, responding to news events, central bank statements, and macroeconomic shifts in real time. The portfolio manager might override mechanical signals if they believe a trend is about to reverse. Discretionary managers argue they navigate crises better; systematic proponents counter that discretion invites [overconfidence bias](/overconfidence-bias/) and costly human error.

Many modern managed futures funds blend both—a core systematic trend-following engine supplemented by discretionary adjustments for tail risk or geopolitical shocks.

## Capital efficiency and leverage

A major attraction of managed futures is leverage. A [futures](/futures-contract/) contract on the [S&P 500](/sp-500-index/) may require only 10% margin—meaning a manager can control a $100,000 position with $10,000 of capital. This multiplies both gains and losses. In profitable years, leverage amplifies returns. In drawdown years, it can wipe out a fund.

Managed futures funds typically target a [volatility](/historical-volatility/) level (say, 12% annualized) across all positions combined. They scale position sizes to maintain consistent risk: larger positions in less-volatile assets, smaller ones in volatile commodities. This approach aims to maximize [alpha](/alpha/) per unit of risk taken.

However, leverage also introduces [liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/) and [counterparty risk](/counterparty-risk/). During market crises, when liquidity evaporates, even well-capitalized funds can be forced to liquidate at distressed prices. The 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management—a [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) employing quantitative strategies with heavy leverage—illustrated this peril.

## Diversification across time and markets

A managed futures strategy diversifies across three dimensions: time (short-term and long-term trends), markets (stocks, commodities, currencies, bonds), and strategies (trend-following, mean-reversion, volatility-based). During a stock [bear market](/bear-market/), agricultural [futures](/futures-contract/) may rally, currencies may strengthen, and [bond futures](/bond/) may trend upward, offsetting equity losses.

This cross-market correlation reduction is the core selling point. A traditional [stock fund](/growth-fund/) and [bond fund](/bond/) already provide [diversification](/diversification/); adding managed futures is meant to diversify even further. Empirically, well-designed managed futures exhibit low correlation with both equities and fixed income, making them a valuable portfolio additive for institutional investors.

## Costs and performance measurement

Managed futures funds typically charge a [management fee](/management-fee/) (0.5% to 2% annually) plus a [performance fee](/performance-fee/) (10% to 20% of profits). These higher fees reflect the complexity of trading, technology infrastructure, and active management. A 12% gross return with 2% management fee and 15% performance fee might net the investor only 6% after all costs—a significant drag.

Performance is measured in absolute terms, not relative to the stock market. A managed futures fund earning 5% in a year when the [S&P 500](/sp-500-index/) returns 15% is still successful if it achieves its volatility target and diversifies a broader portfolio. Institutional investors evaluate managed futures on [Sharpe ratio](/return-on-invested-capital/) (return per unit of risk) and correlation with their existing holdings rather than raw percentage gains.

## When managed futures stumble

Managed futures strategies struggle in choppy, range-bound markets. If commodities oscillate between $50 and $60 with no clear trend, trend-following algorithms buy near $60 and sell near $50 repeatedly, locking in losses. A manager might also face "trend exhaustion": years 2016–2017 saw historically low [volatility](/historical-volatility/), whipping out many trend-following funds.

Regime change is another killer. A strategy trained on 30 years of data might fail when correlations shift. During some crises, all [asset classes](/asset-allocation/) move together (flights to safety), making diversification within managed futures less effective.

## Integration into institutional portfolios

Endowments, pension funds, and large foundations typically allocate 5% to 15% of capital to managed futures. The expectation is not that managed futures will be the portfolio's largest return driver, but rather a defensive hedge that performs when traditional assets struggle. This role—crisis hedge and [diversifier](/diversification/)—justifies the higher fees.

Retail investors access managed futures through mutual funds, ETFs, and separate accounts managed by [CTAs](/commodity-trading-advisor/). Some mutual funds track managed futures indices, offering lower fees but less active management.
