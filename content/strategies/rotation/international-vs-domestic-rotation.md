*An **international vs domestic rotation** is a [tactical allocation](/wiki/tactical-asset-allocation/) decision to shift portfolio weight between home-country equities and foreign equity markets, responding to relative [valuation](/wiki/relative-valuation/), [earnings growth](/wiki/earnings-per-share/), and [currency](/wiki/currency-pair/) movements. A US investor rotating "into international" moves money from domestic [stocks](/wiki/stock/) toward [European](/wiki/european-option/), [Japanese](/wiki/japanese-yen/), or [emerging-market](/wiki/emerging-markets-fund/) equities.*

## Why rotation between home and foreign markets occurs

Equity markets in different regions move on different economic cycles, [interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate/), and valuations. The US [stock market](/wiki/stock-market/) might trade at a 18× [price-to-earnings multiple](/wiki/price-to-earnings-ratio/) while [European](/wiki/frankfurt-stock-market/) stocks trade at 14×, or vice versa. A rotation strategy attempts to buy the cheaper region and sell the more expensive one.

This is distinct from [strategic asset allocation](/wiki/strategic-asset-allocation/), which fixes your long-term weight to, say, 60% US equities and 40% international. A tactical rotation says: "60/40 is our baseline, but we will tactically move to 70% US and 30% international if the US looks especially cheap, or 50% US and 50% international if the international region has better [earnings growth](/wiki/earnings-per-share/) and lower [valuations](/wiki/valuation-real-options/)."

## Key drivers: valuation and earnings momentum

The primary inputs to a rotation decision are:

1. **Relative valuation:** Compare [P/E ratios](/wiki/price-to-earnings-ratio/), [price-to-book](/wiki/price-to-book-ratio/), and [dividend yields](/wiki/dividend-yield/) across regions. If US large-cap [stocks](/wiki/stock/) trade at 20× earnings and developed international at 15×, the international region appears cheaper on this metric.

2. **Earnings growth outlook:** A region with slower [GDP growth](/wiki/gross-domestic-product/) and slowing corporate earnings deserves a lower valuation. If US [earnings growth](/wiki/earnings-per-share/) is expected at 5% and European at 8%, the rotation might favor Europe.

3. **[Interest rate](/wiki/interest-rate/) differentials:** Higher [interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate/) in one region can raise discount rates and depress equity valuations, but they also signal growth risk. A rotation might favor the region with stable or declining rates.

4. **[Currency](/wiki/currency-pair/) valuation:** If the [US dollar](/wiki/us-dollar/) is at an all-time high versus major [currencies](/wiki/currency-pair/), returns from international equities are amplified (a foreign gain, when converted to dollars, yields more dollars). If the dollar is depressed, international returns suffer from [currency headwinds](/wiki/currency-hedging/).

## Emerging markets rotation within the international sleeve

Within the international allocation, many rotation strategies separate [developed international](/wiki/international-etf/) (Europe, Japan, Australia) from [emerging markets](/wiki/emerging-markets-fund/) (China, India, Brazil, Mexico). Emerging markets are younger, more volatile, and often more sensitive to [commodity](/wiki/commodity-etf/) cycles and [capital flows](/wiki/capital-flows/).

A rotation into [emerging markets](/wiki/emerging-markets-fund/) often occurs when:
- Commodity prices are rising (favoring [commodity exporters](/wiki/commodity-currency-pairs/) like Brazil and Russia).
- [Risk appetite](/wiki/risk-on-risk-off/) is high (higher growth but higher volatility tolerance).
- [Currency](/wiki/currency-pair/) valuations in emerging markets are depressed.

A rotation out of [emerging markets](/wiki/emerging-markets-fund/) often occurs when:
- Risk appetite is falling (flight to safety favors developed markets).
- [Interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate/) are rising sharply (capital flows out of emerging markets).
- [Currencies](/wiki/currency-pair/) in emerging economies are depreciating.

## Mechanically executing the rotation

A rotation between domestic and international can be executed via:

- **[ETFs](/wiki/etf/)**: Buy [international equity ETFs](/wiki/international-etf/) (e.g., VEA, VXUS) and compare them to [domestic equity ETFs](/wiki/equity-etf/) (e.g., VOO, VTI). Rotation is a simple rebalancing transaction.
- **[Mutual funds](/wiki/mutual-fund/)**: Switch allocations among [international mutual funds](/wiki/international-mutual-fund/), [emerging-market funds](/wiki/emerging-markets-fund/), and [US equity funds](/wiki/growth-fund/).
- **[Country-specific ETFs](/wiki/international-etf/)**: For finer tactical control, rotate into specific-country [ETFs](/wiki/etf/) (e.g., EWJ for Japan, EWW for Mexico).

A typical [tactical rebalancing](/wiki/tactical-rebalancing-options/) process:
1. Set a baseline allocation (e.g., 60% US domestic, 40% international).
2. Compute relative valuations and growth forecasts every quarter.
3. If international is significantly cheaper or faster-growing, overweight it (e.g., 50% US, 50% international).
4. Rebalance back to baseline when the relative advantage closes.

## Currency exposure as a rotation lever

Rotating into or out of international equities is inseparable from [currency](/wiki/currency-pair/) exposure. When you buy a Japanese [stock](/wiki/stock/), you gain both:
- Equity [price risk](/wiki/call-risk/) (Japan's [Nikkei](/wiki/nikkei-225-index/) may rise or fall).
- [Currency risk](/wiki/currency-risk/) (the [Japanese yen](/wiki/japanese-yen/) may strengthen or weaken versus the [dollar](/wiki/us-dollar/)).

If the yen strengthens 10% while the Japanese stock rises 5%, you gain 15% in dollar terms. If the yen weakens 10% while the stock rises 5%, you gain only -5% in dollar terms.

Some rotation strategies explicitly rotate on currency valuations as well as equity valuations. A [currency](/wiki/currency-pair/) that is deeply undervalued might signal a rotation opportunity, because both equity returns and future [currency appreciation](/wiki/currency-pair/) can drive gains.

## Why momentum works in rotation: [style factor](/wiki/factor-investing/)

Academic research shows that a simple rotation between international and domestic equities based on recent [momentum](/wiki/momentum-investing/) (whichever region outperformed the past 12 months) can outperform a static allocation. This is not because investors can accurately forecast future returns, but because [momentum](/wiki/momentum-investing/) is itself a [factor](/wiki/factor-investing/) — regions that have risen tend to keep rising for 6-18 months on average, before [mean reversion](/wiki/mean-reversion-investing/) pulls them back.

A disciplined [momentum-rotation strategy](/wiki/momentum-rotation-strategy/) would:
1. Calculate rolling 12-month returns for US, developed international, and [emerging markets](/wiki/emerging-markets-fund/).
2. Overweight the region with highest 12-month returns.
3. Rebalance quarterly.

This is straightforward but requires discipline to follow even when the strategy is out of favor.

## Risks and failure modes

- **Timing risk**: A rotation out of the US and into international just before a US market surge forfeits the gains.
- **[Currency](/wiki/currency-pair/) whipsaw**: You rotate into Japan just as the [yen](/wiki/japanese-yen/) begins depreciating, offsetting equity gains.
- **Convergence of valuations**: You buy international because it is cheap, but the [valuation gap](/wiki/valuation-real-options/) closes via the international market falling, not rising.
- **Overconfidence in forecasts**: [Earnings growth](/wiki/earnings-per-share/) and [interest rate](/wiki/interest-rate/) forecasts are notoriously inaccurate, especially beyond 6-12 months.
