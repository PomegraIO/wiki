*Bond-equity allocation is the foundational portfolio decision: what percentage of capital to deploy in [bonds](/wiki/bond-basics/) (fixed income) versus [equities](/wiki/equity-etf/) (stocks). This split determines portfolio [volatility](/wiki/historical-volatility/), expected [return](/wiki/return-on-equity/), and [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) structure. The optimal allocation depends on time horizon, [risk tolerance](/wiki/risk-parity-strategy/), and market valuations.*

| **Aggressive** | 0–20 | 80–100 | 7%+ | 15%+ | 25+ years |

## Why the 60/40 portfolio became the standard

For decades, financial advisors recommended a "60% equities / 40% bonds" allocation as the default balanced portfolio. This split emerged from post-war data (1945–2000) showing that:

1. **Equities** returned ~10% annually but with 16–20% annual [volatility](/wiki/historical-volatility/).
2. **Bonds** returned ~5–6% annually with 4–5% [volatility](/wiki/historical-volatility/).
3. **Correlation** between the two was near zero or slightly negative, meaning when stocks fell, bonds often held steady or rose.

A 60/40 portfolio offered ~7% expected return with ~10% volatility—a reasonable risk-return tradeoff for a 20–30 year horizon. The [diversification](/wiki/diversification/) benefit of combining uncorrelated assets reduced overall [portfolio risk](/wiki/concentration-risk/) below the weighted average of the two components.

The 60/40 became the default because it was intuitive, well-documented, and worked reliably for three decades.

## The breakdown: why 60/40 is in crisis

Since 2020, the 60/40 allocation has underperformed due to regime change:

1. **Rising rates**: As the [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/) raised [interest rates](/wiki/interest-rate/) from near-zero (2020) to 5.5% (2023), [bond prices](/wiki/bond-price-formula/) fell sharply. A 10-year [Treasury bond](/wiki/treasury-bond/) bought at 1% yield generated a -10% mark-to-market loss as yields rose to 4.5%.

2. **Higher correlations**: In recent crises (2022, 2024), stocks and bonds have moved together—both falling on inflation fears and rising rate expectations. This broke the [negative correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) assumption underlying diversification.

3. **Equity valuations**: By 2024, the S&P 500 had risen to historically high [price-to-earnings](/wiki/price-to-earnings-ratio/) multiples (19–21×), implying lower future returns and higher [downside risk](/wiki/drawdown-risk-measure/).

A 60/40 portfolio down 16% in 2022 (bonds fell 13%, equities fell 19%) is no longer viewed as reliably defensive.

## Time horizon as the primary driver

The bond-equity split should align with your investment horizon:

- **0–5 years**: Shift to 80% bonds, 20% equities. You cannot afford a 30% drawdown because you need liquidity within 5 years. [Bonds](/wiki/bond-basics/), particularly shorter-duration ones, provide stability.

- **5–15 years**: 50/50 or 60/40 is reasonable. You can tolerate intermittent downturns and recover within your time horizon.

- **15–30 years**: 30% bonds, 70% equities or more. Over three decades, [equity risk premium](/wiki/equity-risk-premium/) dominates. Annual volatility is smoothed by long compounding windows.

- **30+ years**: Consider 80–100% equities. Over 30 years, the S&P 500 has never had a negative return, and [inflation-adjusted returns](/wiki/inflation-protected-securities/) are substantial (~7% real).

This logic implies that a 30-year-old accumulating retirement savings should weight heavily toward equities, while a 70-year-old in retirement should shift toward bonds.

## Age-based rules of thumb

Simple heuristics exist for bond-equity allocation:

- **"110 minus your age"**: A 40-year-old holds 70% equities (110 - 40 = 70). This rule assumes you should reduce equity exposure as you age and approach retirement.
- **"120 minus your age"** (more aggressive): A 40-year-old holds 80% equities.
- **"100 minus your age"** (more conservative): A 40-year-old holds 60% equities.

These rules are rough starting points. Adjust for [risk tolerance](/wiki/risk-parity-strategy/), income stability, and [emergency funds](/wiki/emergency-fund-sizing/).

## Valuation-based allocation: tactical rebalancing

Sophisticated investors use valuations to adjust the split. When equities are cheap ([price-to-earnings](/wiki/price-to-earnings-ratio/) below historical average), increase equity allocation. When equities are expensive, reduce it. When [bond yields](/wiki/yield-to-maturity/) are attractive (5%+ on 10-year Treasuries), increase bond allocation.

This approach is called **tactical asset allocation** and requires:

1. Defining valuation benchmarks (e.g., "P/E below 15 = cheap").
2. Rebalancing systematically (quarterly or annually).
3. Accepting that rebalancing is a discipline, not a prediction.

A rigorous tactical allocator might shift from 60/40 to 40/60 (bonds/equities) when the S&P 500 [P/E](/wiki/price-to-earnings-ratio/) exceeds 18 and [10-year Treasury](/wiki/treasury-bond/) yields fall below 3%. This captures the intuition that expensive equities and cheap bonds imply a valuation-driven shift.

## Global diversification and bonds

Bond-equity allocation is often conducted domestically (US stocks and US bonds). Global diversification adds complexity:

- **International equities**: [Emerging-markets stocks](/wiki/emerging-markets-fund/) offer higher expected returns (8–10%) but with higher volatility and [currency risk](/wiki/currency-risk/).
- **Foreign bonds**: [Eurobonds](/wiki/eurobond/) and foreign government [bonds](/wiki/bond-basics/) offer diversification from US rates but carry [currency risk](/wiki/currency-risk/).

A sophisticated portfolio might be: 40% US equities, 15% international equities, 30% US bonds, 15% international bonds. This broadens diversification but complicates currency hedging decisions.

## [Risk parity](/wiki/risk-parity-strategy/) and equal-risk allocation

An alternative to 60/40 is **risk parity**: allocate capital so each asset class contributes equally to portfolio [volatility](/wiki/historical-volatility/). Since [bonds](/wiki/bond-basics/) are less volatile than equities, a risk-parity portfolio might hold 30% equities and 70% bonds (because 30% of the more-volatile equities contributes the same portfolio risk as 70% of less-volatile bonds).

The advantage: risk parity sidesteps the "60/40 concentration" problem where 60% of capital in equities contributes 90% of portfolio volatility. The disadvantage: risk parity historically underperformed 60/40 in bull markets, because it systematically underweights the best-performing asset.

## Rebalancing discipline and drift

Bond-equity allocation naturally drifts as equities and bonds appreciate at different rates. If you start with 60/40 and equities rally 20% while bonds are flat, you end with 65/35. Rebalancing—selling equities and buying bonds to restore 60/40—locks in gains and "buys low" when bonds underperform.

Rebalancing costs trading fees and [capital gains tax](/wiki/capital-gains-tax/), so it must be disciplined: rebalance only when the drift exceeds a threshold (e.g., when equity allocation exceeds 65% or falls below 55%), not continuously.

## Recent evolution: the rise of alternatives

Since 2008, the rise of [hedge funds](/wiki/hedge-fund/), [real estate](/wiki/real-estate-investment-trust/), [commodities](/wiki/commodity-etf/), and other alternatives has challenged the primacy of bond-equity allocation. Many endowments (Yale, Harvard) and large pension funds (CalPERS) now run "portfolio of portfolios":

- 30% public equities
- 15% private equity
- 10% real estate
- 10% commodities/inflation hedges
- 20% fixed income
- 15% alternatives (hedge funds, distressed, etc.)

This approach provides more granular [risk diversification](/wiki/diversification/) and potentially higher returns but requires access to illiquid investments and sophisticated managers. For retail investors, a simple bond-equity split remains the practical starting point.
