*An **equal weight portfolio** allocates the same percentage of capital to each holding, regardless of company size, [market capitalization](/wiki/market-capitalization/), or index weight. In an equal-weight S&P 500 index, each of 500 companies gets 0.2% of the portfolio. This contrasts with [cap-weighted indices](/wiki/cap-weighted-index/), where larger companies receive larger weights.*

<aside class="wiki-infobox">

| Aspect | Equal Weight | Cap-Weighted |
|---|---|---|
| **Weight allocation** | Same % per holding | Proportional to market cap |
| **Rebalancing** | Frequent (drift is high) | Infrequent (self-rebalancing) |
| **Size bias** | Overweight small-cap | Overweight large-cap |
| **Performance** | Outperforms large-cap in value cycles | Outperforms in tech/growth cycles |
| **Turnover** | High (frequent rebalancing) | Low |
| **Tax efficiency** | Lower (turnover-driven gains) | Higher (buy-and-hold friendly) |

</aside>

## Mechanics and construction

Equal weighting is simple in concept: divide total portfolio value by number of holdings. If a portfolio has $100,000 and 50 stocks, each stock gets $2,000 (2%). As prices change, one stock might grow to $2,500 while another drops to $1,500. The portfolio drifts out of balance. Equal weight strategies require periodic [rebalancing](/wiki/asset-rebalancing/): selling winners that grew above target weight and buying losers that fell below target weight.

This differs from [cap-weighted](/wiki/cap-weighted-index/) indices, where the largest companies (Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia) naturally receive the largest weights as they grow. A cap-weighted S&P 500 index gives ~7% weight to the top 10 companies; an equal-weight S&P 500 gives each of the top 10 companies only 0.2%.

## Rebalancing discipline and frequency

Equal weight portfolios require disciplined rebalancing. Some practitioners rebalance annually, others quarterly or semi-annually. Frequent rebalancing (monthly) is more costly (transaction fees, taxes) but keeps weights tighter. Infrequent rebalancing (annual) is cheaper but allows larger drift.

The discipline of rebalancing—selling winners and buying losers—embodies a [contrarian](/wiki/contrarian-investing/) mindset: winners have gotten expensive relative to peers, and losers may be poised to recover. This mechanical buying low and selling high can be a performance advantage, especially in [mean-reversion](/wiki/mean-reversion-investing/) environments where outperformers fade and underperformers recover.

## Equal weight versus cap weighting: performance

Historically, equal-weight indices have outperformed cap-weighted indices in certain periods, particularly in value-driven or [mean-reversion](/wiki/mean-reversion-investing/) cycles. This is because equal weighting overweights small-cap and "cheaper" names relative to cap weighting, capturing a [size factor](/wiki/size-factor/) and [value factor](/wiki/value-factor/) premium.

However, equal weighting has underperformed in recent decades when large-cap technology dominance was extreme. From 2010–2023, the cap-weighted S&P 500 returned ~13% annualized, while equal-weight S&P 500 returned ~10%, because equal weighting forced the portfolio to trim exposure to mega-cap tech winners.

## Expense ratios and liquidity

Equal-weight ETFs (like RSP for equal-weight S&P 500) typically have higher expense ratios (0.20–0.40%) than cap-weighted equivalents (0.03–0.05%) because rebalancing costs are embedded in the fund. Also, equal-weighting small stocks within an index that includes small-caps requires buying less-liquid names, which compounds costs.

Liquidity is lower for equal-weight products than for cap-weighted counterparts because the strategy requires frequent trading and smaller positions in less-liquid stocks.

## Small-cap and value factor exposure

Equal weighting creates a structural tilt toward smaller companies and value stocks. By definition, small-cap stocks make up a larger percentage of an equal-weight portfolio than they do in the actual economy. This can be a feature (if you believe small-caps are undervalued) or a bug (if you prefer to match market-cap weights).

Over very long periods, small-cap stocks have delivered higher returns than large-cap stocks, supporting the [Fama-French three-factor model](/wiki/fama-french-three-factor-model/). But small-cap returns are volatile and have underperformed large-cap for the past 15 years.

## Tax efficiency and turnover

Equal-weight portfolios generate higher tax liabilities due to frequent rebalancing. Every rebalance locks in gains on the winners sold and losses on losers bought. In a taxable account, this creates annual tax drag that reduces net-of-tax returns. [Tax-loss harvesting](/wiki/tax-loss-harvesting/) can offset some of this, but equal weighting is inherently less tax-efficient than buy-and-hold cap weighting.

In [tax-deferred accounts](/wiki/401k-plan/), this drag is irrelevant, making equal weighting more attractive.

## Psychological benefits of rebalancing

Some research suggests that the discipline of rebalancing provides a psychological anchor against [behavioral biases](/wiki/overconfidence-bias/). Forced selling of winners prevents [overconfidence](/wiki/overconfidence-in-investing/) in outperformers and forces purchase of underperformers, countering [recency bias](/wiki/recency-bias-trading/) (the tendency to buy what's been rising). This may explain some equal-weight outperformance in certain markets.

## Equal weight in different asset classes

Equal weighting is less common in [bond](/wiki/bond/) portfolios (because duration varies by maturity and credit quality), but exists in [equity](/wiki/equity-etf/) and [commodity](/wiki/commodity-etf/) ETFs. For a portfolio of bonds, an equal-dollar weighting (all $2,000 each) ignores duration risk; a duration-weighted approach is more prudent.

Equal weighting also appears in [factor-based investing](/wiki/factor-investing/) and [smart beta](/wiki/smart-beta/) strategies, where all [factor exposures](/wiki/factor-etf/) or all [dividend-paying stocks](/wiki/dividend-focused-etf/) are equal-weighted rather than cap-weighted.

## Comparison to other weighting schemes

- **Cap-weighted**: Largest companies get largest weights. Self-rebalancing. Lower turnover. Tax-efficient.
- **Equal-weight**: All holdings same weight. Manual rebalancing required. Higher turnover. Higher costs.
- **Risk-weighted or inverse-volatility**: Less volatile stocks get higher weights. Reduces portfolio volatility.
- **Fundamental weighting**: Weights based on sales, earnings, dividends, not price. Value-tilt by design.

## Practical implementation

Equal-weight indices are implemented via ETFs (RSP for equal-weight S&P 500 is the most liquid). You can also build equal-weight manually by calculating target weights, executing trades to hit them, and scheduling rebalancing periods.

For individual investors, maintaining equal weighting across 500 stocks is impractical; equal-weight ETFs or focused equal-weight portfolios (10–50 holdings) are more realistic.
