*The **accruals anomaly** describes a persistent pattern in which stocks with high accounting accruals—non-cash additions to reported earnings—deliver lower returns than stocks with high cash flows relative to reported profits. This effect sits at the intersection of accounting, market psychology, and [factor investing](/wiki/factor-investing/), revealing how investors systematically misprice earnings quality.*

## What accruals are and why they matter

Reported earnings split into two components: cash earnings (cash flow from operations) and accruals (the non-cash difference between net income and operating cash flow). A company that earns $100 million in net income but generates only $60 million in cash flow from operations has $40 million in accruals. These accruals arise from changes in working capital, depreciation, deferred revenue, and countless other items that satisfy accounting rules but do not move cash.

Accruals themselves are not sinister. They are a required part of [accrual accounting](/wiki/accrual-accounting/), which matches costs to the periods in which they are incurred rather than when cash changes hands. The problem lies in what they reveal. When a company's reported earnings are driven heavily by accruals rather than cash, those earnings are more prone to reversal. Cash does not lie; accruals can.

## The empirical pattern: high accruals, low returns

Academic research, beginning with work by Sloan in 1996, documented a striking anomaly. Portfolios of stocks with high accruals deliver returns 5–10 percentage points lower per year than portfolios of stocks with low accruals, even after controlling for [market risk](/wiki/market-risk/), firm size, and price-to-earnings multiples. This effect persists across decades, markets, and sectors. It is one of the most robust [factor investing](/wiki/factor-investing/) findings.

The mechanism is straightforward: the accruals anomaly reflects mispricing. Investors and analysts tend to extrapolate recent earnings growth too optimistically, without adequately adjusting for the accrual component. A company reporting 15% earnings growth on the strength of large accruals attracts optimistic forecasts. Months or quarters later, as those accruals reverse and cash earnings miss expectations, the stock re-rates downward. The patient investor who sold the high-accrual stock and bought the low-accrual stock captures this correction.

## Why the anomaly persists

If high accruals are an obvious red flag, why do investors still misprice them? Several factors contribute.

First, analyst consensus and investor attention cluster on reported net income, not operating cash flow. Earnings per share is broadcast in headlines and earnings calls; accruals are buried in the [cash flow statement](/wiki/cash-flow-statement/). The cognitive burden of reverse-engineering accruals from financial statements is real.

Second, managerial discretion creates ambiguity. Some accruals reflect legitimate economic events (a customer granted extended payment terms); others reflect accounting judgments that managers use to smooth reported earnings. Investors struggle to distinguish benign accruals from manipulative ones, so they apply a blunt heuristic—and the market as a whole misprices the entire category.

Third, [loss aversion](/wiki/loss-aversion/) and recency bias amplify the effect. A company that recently reported strong earnings growth (partly accrual-driven) feels "safer" to own than a company with flat cash flows. Selling winners and buying boring cash-generators requires emotional discipline.

## Operationalizing the factor

Practitioners measure accruals in several ways. The broadest approach is total accruals as a percentage of assets:

Accruals = (Net Income − Operating Cash Flow) / Total Assets

This captures all non-cash earnings. Refinements focus on discretionary accruals (those under managerial control) or working-capital changes, but the simple version works well in practice.

A [factor investing](/wiki/factor-investing/) portfolio might rank stocks by this metric and go long the lowest-quintile (lowest-accrual) stocks while shorting or ignoring the highest-quintile (highest-accrual) stocks. The resulting "quality" or "earnings-quality" tilt has delivered consistent excess returns, though with its own volatility and periods of underperformance.

## Connection to quality and value investing

The accruals anomaly is sometimes framed as a [value investing](/wiki/value-investing/) discovery, since cash-generation metrics tend to reward cheaper, less glamorous companies. It is equally a quality effect, as it highlights earnings sustainability and resilience. [Price-to-sales ratio](/wiki/price-to-sales-ratio/) and [enterprise value](/wiki/enterprise-value/) multiples implicitly reward low-accrual companies because they generate cleaner cash returns on capital.

Modern multi-factor [index funds](/wiki/index-fund/) and [factor-based ETFs](/wiki/etf/) now incorporate accruals screens, which has democratized access to the anomaly but also potentially dampened its excess return as capital flows in. [Factor decay](/wiki/factor-decay/) is a real consideration for strategies built on this signal.

## Limitations and caveats

The accruals anomaly is not bulletproof. In the presence of strong growth or secular tailwinds, high-accrual companies may outperform because they are reinvesting heavily for future cash flows. Defensive or mature sectors show cleaner accrual reversals than cyclical or high-growth industries. The anomaly also weakens during credit expansions, when accruals are genuinely reflective of operating vitality.

Additionally, gaming the metric is possible. A company that cuts capital investment or defers maintenance to boost near-term cash flow may show low accruals but declining competitive position. The factor works best as part of a broader [quality](/wiki/quality-factor/) framework, not in isolation.
