*A **solvency ratio** measures whether a company can repay its debts over time; a **liquidity ratio** measures whether it can meet obligations due within the next year. Both matter, but they answer different questions: one about survival over years, the other about survival over months.*

## Why the distinction matters

Solvency and liquidity are not the same thing. A firm can be technically solvent—owning enough assets to cover all liabilities if sold—yet face immediate [liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/) because those assets cannot be converted to cash fast enough. Conversely, a company drowning in debt may have enough operating cash flow to meet this quarter's bills, making it liquid but ultimately insolvent. Lenders and investors must track both signals because they predict different kinds of failure.

## Solvency ratios: long-term debt capacity

Solvency ratios measure the relationship between debt and assets or earnings, revealing whether a company can service and eventually retire its obligations from operating performance or asset sales.

**[Debt-to-equity ratio](/debt-to-equity-ratio/)** divides total liabilities by shareholders' equity. A ratio above 1.0 means the firm owes more than shareholders have invested; above 2.0 or 3.0 often signals high financial risk, depending on industry. An electric utility with a 2.5 ratio may be normal; a software company with the same ratio is overleveraged.

**[Interest coverage ratio](/interest-coverage-ratio/)** divides [EBITDA](/ebitda/) or operating income by annual interest expense. A ratio below 1.5 suggests the company struggles to cover interest from operations; below 1.0 means it cannot. Investment-grade bonds typically require coverage above 2.5 to 3.0.

**[Debt-to-EBITDA](/debt-to-ebitda-ratio/)** expresses total debt as a multiple of annual earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization. Ratios above 4.0 to 5.0 are considered risky; financial sponsors in a [leveraged buyout](/leveraged-buyout/) may push this to 5.0 to 6.0 in the first year, expecting operational improvement to bring it down.

These ratios ignore when the cash is due—a deliberate choice. A firm with strong earnings but $500 million due in five years is solvable through future cash flow; one with $50 million due next month is not, even if its 10-year solvency looks solid.

## Liquidity ratios: short-term payment ability

Liquidity ratios measure immediate or near-immediate capacity to convert assets to cash or deploy cash to pay bills.

**[Current ratio](/current-ratio/)** divides current assets (cash, receivables, inventory expected to convert within 12 months) by current liabilities (payables, short-term debt, accrued expenses due within 12 months). A ratio of 1.5 to 2.0 is typical for healthy industrial companies; below 1.0 signals distress.

**Quick ratio** (or acid-test ratio) is more conservative: it excludes inventory from the numerator, dividing only cash and [accounts receivable](/accounts-receivable/) by current liabilities. A quick ratio above 1.0 is reassuring; below 0.5 raises red flags.

**Cash ratio** uses only cash and equivalents, the most extreme version. Few firms maintain a cash ratio above 0.5, and most cannot—it would mean holding cash equal to half of annual payables, a waste of capital.

## The insolvent-but-liquid trap

A company with strong current assets and positive cash flow can be completely insolvent. Picture a retail chain with billions in inventory and receivables (good for current ratio) but $20 billion in long-term [debt](/debt-financing/) and only $1 billion in shareholder equity (terrible solvency). Rising interest rates or a recession that slows sales can turn inventory into dead weight and receivables into defaults, eventually starving the firm of cash. The [current ratio](/balance-sheet/) looked healthy until it did not.

Similarly, a restaurant or retailer with seasonal cash flows might show a current ratio above 2.0 just before inventory writedowns or rent renegotiations that reverse the picture.

## The solvent-but-illiquid case

Less intuitive but equally real: a stable, profitable firm can run out of cash. Suppose a manufacturing company with strong long-term earnings has all assets tied up in fixed plant and machinery (low solvency risk, high EBITDA). But it has a large bond maturity next month and receivables are slow to collect. Without access to short-term credit, it could miss the payment despite being economically viable. This is why even healthy firms maintain [revolving credit facilities](/credit-spread/)—insurance against temporary mismatches between cash inflows and outflows.

## Applying both lenses

Bankers evaluating a [loan](/loan-origination-fees/) application examine solvency ratios to size debt capacity—"Can this firm ultimately afford to pay us back?"—and liquidity ratios to gauge covenant compliance and default risk—"Will they miss a payment in the next 12 months?" A business with excellent solvency but declining liquidity is a downgrade risk. One with poor solvency but fortress liquidity might survive longer than expected, though it is ultimately doomed.

The healthiest firms show strength in both dimensions: moderate leverage, strong interest coverage, and a current ratio above 1.5. A firm weak on either axis warrants scrutiny. Solvency deteriorates slowly and is structural; liquidity can evaporate in weeks.
