## Why These Metrics Diverge

**Operating margin** follows accrual accounting strictly: it deducts all expenses that reduce profit, including [depreciation](/depreciation/) and [amortization](/amortization/) of long-lived assets—costs that are non-cash but real in economic terms. **EBITDA margin** is calculated by taking earnings before subtracting interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, then dividing by revenue. The result: EBITDA margin is always equal to or higher than operating margin, because you are not deducting D&A.

The wider the gap, the more a company's profitability story depends on D&A assumptions. A company with $100 million in revenue, $20 million in operating income, but $15 million in annual D&A would have:

- Operating margin: 20%
- EBITDA margin: 35% (($100M − COGS − OpEx + $15M D&A) ÷ $100M)

That 15-percentage-point gap is material. It tells you the company is profitable operationally, but much of that profit is theoretical—it reflects the economic benefit of past asset purchases, not cash generated by today's operations.

## When D&A Distorts the Picture

**Capital-intensive industries** experience the most dramatic distortion. A steel mill, airline, or telecom operator must maintain or replace massive fixed assets. Their D&A can easily eat 20–40% of gross profit, making operating margin look far weaker than the cash being generated.

By contrast, a software-as-a-service company or e-commerce platform has lower PP&E intensity. D&A is small relative to revenue. The gap between EBITDA margin and operating margin narrows, and both metrics point in the same direction.

Consider two companies with identical $50 million revenues and both generating $15 million in [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/):

- **Airline**: $10 million operating income, $12 million D&A → Operating margin 20%, EBITDA margin 44%
- **SaaS platform**: $12 million operating income, $2 million D&A → Operating margin 24%, EBITDA margin 28%

The airline's metrics look far worse on the operating margin line, yet both companies produce comparable cash. Using only operating margin would mislead you into thinking the SaaS business is superior. EBITDA margin reveals that the airline is not broken—it is just capital-heavy.

## The Case for Operating Margin

**Operating margin** is the stricter, more conservative measure. It reflects the true accrual profit from operations, fully accounting for the wear and tear on productive assets. Because D&A is a real economic cost—you must eventually replace those assets—it belongs in profitability math.

Operating margin also resists manipulation. A company cannot choose its D&A schedule without bound; accounting rules and auditors police it. EBITDA, by contrast, can be gamed: the phrase "adjusted EBITDA" often signals add-backs of questionable items (restructuring charges, stock-based compensation, one-time losses) that make the number prettier.

For **long-term [valuation](/intrinsic-value/)**, operating margin is often preferred because it grounds your analysis in sustainable, rule-compliant profit. When comparing two mature competitors with similar asset bases, operating margin answers the question: "Which one runs a more profitable business?"

## The Case for EBITDA Margin

**EBITDA margin** highlights operational cash generation before capital structure and accounting method choices. It is especially useful when:

- **Comparing across different [acquisition](/acquisition/) histories.** One company may have been recently built through acquisition, saddled with intangible amortization from the [purchase method](/business-combination-purchase/), while a peer grew organically. EBITDA margin strips out that accounting artifact.

- **Analyzing capital-heavy businesses in [credit analysis](/credit-rating/).** Lenders care first about cash available to service debt. EBITDA margin shows how much profit the business generates before you pay off the balance sheet.

- **Assessing operational leverage in [cyclical industries](/business-cycle/).** During a downturn, a capital-heavy business's D&A does not fall (it is fixed), so operating margin can collapse while EBITDA margin stays resilient. That resilience matters if you are trying to forecast recovery.

- **Comparing companies with very different accounting policies.** One firm may lease assets ([operating lease](/operating-lease/)); another may buy them. Leases do not hit D&A but do affect profitability. EBITDA margin smooths some of that noise.

## Real-World Example: Telecom vs. Tech

Two companies, each with $10 billion in annual revenue:

**Telecom operator:**
- Operating income: $2.0 billion
- D&A: $2.8 billion
- Operating margin: 20%
- EBITDA margin: 48%

**Cloud infrastructure company:**
- Operating income: $2.2 billion
- D&A: $0.5 billion
- Operating margin: 22%
- EBITDA margin: 27%

By operating margin alone, the cloud company looks marginally better (22% vs. 20%). But the telecom operator's EBITDA margin (48%) is far higher, signaling that its core business is highly profitable—the weakness in operating margin is purely a function of heavy ongoing [capex](/capital-flows/) and the resulting D&A.

A growth investor might prefer the cloud company's lower asset intensity. A [hedge fund](/hedge-fund/) analyst evaluating debt capacity would be impressed by the telecom's EBITDA margin and would know the company can handle servicing bonds despite a modest operating margin.

## Which Metric Should You Use?

**Use operating margin** to answer: "Is this company's core business genuinely profitable?" It is the gold standard for [valuation](/intrinsic-value/) and long-term health.

**Use EBITDA margin** to answer: "How much cash is this business generating before capital structure decisions?" It is invaluable in [leveraged buyout](/leveraged-buyout/) analysis, [credit](/credit-rating/) work, and cross-industry comparisons where asset bases differ wildly.

**Use both** in real analysis. Plot them side by side. If they diverge sharply, investigate why. A widening gap signals that D&A is rising—often because the company is expanding its asset base, or because intangible amortization from an acquisition is kicking in. A narrowing gap might suggest the company is relying less on fixed assets going forward, or that older assets are finally fully depreciated.
