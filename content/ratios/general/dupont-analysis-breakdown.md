*A company that delivers a 20% [return on equity](/return-on-equity/) might be a pricing powerhouse with fat margins, or it might be a lean operator that wrings profit from minimal assets, or it might be a leveraged business riding on borrowed money. **DuPont analysis** breaks ROE into three factors—profit margin, asset turnover, and equity multiplier—so you see which levers are driving the return.*

## The Basic Formula and Its Logic

[Return on equity](/return-on-equity/) is defined as:

**ROE = Net Income / Shareholders' Equity**

This single ratio tells you what profit the company earned for each dollar shareholders invested. But it hides the sources of that return. DuPont analysis expands ROE into three components:

**ROE = (Net Income / Sales) × (Sales / Assets) × (Assets / Equity)**

Notice that Sales, Assets, and Net Income appear in numerators and denominators that cancel out, leaving just Net Income / Equity. So the formula is mathematically identical to the basic ROE definition. What it reveals is *how* the company achieves ROE.

The three factors are:

1. **Net Income / Sales = Profit Margin.** This is how much revenue sticks as profit. A company with 10% margins earns $0.10 profit per dollar of sales.

2. **Sales / Assets = Asset Turnover.** This is how efficiently the company converts assets into revenue. A company with asset turnover of 2.0 generates $2 in sales for every $1 of assets on the [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/).

3. **Assets / Equity = Equity Multiplier.** This is the ratio of total assets to shareholders' equity. It tells you how much of the company is financed by [debt](/debt-financing/) vs. equity. An equity multiplier of 2.0 means assets are twice equity, implying the company is half-financed by debt.

## A Worked Example

Imagine two companies, both reporting ROE of 15%.

**Company A:**
- Net Income: $10 million
- Sales: $100 million
- Total Assets: $50 million
- Shareholders' Equity: $25 million

Profit Margin = $10M / $100M = 10%
Asset Turnover = $100M / $50M = 2.0
Equity Multiplier = $50M / $25M = 2.0
ROE = 10% × 2.0 × 2.0 = 40%

(Note: I adjusted the numbers for clarity; actual ROE = $10M / $25M = 40%.)

**Company B:**
- Net Income: $7.5 million
- Sales: $50 million
- Total Assets: $50 million
- Shareholders' Equity: $25 million

Profit Margin = $7.5M / $50M = 15%
Asset Turnover = $50M / $50M = 1.0
Equity Multiplier = $50M / $25M = 2.0
ROE = 15% × 1.0 × 2.0 = 30%

Company A earns 40% ROE by combining a modest 10% margin with efficient asset turnover and moderate leverage. Company B earns 30% ROE by running fat margins (15%) but turning assets over slowly and using the same leverage. Neither approach is inherently better, but they reveal different business models.

## Profit Margin: The Pricing and Cost Lever

The profit margin component reflects the company's pricing power and cost control. A company with high profit margins (like Apple or Coca-Cola) extracts more profit from each dollar of sales. This could be because:

- It has brand pricing power and can charge premium prices.
- It has economies of scale and low manufacturing costs.
- It operates in a high-margin industry (software, pharmaceuticals) rather than a low-margin one (groceries, commodities).

A declining profit margin year-over-year signals that either the company is cutting prices to stay competitive, or costs are rising faster than revenue. Both are red flags, even if assets or leverage improve.

## Asset Turnover: The Efficiency Lever

Asset turnover reflects how hard the company works its assets. A high-asset-turnover business (like a fast-fashion retailer or quick-service restaurant) generates strong sales from a lean balance sheet. A low-asset-turnover business (like a utility, mining company, or semiconductor manufacturer) requires massive capital investment to generate each dollar of revenue.

Asset turnover is not a quality measure—some industries naturally require more assets than others. But within an industry, rising asset turnover often signals operational improvement: the company is extracting more revenue from the same or fewer assets. Falling asset turnover can indicate that new capital projects have not yet generated revenue, or that the company is becoming less efficient.

## Equity Multiplier: The Leverage Lever

The equity multiplier is where [leverage](/leverage-ratio-forex/) enters. A multiplier of 1.0 means the company has no debt—assets equal equity. A multiplier of 3.0 means assets are three times equity, implying two-thirds of assets are debt-financed.

Higher leverage mechanically boosts ROE, assuming the company earns more on its assets than it pays in [interest](/interest-rate/). This is called "financial leverage," and it can be attractive: a company borrowing at 5% and earning 15% on assets spreads a profitable gap. But leverage also amplifies losses and increases the risk of financial distress.

A rising ROE that is driven entirely by increasing leverage—while profit margins and asset turnover stagnate—is often a warning sign. The company is borrowing more to prop up returns, not improving underlying business quality.

## Using DuPont to Diagnose Changes

The real power of DuPont analysis emerges when a company's ROE changes year-over-year.

Suppose company earnings rise and ROE ticks up from 12% to 15%. Where did the gain come from?

- If profit margin rose from 6% to 8%, but asset turnover and multiplier stayed flat, the company improved pricing or cost control.
- If asset turnover rose from 1.5 to 1.75, but margins and multiplier stayed flat, the company is using assets more efficiently.
- If the equity multiplier rose from 1.6 to 1.8, but margins and turnover stayed flat, the company borrowed more money to boost returns.

Each tells a different story about what the company actually improved. A margin-driven gain usually reflects stronger competitive positioning. A turnover gain reflects operational discipline. A leverage-driven gain reflects a financial engineering choice that may or may not be sustainable.

## A Critical Caveat: Leverage Masks Risk

Many analysts and investors focus on DuPont analysis to celebrate high ROE without scrutinizing the leverage component. A company can post 25% ROE by using extreme debt and thin margins, but it will be fragile. A sudden recession that cuts margins, or a [interest rate](/interest-rate/) spike that raises [cost of debt](/cost-of-debt/), will rapidly destroy returns and endanger solvency.

Conversely, a company with 15% ROE driven by fat margins and low leverage is often a safer, more durable business. The DuPont breakdown allows you to see this distinction. Raw ROE alone does not.

## Extensions and Variations

Some analysts extend DuPont to five or more factors. For example, they decompose "Net Income / Sales" into "EBIT / Sales" (operating margin) and "Net Income / EBIT" (tax and [interest](/interest-rate/) burden). This adds granularity but also complexity.

The three-factor version is most useful for quick diagnosis. The five-factor version helps if you need to isolate tax policy, [interest expense](/interest-rate/), or other specific leverage effects.
