*The **net debt to EBITDA ratio** subtracts a company's cash and equivalents from gross debt, then divides by operating earnings before depreciation and taxes. This adjustment is crucial: a company drowning in gross debt may be perfectly safe if it holds cash sufficient to pay creditors off tomorrow. By netting, the ratio captures the true financial burden—what lenders actually care about. It is a favourite of private equity, credit analysts, and institutional investors because it reflects genuine leverage independent of accounting choices.*

## Why net debt matters more than gross debt

A company with £100 million in debt looks risky until you learn it holds £90 million in cash. Its true obligation is only £10 million—a very different story. This is the insight behind netting: gross debt overstates financial burden when the company holds substantial cash.

The formula is simple:

**Net Debt = Total Debt − Cash and Cash Equivalents**

**Net Debt to EBITDA = Net Debt ÷ EBITDA**

Why [EBITDA](/ebitda/) in the denominator? Because it represents operating earnings before the distortions of [depreciation](/depreciation/), [amortisation](/amortization/), interest, and taxes. For a capital-intensive business or one with high [goodwill](/goodwill/) write-downs, EBITDA is closer to the true cash the business generates than net income. Two firms with identical EBIT may report very different net income due to depreciation schedules; EBITDA neutralises that noise.

## Gross debt versus net debt in practice

Consider two retailers:

- **Retailer A:** £200M debt, £5M cash, £100M EBITDA. Gross debt-to-EBITDA = 2.0. Net debt-to-EBITDA = 1.95.
- **Retailer B:** £200M debt, £80M cash, £100M EBITDA. Gross debt-to-EBITDA = 2.0. Net debt-to-EBITDA = 1.2.

Both show identical gross leverage. But Retailer B, holding substantial cash, could reduce its net debt by 40% in a week if needed. Its true leverage is much lower. A lender would charge Retailer B a lower interest rate and extend more credit.

This matters especially for cyclical or seasonal businesses. A retailer may hold large cash balances before the holiday season, then spend it down post-season. Gross debt-to-EBITDA ignores that cycle; net debt-to-EBITDA captures it. Similarly, a company undergoing [acquisition](/acquisition/) integration often accumulates cash before deploying it, temporarily inflating its net-debt ratio, then uses the cash for integration, normalising the ratio.

## Cash: a proxy for financial flexibility

Holding cash is optionality. A highly leveraged company with large cash reserves can weather a revenue shock, make emergency purchases, or refinance maturing debt without panic. A company with identical leverage but minimal cash is brittle; any adverse event forces it to cut capex, shrink the workforce, or negotiate with creditors.

The net debt ratio reveals that flexibility. It is the lender's way of asking: "If things get bad, does this company have dry powder?" A firm with net debt-to-EBITDA of 3.0 but cash equal to three years of interest coverage is safer than one at 2.0 with minimal cash reserves.

This is also why [mergers and acquisitions](/merger/) often trigger covenant tests on net leverage rather than gross. An acquiring company might issue debt to finance the purchase, temporarily spiking gross debt. But if it funded part of the deal from the target's cash or existing cash reserves, net debt rises less; the covenant language typically allows for this "debt reduction from target cash."

## The private equity lens

Private equity firms focus obsessively on net debt-to-EBITDA because it directly affects returns. A deal financed with £100M net debt generating £20M EBITDA has net leverage of 5.0—very high and probably unsustainable. The same company with £50M net debt has leverage of 2.5—more manageable. The PE investor models how quickly operating improvements and debt paydown can bring leverage down to a "exit" level (typically 3.0–4.0) where the company can be refinanced or sold.

The ratio also affects leverage ratios imposed by lenders in leveraged deals. A typical covenant might require: "Maintain net debt-to-EBITDA below 4.0." As EBITDA grows (from operational improvement), leverage automatically improves, creating an incentive to invest in the business. If leverage climbs toward the covenant ceiling, the borrower must cut debt or risk a covenant breach.

## EBITDA and its quirks

[EBITDA](/ebitda/) is not flawless. It ignores working capital needs, capital expenditures, and taxes—all of which are real cash drains. A company with £50M EBITDA might actually generate only £20M in free cash flow after capex and working-capital investment. Using EBITDA as the denominator can overstate the company's leverage capacity.

Conservative analysts adjust EBITDA for one-time items. A company that benefited from a one-time asset sale, gain on [fair value](/fair-value/) adjustment, or legal settlement will see inflated EBITDA that may not recur. Subtracting those adjustments gives "normalised" or "run-rate" EBITDA, which is more predictive of future leverage capacity.

Some lenders even use EBIT instead of EBITDA (resulting in "net debt-to-EBIT"), a stricter test that accounts for the real cash cost of depreciation and amortisation. This is common in asset-intensive industries where depreciation is substantial.

## Reading the ratio across industries

Capital-intensive industries naturally carry higher net debt-to-EBITDA because they require large upfront investment and generate returns over many years. A utility might comfortably operate at 4.0–5.0; a technology company at 1.0–2.0. The industries have different risk profiles.

Investment-grade companies typically target net debt-to-EBITDA below 3.0. High-yield (non-investment-grade) firms often run at 4.0–6.0. Above 6.0 signals severe financial stress or a company in turnaround mode. Companies with net leverage below 1.0 are unusually strong—they could pay down debt quickly if needed.

## The cash adjustment: what counts?

"Cash" in the numerator is typically restricted to liquid, immediately available funds: cash on the balance sheet and short-term investments. Restricted cash (held for specific purposes and not available for debt repayment) is sometimes excluded. Operating lease obligations and other off-balance-sheet liabilities are sometimes added back to debt by conservative analysts, though the ratio name does not capture these adjustments.

Different lenders and analysts may define "cash" slightly differently, which is why when comparing net debt ratios across sources, reading the footnotes is essential. Some exclude short-term debt; others include it. Small definitional shifts can move the ratio meaningfully.

## Rising net debt and operational deterioration

When net debt-to-EBITDA climbs, it signals one of three things: debt is rising, EBITDA is falling, or cash is being deployed. The first two are concerning; the third depends on context. A company using cash to buy back shares while debt grows is a red flag (leverage increases, no operational benefit). A company using cash to fund organic growth or capital expenditure is investing; if the capex raises EBITDA, leverage eventually improves.

Lenders watch the ratio closely. If it trends upward over two or three quarters, they begin tightening covenants, demanding financial updates more frequently, or signalling that future debt availability will be limited. A company that hits covenant limits on net debt-to-EBITDA cannot raise more money and must focus on deleveraging—cutting costs, growing earnings, or selling assets.
