*The **interval measure** answers a blunt operational question: if your company's revenue stopped tomorrow, how many days could you pay your staff, suppliers, and rent from liquid assets alone? It translates raw cash and receivables into a tangible runway—a number of days—that carries intuitive weight for founders and creditors alike.*

## The formula and its simplicity

The interval measure is refreshingly straightforward:

**Interval Measure = (Cash + Accounts Receivable) ÷ Daily Operating Expenses**

Numerator: the same "quick" liquid assets used in the [acid-test ratio](/acid-test-ratio/)—cash and receivables, excluding inventory and prepaid expenses.

Denominator: the daily cost of running the business—wages, utilities, materials, rent, everything that keeps the operation alive—divided by the number of days in a period (usually 365).

The output is pure days. A manufacturing firm with $2 million in liquid assets and daily operating costs of $25,000 has an interval of 80 days. If sales ground to a halt, the firm could sustain itself for roughly 11 weeks before running out of cash.

## Why days matter more than ratios

A [current ratio](/current-ratio/) of 1.2 is abstract. An interval measure of 90 days is concrete. Executives, boards, and investors grasp days instantly: "We have three months of runway." This clarity makes interval measure especially valuable in startup contexts, where cash burn is a constant metric, and in distressed situations, where lenders need to know how quickly a firm will fail.

For venture capital or [private equity](/private-equity-fund/) backed firms, the interval measure becomes part of the operational playbook. "We need 18 months of runway" is a more precise target than "maintain a quick ratio above 0.9." It forces conversations about burn rate and breakeven milestones.

## What counts as a daily operating expense?

Here lies the first challenge: defining the denominator correctly. Operating expenses typically include:

- Payroll and benefits
- Cost of materials and production
- Rent, utilities, and facility costs
- Professional services and insurance
- Marketing and customer acquisition

What is *usually excluded*:

- Capital expenditures (new equipment or buildings)
- Debt repayment (principal, not just interest)
- Taxes (often estimated or deferred)

The distinction matters. A biotech firm might spend $10 million a year on equipment and clinical trials—massive cash outflows—but a narrow operating-expense definition could misrepresent its true cash burn. A more honest interval measure might include capex and debt service, producing a harsher runway estimate.

There is no single "correct" definition. Different stakeholders—management, banks, investors—may use different versions of the denominator. Transparency is essential: always state what you included and excluded.

## Timing and the receivables trap

The interval measure includes [accounts receivable](/accounts-receivable/) as liquid. This assumes customers pay on schedule. But if customers slip and payments arrive 60 days late, and your interval is 80 days, you may face a genuine cash crisis despite appearing liquid on paper.

A manufacturer that sells with net-90 terms (customers pay 90 days later) might show an interval of 120 days but actually face a 30-day cash crunch if a major customer delays payment. The ratio assumes receivables convert to cash at their stated collection rate; reality often lags.

Prudent practitioners sometimes apply a haircut: counting only 70% or 80% of receivables as liquid, or excluding invoices older than 60 days. A startup selling to Fortune 500 customers might count receivables fully; one selling to startups might discount them heavily.

## The inventory and capex blind spots

The interval measure excludes inventory because it is not immediately liquid. But in some industries—retail, agriculture, real estate—inventory *is* a crucial safety valve. A grocery chain can liquidate inventory for 70% of cost within days; a software firm's servers are nearly worthless in a fire sale.

Similarly, capex is often omitted, which can distort the picture. A tech firm with low operating expenses but heavy quarterly server and R&D spending might show a false sense of security. The "true" burn rate, including capex, might be much harsher.

The best practice: compute interval measure multiple ways. One using only wages and utilities; another including capex and debt service. Compare them side by side.

## Interval measure across industries

A retail chain might target 45–60 days of operating expense coverage. This reflects frequent inventory turnover, regular supplier payments, and thin margins—if sales slip, costs stay relatively fixed and reserves deplete quickly.

A software company with high fixed costs and low inventory might comfortably operate with 30 days of interval because cash inflows from subscriptions are predictable and steady.

A bank faces a unique scenario: its "daily operating expenses" are interest paid to depositors plus operating costs, but its inflows are loan repayments and interest received. The interval concept morphs into something closer to [liquidity coverage ratio](/liquidity-coverage-ratio/) and regulatory capital requirements.

## Interval measure in crisis planning

During the 2008 financial crisis, firms that tracked interval measure knew exactly when the lights would go out if credit markets froze. The metric became a central focus of board discussions and vendor negotiations.

Boards now increasingly require interval measure disclosure as part of forward guidance. "We maintain a minimum 90-day operating reserve" is a standard safety benchmark. If interval drops below 60 days, it triggers a conversation about cost cuts, asset sales, or emergency financing.

## The cash-cycle and payables dimension

The interval measure considers only cash inflows (current receivables) and ignores [accounts payable](/accounts-payable/) and accrued expenses. A firm might have 60 days of operating runway but also owe suppliers in 45 days, creating a genuine squeeze.

More sophisticated analysts compute *adjusted* interval measures that subtract payable obligations from liquid assets. This is closer to true available liquidity. A firm with $5 million in liquid assets, $3 million due to suppliers in 30 days, and daily operating costs of $50,000 has an available interval of closer to 40 days, not 100.

## Limitations and complementary metrics

The interval measure is a snapshot, not a forecast. It does not account for seasonal revenue swings, one-time expenses, or market disruptions. A ski resort might show a 120-day interval in July and face a liquidity crisis in November.

It also assumes constant burn. In reality, firms can cut costs quickly: pause hiring, defer discretionary spending, renegotiate contracts. A firm with a 45-day runway might survive 90 days if management cuts aggressively.

The interval measure is most useful alongside [cash flow statement](/cash-flow-statement/) trends, [free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) growth, and revenue trajectory. A deteriorating interval combined with shrinking revenue and rising unit costs is a genuine red flag. A stable or improving interval in a growing firm is a sign of discipline.
