*The **inventory to net working capital ratio** measures how much of a company's net working capital is locked in inventory. A high ratio suggests the firm has overcommitted to stock, tying up cash that could deploy elsewhere; a low ratio indicates more liquid, flexible working capital. It bridges working capital quality with inventory management strategy.*

## Why the ratio matters for cash flow

Every dollar of inventory sitting in a warehouse is a dollar not earning interest, not paying a supplier bill, and not available for opportunity. The [cash conversion cycle](/cash-conversion-cycle/) depends partly on how quickly inventory turns into sales; a high inventory-to-working-capital ratio is often a symptom of slow turnover. A manufacturing firm might naturally run a 55% ratio; a retailer in the grip of a clearance sale might spike to 80%. The ratio becomes a canary for operational health: rapid growth that bloats inventory is often masked by rising sales figures until cash runs dry.

## Calculation and interpretation

The formula is straightforward. Take net working capital — [current assets](/balance-sheet/) minus [current liabilities](/balance-sheet/) — and divide total inventory by that figure. Multiply by 100 for a percentage.

$$\text{Ratio} = \frac{\text{Inventory}}{\text{Current Assets} - \text{Current Liabilities}} \times 100$$

A ratio of 40% means two-fifths of the firm's cushion sits in boxes. A ratio of 120% signals that inventory alone exceeds the entire working capital pool; the firm is operating on borrowed time, relying on receivables turnover and fresh supplier credit to stay solvent. This is common in distressed retail but unsustainable long-term.

The ratio also behaves differently by season. A toy company's ratio explodes in August (pre-holiday stock) and shrinks by January. Seasonal peaks are normal; persistent elevation is not.

## Comparing across industries

Retail and manufacturing naturally carry higher ratios. A grocery chain, which restocks multiple times per week, might comfortably operate at 50–65%. A specialty chemicals manufacturer with long lead times might push 70%. A software-as-a-service company, by contrast, carries almost no inventory and typically scores below 10%.

Cross-industry comparison is nearly meaningless. The ratio is most valuable when tracked against a company's own historical trend or against direct competitors operating in the same supply ecosystem. An unexpected jump — say, from 45% to 65% over a quarter — warrants investigation: Did demand collapse? Is the supply chain miscalibrated? Has the firm front-loaded inventory ahead of anticipated shortage?

## Inventory obsolescence and hidden risk

The ratio assumes inventory value is accurate. In practice, it often overstates liquidity. Write-downs for slow-moving, damaged, or obsolete stock are frequent; the balance sheet often lags reality. A company reporting $50 million in inventory might actually sell only $40 million at cost; the extra $10 million is deadweight. The ratio picks up total inventory, not sellable inventory, so high ratios can mask worse-than-apparent liquidity strain.

This is why creditors and investors often layer in inventory [turnover](/inventory-turnover/) analysis: a high ratio combined with low turnover (slow-moving goods) is more worrying than a high ratio with fast turnover (goods move quickly despite large absolute balances).

## Relationship to short-term solvency

The ratio anchors in the broader picture of [liquidity risk](/liquidity-risk/). A firm with negative working capital — where current liabilities exceed current assets — runs an undefined or inverted ratio. That's the real crisis. But a firm with adequate positive working capital can still be dangerously illiquid if that cushion is almost entirely inventory. The [current ratio](/current-ratio/) and [quick ratio](/quick-ratio/) strip out inventory; this ratio shows why that matters.

## Strategic implications

Reducing the ratio is often a competitive advantage. Just-in-time manufacturing — pioneered by Toyota — aims to keep inventory at a fraction of working capital. Leaner firms turn cash faster, finance growth from operations, and weather downturns better. The trade-off: rigid supply chains, higher per-unit logistics cost, and vulnerability to supply shocks. But for firms with stable, predictable demand, low ratios are a sign of operational discipline.

Conversely, a high ratio can signal smart positioning: a retailer loading up on merchandise before a price increase or in anticipation of surge demand is choosing to temporarily sacrifice liquidity for margin. The ratio alone cannot judge strategy; context always matters.
