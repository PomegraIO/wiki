*The **acid-test ratio** measures how many dollars of cash and near-cash assets a company can deploy to cover each dollar of current liabilities, excluding inventory and prepaid expenses. Also called the quick ratio's stricter cousin, it answers the bluntest question: if your firm faced a sudden cash crisis tomorrow, could you pay your bills due within the year?*

## The formula and its strictness

The acid-test ratio strips your balance sheet down to its most defensive assets:

**Acid-Test Ratio = (Cash + Accounts Receivable) ÷ Current Liabilities**

Unlike the broader [current ratio](/current-ratio/), which includes inventory and prepaid expenses, the acid test assumes inventory might take weeks or months to convert to cash—and prepaid expenses cannot be converted at all. It answers a harder question: if you sold nothing tomorrow, would you survive?

A ratio of 1.0 means the company has one dollar of liquid assets for each dollar of short-term debt. Ratios below 0.5 suggest the firm would struggle to pay bills if revenue dried up for 30 days. Ratios above 1.5 indicate substantial cash coverage, though excessively high ratios sometimes signal idle capital that could be deployed more productively.

## Why prepaid expenses get excluded

Prepaid rent, insurance premiums, and advance payments are balance-sheet assets—they represent real economic value. But they cannot be sold or pledged to creditors. If a retailer has paid rent six months in advance and faces a cash crunch, that prepaid expense provides no liquidity. The acid test refuses this accounting fiction.

Similarly, [inventory](/inventory-turnover/) sits somewhere between cash and dead weight. A grocery store's shelves represent real assets, but converting them to cash takes time—and might require discount sales if the urgency is acute. The acid test sidesteps this uncertainty by leaving inventory out entirely.

## Accounts receivable: the catch

The acid test *includes* accounts receivable—money owed by customers. This assumption works well if customers reliably pay within 30 days. But it can mislead. A software firm with a customer facing bankruptcy might hold a $500,000 receivable that never gets paid. A startup selling on 90-day terms faces longer collection cycles than the ratio assumes.

Credit quality matters. Receivables from Fortune 500 customers are more liquid than those from distressed small businesses. Some analysts apply a haircut—treating only 70% or 80% of receivables as truly liquid—or exclude the oldest invoices entirely.

## When the acid test matters most

Creditors and short-term lenders scrutinize this ratio before extending credit. A covenant in a [loan agreement](/loan-origination-fees/) might require the borrower to maintain an acid-test ratio above 0.8 at all times. If the ratio falls below that threshold, the lender can demand repayment.

Sectors matter enormously. A [bank](/federal-reserve/) or insurance company faces regulatory acid-test-like requirements because their entire model depends on payouts—they live or die by liquidity. A capital-intensive manufacturing firm might run an acid test of 0.4 and still be solvent, because it relies on long-term financing and has months before supplier payments come due. A grocery chain, conversely, needs a higher ratio because inventory spoils and competitive pressures force narrow margins.

## Acid test vs. quick ratio

The quick ratio includes inventory alongside cash and receivables. The acid test excludes inventory. This single difference makes the acid test the more pessimistic measure—and the more trustworthy one in a downturn. If you want to know whether a company can weather a genuine crisis, the acid test cuts through the fog.

Some analysts compute both: a quick ratio above 1.0 suggests healthy liquidity under normal conditions, while an acid test above 0.6–0.8 indicates the firm can survive if sales stop entirely. Together, they sketch a fuller picture than either alone.

## The cash-heavy exception

Some businesses—software platforms, payment processors, certain fintech firms—accumulate substantial cash because they collect customer payments before paying suppliers. Their acid test might register 3.0 or higher. This is not a warning sign; it reflects the natural cash-generation cycle of the business model. [Free cash flow](/free-cash-flow/) and timing of inflows and outflows often tell a clearer story than a raw ratio.

Similarly, firms raising capital or preparing for acquisition often hold excess cash, temporarily inflating the acid test. Context—earnings, cash burn, market position—always trumps the ratio itself.

## Limitations and the broader picture

The acid test is a snapshot. It tells you the liquidity position on a single day—usually quarter-end or year-end—but not whether that position is stable or deteriorating. A firm might have a healthy acid test today and face a cash crunch three months later if major customers go quiet or supplies become expensive.

It also ignores access to credit. A company with a 0.6 acid test but strong [cash flow](/cash-flow-statement/) and a committed bank credit line might be far safer than a competitor with a 1.2 ratio but shrinking revenue and no backup financing.

The acid test is best used alongside [earnings quality](/earnings-quality/), [cash conversion cycle](/cash-conversion-cycle/) metrics, and operating trends. A deteriorating acid test combined with rising costs and slowing collections is a genuine warning. A stable or rising acid test in a growing, profitable firm is often a sign of strength.
