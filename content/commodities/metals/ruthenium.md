*Ruthenium is a silvery-grey transition metal belonging to the platinum group, valued for its hardness and corrosion resistance in applications ranging from computer storage to emerging clean-energy technologies. Unlike more famous cousins like platinum or palladium, ruthenium trades almost entirely through opaque dealer networks with minimal exchange infrastructure, making it illiquid and volatile despite meaningful industrial demand.*

## Why ruthenium matters to data and green energy

Ruthenium entered industrial consciousness through the hard disk boom. Starting in the late 2000s, manufacturers began coating disk platters with ruthenium layers—thin films of just a few nanometres—to reduce corrosion and enable higher data density. A single 4-terabyte hard drive contains about 5 grams of ruthenium, seemingly minor until multiplied across millions of units shipped annually. The transition to cloud computing, data centres, and edge servers kept ruthenium demand steady even as consumer PC shipments fell.

More recently, green-hydrogen electrolysers have emerged as a potential major outlet. Alkaline and proton-exchange-membrane (PEM) electrolysers use ruthenium-based catalysts to split water into hydrogen and oxygen efficiently. As governments and utilities invest in hydrogen infrastructure—whether for industrial heat, synthetic fuels, or long-term energy storage—ruthenium demand could accelerate significantly. That prospect has attracted speculative interest and price volatility.

The metal is also used in electrical contacts, thermocouples, and specialty alloys, but these applications are mature and relatively stable. Ruthenium's growth story hinges on the hydrogen transition materialising, which is uncertain and policy-dependent.

## The dealer market and price opacity

Unlike platinum or palladium, which trade on major commodity exchanges and through standardised futures markets, ruthenium exists almost entirely in bilateral dealer networks. A producer (typically a refiner recovering ruthenium from nickel or platinum ore) negotiates prices with industrial buyers—disk manufacturers, electrolyser makers, chemical companies—through specialist brokers. There is no central [limit-order book](/limit-order/), no real-time transparent price feed, and minimal published quotes.

This opacity serves dealers well. A buyer purchasing 100 kilograms may be quoted one price; a seller hedging exposure via a future contract on NYMEX faces much wider [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/) because volume is thin and liquidity is sporadic. The NYMEX ruthenium futures contract, though listed, rarely sees more than a handful of contracts traded per day. For an industrial user needing to lock in prices six months forward, hedging is difficult and costly.

Dealers maintain inventories and profit from the spread between the price they pay refineries and the price they charge end-users. Information asymmetry is high: only dealers and major participants see real transaction prices. Published indices (such as those from Johnson Matthey) are derived from dealer surveys and can lag or misrepresent actual market clearing prices.

## Industrial demand and supply constraints

South Africa produces roughly 95% of the world's ruthenium, as a byproduct of platinum and nickel mining. Russia is the secondary source. This extreme concentration means that any disruption to South African mining—labour strikes, electricity cuts, or political instability—ripples instantly through ruthenium markets. The 2021–2022 energy crisis in South Africa caused platinum and ruthenium prices to spike 40–50% as mining shutdowns restricted supply.

Because ruthenium is a byproduct, its supply is inelastic. A mining company cannot simply increase ruthenium output; it can only extract more platinum or nickel, hoping that ruthenium byproduct increases proportionally. If platinum demand falls, ruthenium supply may fall sharply despite strong ruthenium-specific demand—a structural mismatch that creates scarcity and price spikes.

Industrial demand is growing but still modest. All hard disk coating globally consumes roughly 70–80 tonnes of ruthenium annually (at recent prices). Hydrogen electrolyser demand is emerging and could add 30–50 tonnes per year if the sector scales aggressively, but that scaling depends on government subsidies, carbon pricing, and technology breakthroughs that remain uncertain.

## Price volatility and trader risks

Ruthenium prices have swung from £20 per ounce to over £300 per ounce in the past decade, largely driven by South African supply shocks and sentiment shifts about the hydrogen economy. Because there is no deep [futures](/futures-contract/) market and limited spot liquidity, prices can gap sharply on rumours or geopolitical events.

Traders attempting to speculate on ruthenium face substantial hurdles. Position sizes must be modest to avoid facing execution prices far worse than the last dealer quote. Holding inventory incurs carrying costs (storage, insurance, financing) that erode returns if prices stagnate. Exit liquidity can vanish if a dealer's inventory becomes full or if industrial demand softens unexpectedly.

Industrial users seeking to hedge ruthenium exposure are often forced into long-term fixed-price contracts with dealers, accepting whatever premium the dealer demands for bearing the risk. Smaller manufacturers may be shut out of hedging entirely and accept spot [currency risk](/currency-risk/) and commodity-price risk as a cost of doing business.

## Hydrogen hype and downside risks

The hydrogen narrative around ruthenium is real but fragile. If governments scale hydrogen infrastructure rapidly, ruthenium demand could triple. Conversely, if hydrogen policy falters—subsidies are withdrawn, electrolyser technology shifts to less ruthenium-intensive catalysts, or hydrogen demand remains niche—prices could collapse back to historical levels.

This binary outcome creates speculative appeal for some traders but represents uncompensated risk for industrial users. A disk manufacturer cannot predict government hydrogen policy and cannot reasonably hedge against a hydrogen collapse.
