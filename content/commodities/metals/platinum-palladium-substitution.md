## How Catalytic Converters Use These Metals

Catalytic converters sit in a vehicle's exhaust stream and use precious metals to catalyze chemical reactions—converting harmful pollutants (carbon monoxide, hydrocarbons, nitrogen oxides) into inert gases before they reach the atmosphere. Platinum and palladium are the primary active catalysts, chosen for their durability at high heat and their stability across the range of exhaust temperatures an engine produces.

The role each metal plays depends on engine type and exhaust composition. Petrol engines run "lean" at highway speeds, exposing catalysts to oxidizing conditions where palladium excels. Diesel engines run hotter in particulate-filter aftertreatment stages, favoring platinum's thermal stability and resistance to sulfur poisoning. A typical modern petrol car might use 2–5 grams of palladium and 1–3 grams of platinum per converter; a diesel car reverses that ratio.

## The Price-Loading Trade-off

The choice of metal blend is not fixed in engineering stone. Automakers, under margin pressure and exposed to volatile precious metals prices, adjust catalytic converter recipes in real time. When palladium prices soar relative to platinum, engineers redesign the coating to use less palladium and more platinum while maintaining emission performance—a process known as "substitution."

This substitution works because both metals are catalytically competent; they differ in efficiency and cost, not absolute capability. A converter that once was 60% palladium–40% platinum might become 40% palladium–60% platinum if the price ratio shifts sharply. The car still meets emissions standards; the automaker's cost per unit drops.

The switching point typically occurs when the [price spread](/bid-ask-spread/) widens to 2× or greater. If palladium costs three times as much as platinum per gram, a carmaker designing a new model will skew the recipe aggressively toward platinum. When prices converge, designs revert.

## Why Supply Matters: Byproduct Concentrations

A critical asymmetry: platinum is mined as a primary product, with its own dedicated geology and extraction routes. Palladium, by contrast, is a byproduct of [nickel](/nickel/) and copper mining, meaning its supply is tethered to mining decisions made for other metals.

When nickel prices slump, nickel producers cut output, and palladium byproduct supply falls alongside it. Automakers cannot instantly redesign converters, so they compete for scarce palladium in spot markets, driving prices further up and triggering the substitution lever harder. Platinum supply, meanwhile, depends on South African geological reserves and mining labour—a different vulnerability.

In the 2010s, South African platinum strikes repeatedly constrained supply at moments when palladium prices were already climbing due to weak nickel output. The combination pushed some automakers to extreme substitution, using 90% palladium in some models to secure stable supply arrangements.

## Demand Forecasting and the Substitution Ceiling

The substitution mechanism matters for commodity investors and central banks holding metal reserves, because it decouples auto production volume from precious metals demand. A 5% increase in auto sales does not automatically translate to a 5% increase in platinum or palladium use.

If a surge in car production coincides with high palladium prices, carmakers shift the blend heavily toward platinum. Net result: platinum demand spikes, palladium grows less than production volume would suggest. Conversely, if a recession crushes car volumes but platinum supply tightens simultaneously, palladium substitution into converters might sustain demand even as unit volumes fall.

For pricing, substitution creates a demand elasticity that dampens extreme price movements. Once palladium becomes too costly relative to platinum, demand for additional palladium erodes quickly; converters already in production can absorb shocks through reformulation. This elasticity has historically kept palladium prices below the wild spikes seen in [crude oil](/crude-oil/) or agricultural commodities.

## Recent Trends and the EV Transition

The rise of electric vehicles introduces a new variable: EVs require no catalytic converters. As EV sales penetration climbs, total converter demand falls, reducing the pool of catalytic jobs to fill—and thus reducing the scope for platinum–palladium substitution.

In the 2020s, analysts initially expected this to crater precious metals demand. In practice, the shift has been more gradual. Internal-combustion engine production has fallen, but the absolute volume of new cars remains vast, supporting converter demand at historical levels in absolute tonnes even as the percentage of the fleet declines. The substitution lever remains active, if applied to a shrinking denominator.

Regulatory changes also matter. Stricter emissions standards in Europe and China have pushed some automakers to use higher total metal loading per converter—both to meet tighter NO<sub>x</sub> limits and to extend catalyst life in dirtier air. When standards tighten, substitution debates shift; the goal becomes meeting emissions targets first, then optimizing cost within that constraint.
