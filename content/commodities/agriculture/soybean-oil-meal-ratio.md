*The **soybean oil-to-meal ratio** compares the price of soybean oil to soybean meal, the two main products from crushing soybeans. Traders use this ratio to gauge whether crushing (processing) soybeans into oil and meal is profitable, and to anticipate shifts in demand for each product — a key signal in [commodity](/copper/) markets.*

## What Crushing Produces

A bushel of soybeans (60 pounds) yields roughly:
- 11 pounds of soybean oil (18% of the bushel)
- 48 pounds of soybean meal (80% of the bushel)

When a processor (a soybean-crushing mill) buys soybeans and crushes them, those two products come out. The processor sells them separately, and the combined revenue must exceed the cost of beans plus processing to be profitable. The soybean oil-to-meal ratio doesn't directly calculate that profit, but it signals whether the market conditions favor one product over the other — and crushers listen.

## How the Ratio Works

The ratio is straightforward: **Oil Price ÷ Meal Price**. Both prices are quoted per pound on [commodity](/copper/) markets.

**Example:** If soybean oil trades at $0.60 per pound and meal trades at $4.00 per pound, the ratio is 0.60 ÷ 4.00 = **0.15**.

A ratio of 0.15 means oil is 15% of the price of meal. Because a bushel yields roughly 11 pounds of oil and 48 pounds of meal, a low ratio (oil cheap relative to meal) means the bushel's value is concentrated in the meal. A high ratio (oil expensive) means more value flows to oil.

## What the Ratio Tells Traders

### High Ratio: Oil Is Expensive

When the soybean oil-to-meal ratio is high (e.g., 0.16–0.18), soybean oil is trading at a premium relative to meal. This typically happens when:

- **Biodiesel demand surges:** Soybean oil is the feedstock for biodiesel, so government mandates or energy-price spikes drive oil prices up. Biofuel blending requirements in the US, Europe, and Brazil can push oil demand quickly.
- **Food-use demand is strong:** High oil prices reflect strong demand for cooking oil, margarine, and processed foods, signaling a robust economy and food consumption.
- **Meal supply is abundant:** If there's a large soybean crop, meal prices can be depressed (oversupply), widening the ratio.

For crushers, a high ratio is **good news**. The oil they produce is worth a lot, so even if meal is cheap, the overall crush margin is healthy. Crushers ramp up processing, buying more soybeans and crushing them aggressively.

### Low Ratio: Oil Is Cheap

When the ratio is low (e.g., 0.08–0.12), soybean oil is trading cheaply relative to meal. This typically happens when:

- **Biodiesel demand is weak:** Energy prices have fallen, or biofuel mandates are eased. Oil demand drops, prices decline.
- **Livestock-feed demand is robust:** Meal, the protein-rich animal feed, is in high demand (e.g., dairy herds expanding, poultry flocks large). Meal prices stay firm or rise.
- **Oil oversupply:** A large oil harvest or weak biofuel seasons leave crushers with excess oil inventory.

For crushers, a low ratio is **challenging**. The oil they produce is cheap, so they depend on a strong meal price to justify crushing. If meal is also weak, crush margins compress. Crushers may slow or halt processing.

## The Crush Spread: The Real Profitability Measure

While the oil-to-meal ratio is a quick market signal, the actual profitability of crushing is the **crush spread** — sometimes called the **soy crush margin**.

**Crush Spread = (Oil Price × 11) + (Meal Price × 48) − Soybean Price − Processing Costs**

This formula reflects the revenue from crushing one bushel minus the cost of the bushel and the mill's operating cost.

The ratio doesn't include the soybean input cost, but it does move in lockstep with it. When the ratio is wide (oil expensive), processors are optimistic and bid for soybeans, driving up soybean prices. When the ratio narrows (oil cheap), processors are pessimistic and reduce bids. In effect, the ratio is a leading indicator for crush profitability.

## Seasonality and Crush Cycles

The soybean oil-to-meal ratio is not random; it shifts predictably with the soybean crop cycle and global demand patterns.

**New-crop season (post-harvest, September–December in the Northern Hemisphere):** After the US harvest, large volumes of soybeans flow to processors. Oil and meal supplies are both abundant. Historically, oil tends to outpace meal in price decline, so the ratio often narrows (widens). Crushers compete for volume, and crush margins compress.

**Mid-crop (December–March):** As stocks normalize and demand patterns emerge, the ratio stabilizes. If a biodiesel season is underway in the Northern Hemisphere winter (e.g., mandated winter biofuel blending in the EU), oil demand is strong, and the ratio can widen.

**South American crop (March–May):** Brazilian soybeans arrive in global markets. If they're abundant, oil supplies rise, and the ratio can narrow again.

**Energy and agricultural demand cycles:** A stronger oil price environment (e.g., OPEC cuts, geopolitical risk) can support biodiesel demand year-round, keeping the oil-to-meal ratio elevated.

## Why Traders Watch It

### Processor Planning

If a crusher sees the ratio climbing (oil getting expensive), they know the margin on that barrel of oil is widening. They'll place bids for soybeans, crush more, and sell oil aggressively to capture the premium. If the ratio drops suddenly, they slow or stop crushing.

### Hedging Strategies

Commodity traders use the ratio to structure [hedges](/derivatives-hedging/). A processor holding soybeans might:
- Sell a futures contract on soybean oil (locking in the oil price)
- Sell a futures contract on soybean meal (locking in the meal price)
- Lock in the crush margin via this synthetic spread

If the ratio moves against them (oil drops relative to meal), the hedge protects their operating margin.

### Demand Forecasting

A sustained, elevated oil-to-meal ratio signals strong biodiesel and food-oil demand — a sign of economic health or energy-policy stimulus. A persistent low ratio signals weak oil demand and potential oversupply, a bearish signal for biofuel stocks and oil-seed producers.

## Regional Variations

The ratio varies across regions based on local production, trade flows, and demand.

- **United States:** The ratio follows biodiesel mandates (Renewable Fuel Standard). Government-mandated biodiesel use rises in winter, supporting oil demand.
- **Brazil:** The ratio reflects South American harvest timing and global export patterns. Brazil is the world's largest soybean exporter.
- **EU:** High biofuel blending mandates keep the ratio elevated; European crushers rely on strong oil demand.
- **China:** A large importer of US and Brazilian soybeans, China's soy crush capacity and meal demand heavily influence global ratios.

## Practical Trading Example

Suppose a crusher observes:
- Soybean oil at $0.65/lb
- Soybean meal at $4.10/lb
- Soybean futures at $12.50/bu (equivalent to ~$0.208/lb for a 60-lb bushel)
- Processing cost: ~$0.50/bu

Crush spread = (0.65 × 11) + (4.10 × 48) − 12.50 − 0.50 = 7.15 + 196.8 − 12.50 − 0.50 = **$190.95 per bushel**

That's profitable (assuming overhead is covered). The crusher feels emboldened to buy soybeans at $12.50 and crush them, selling oil and meal at those prices.

A week later, oil crashes to $0.50/lb (biodiesel-mandate news disappoints). The new ratio is 0.50 ÷ 4.10 = 0.122 (down from 0.158). The crush spread is now:
(0.50 × 11) + (4.10 × 48) − 12.50 − 0.50 = 5.50 + 196.8 − 12.50 − 0.50 = **$189.30**

Margins are tighter. The crusher still profits, but with less cushion. If oil drops further, crush spreads can turn negative, and processing halts.
