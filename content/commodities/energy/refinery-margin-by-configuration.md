## The Three Refinery Archetypes

Refineries do not all process crude oil the same way. The three broad types—topping, hydroskimming, and coking—differ in capital intensity, complexity, and margin capture. A **topping refinery** contains just atmospheric distillation: it heats crude, separates it into broad cuts (naphtha, kerosene, diesel, fuel oil), and ships them out. It is cheap to build, easy to operate, and generates thin margins because those cuts are still far from finished products.

A **hydroskimming refinery** adds hydrotreating and catalytic reforming. It removes sulfur and nitrogen, improves octane ratings, and bleeds off light ends. More of the crude becomes sellable gasoline and middle distillates. Investment is moderate; margins expand.

A **coking refinery** goes further. It cracks the heaviest, least valuable vacuum gas oil into lighter hydrocarbons, captures additional gasoline and diesel, and recovers delayed coking units that turn thick residuum into petroleum coke and more liquid fuels. Capital and operating costs jump sharply. So do revenues.

## Margin Width by Type

The [crack spread](/crack-spread/)—the difference between crude input cost and refined product selling prices—varies by configuration because the product slate differs.

A topping refinery might yield 45% gasoline, 30% diesel, 15% fuel oil, 10% loss. If crude costs $70/bbl and gasoline averages $80, diesel $78, fuel oil $65, the revenue is roughly (0.45 × $80) + (0.30 × $78) + (0.15 × $65) = $36 + $23.40 + $9.75 = $69.15/bbl. After operating costs of $4–6/bbl, the gross margin is $2–5/bbl—thin.

A hydroskimming plant might yield 58% gasoline, 28% diesel, 8% fuel oil, 6% loss. Same crude at $70, but tighter, higher-value cuts. Revenue approaches $74–76/bbl; margins run $5–10/bbl.

A coking refinery might yield 50% gasoline, 25% diesel, 8% jet, 12% coke, 5% loss. The sale of high-margin jet fuel and coke—and fewer heavy residuals—lifts revenue to $78–82/bbl. After operating costs of $6–8/bbl, margins hit $8–16/bbl or higher, especially when crack spreads widen.

These are illustrative. Real numbers depend on crude quality, product prices, plant reliability, and energy costs.

## Crude Type Matters as Much as Configuration

A heavy, sour crude (high sulfur, high density) is cheaper than light sweet crude. But it yields more residue per barrel—more of the unwanted heavy fraction. A topping refinery cannot process it profitably; the heavy fuel oil it produces has minimal value. A hydroskimming refinery struggles. Only a coking refinery, or a dedicated heavy-crude hydrotreater, can convert that cheap feedstock into profitable gasoline and diesel.

Conversely, light sweet crude is expensive but almost any refinery can profit from it. A topping plant can run it with decent margins. The spread between input cost and output value narrows less.

Location and logistics also matter. A refinery near oil-heavy regions often buys discounted, heavy crude. Its configuration must justify the lower input cost through high-value extraction. Refineries in developed markets with tight fuel specs and high product prices can afford to run lighter, simpler configurations.

## How Margins Compress and Expand

Refinery margins move with product price cycles. In a boom, gasoline and diesel premiums over crude widen, and coking refineries compound the gain—their large gasoline and diesel slate captures the full crack spread plus bonus liquids revenue. In a slump, spreads collapse, and simple plants suffer faster because they cannot pivot to alternative, higher-margin cuts.

Seasonal shifts also ping margins. Winter demand for heating oil lifts middle-distillate cracks; summer lifts gasoline. A hydroskimming plant with balanced gasoline–diesel output has better margin stability than a topping plant tied to fuel oil.

Unexpected refinery downtime—maintenance, accident, environmental—tightens regional refining supply, widens cracks, and temporarily boosts remaining plants' margins. A major outage in a region of simple refineries can favor distant coking units.

Technological edge matters too. A coking refinery with modern hydrotreating and fluid catalytic cracking can squeeze more high-octane gasoline from a barrel than an older one running the same configuration. Its margins reflect superior engineering.

## Margin Pressure: Why Complexity Does Not Always Win

Despite higher unit margins, complex refineries face higher fixed costs, more capital at risk, and greater downtime exposure. A single fouled heat exchanger shuts a coking unit for weeks; a topping refinery stumbles less catastrophically. Over a full cycle, the complex plant must operate at high rates and capture sustained cracks to justify its investment.

Environmental regulations also hit complex plants harder. Modern coking units must capture and process coke-plant vapor, install advanced sulfur recovery, meet strict emissions—all capital. A topping refinery's simpler footprint carries lower compliance burden.

Market structure also constrains margins. If global refining capacity is excess and crude prices collapse faster than product prices, even coking refineries see margins pinched. Conversely, tight capacity—few coking units in a region—can sustain premium margins for years. Geopolitics (sanctions, wars, port closures) can isolate refineries, isolating their margins upward or downward.
