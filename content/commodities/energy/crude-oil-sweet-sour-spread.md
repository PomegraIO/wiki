*The **sweet–sour crude spread** is the price difference between light-sweet crude (low sulfur, low density) and heavy-sour crude (high sulfur, high density). Light-sweet sells at a significant premium because refiners can convert it into high-value products with less capital-intensive equipment, while sour crude requires expensive desulfurization units and yields lower margins.*

## Why light-sweet commands a premium

Crude oil's value to a refinery depends on two properties: **sulfur content** and **density** (API gravity).

Sulfur is poison. When crude burns in engines or heats buildings, sulfur dioxide (SO₂) is released, contributing to acid rain and air pollution. Governments worldwide restrict fuel sulfur content—typical diesel contains under 10 ppm sulfur (parts per million) in developed markets. A refiner processing 1,000 barrels per day of crude with 3% sulfur must invest in a hydro-desulfurization unit (costing 200–400 million dollars for major refineries) to strip out sulfur and convert it into elemental sulfur or hydrogen sulfide. This unit runs continuously, consumes hydrogen and energy, and requires skilled staff. Operating cost: 2–5 dollars per barrel of crude processed.

A light-sweet crude with 0.4% sulfur enters the same refinery and skips or bypasses much of that desulfurization infrastructure. The refiner saves 2–5 dollars per barrel in opex, and the crude requires less specialized equipment to begin with.

**API gravity** (density relative to water) affects product yield. Light crude (high API, over 35) is easier to fractionate into gasoline, jet fuel, and diesel—higher-value products. Heavy crude (low API, under 25) is thicker, more viscid, and requires more heating and processing to yield the same products; much of it becomes heavy fuel oil (bunker fuel), a low-margin commodity. A refinery can earn 25–30 dollars per barrel margin on light crude (turning it into gasoline and jet) but only 5–10 dollars per barrel on heavy crude (much of which becomes fuel oil).

Combining both factors: light-sweet crude can be processed cheaply and yields high-margin products. Heavy-sour crude needs expensive infrastructure and yields low-margin heavy fuel oil. The price difference reflects this fundamental refinery economics.

## The spread in practice

In normal market conditions (ample crude supply, steady refinery runs), the sweet–sour spread hovers at 5–10 dollars per barrel. Brent crude (benchmark light-sweet, 0.36% sulfur, 38 API) might trade at 85 dollars per barrel while Maya crude (benchmark heavy-sour, 3.3% sulfur, 21 API) trades at 75–80 dollars per barrel. The 5–10 dollar gap represents the refinery margin differential.

The spread **widens** when:
- Refineries run at high capacity utilization. Every barrel processed generates margin; refiners bid aggressively for light-sweet crude to maximize it.
- Gasoline and jet fuel prices are strong relative to fuel oil. Light-sweet yields more of these, so the premium expands.
- Heavy-sour crude is abundant and hard to place. Producers of sour crude (Russia, Mexico, Canada, Venezuela) may flood the market; refineries with desulfurization capacity discount it to attract buyers.

The spread **narrows** when:
- Refinery utilization falls (seasonal maintenance, recession, lockdowns). Processing margin weakens across the board; the sweet premium erodes because fewer refineries are bidding aggressively.
- Fuel oil demand surges. During winters with high heating oil demand or in shipping when bunker fuel demand peaks, the high fuel oil yield from sour crude becomes more valuable; the spread compresses.
- Heavy-sour crude is scarce (e.g., sanctions on Venezuela or Russia). Refiners with desulfurization units must pay up for sour crude; the spread narrows.

Historically (2010–2019), the spread averaged 5–7 dollars. In 2020 (pandemic), spreads collapsed below 2 dollars as refinery runs cratered. In 2021–2022, as demand rebounded and refinery maintenance windows coincided with high energy prices, spreads sometimes exceeded 15 dollars.

## Application: refiner margin hedging

Traders and refinery operators use the sweet–sour spread as a proxy for refining margin. A typical "crack spread" (see commodity derivatives) involves buying crude oil futures and selling gasoline and fuel oil futures to lock in the processing margin. The sweet–sour spread is a simplified version: it tracks the economic advantage of processing light-sweet versus sour.

A refiner with a large sour-crude contract wants to hedge the risk that prices fall, squeezing margin. The refiner might:
1. Buy futures or swaps on light-sweet crude (e.g., Brent)
2. Sell futures on heavy-sour crude (e.g., Maya) for a future delivery
3. Lock in the spread

If the spread widens (Brent rises faster than Maya, or Maya falls faster), the refiner benefits—the locked-in economic advantage of processing sour crude increases. Conversely, if the spread narrows, the hedged position offsets losses.

Traders also exploit geographical sweet–sour spreads. If light-sweet crude in the Atlantic Basin (Brent) is trading at a 12 dollar premium over sour in the Gulf (Maya), but transport costs are 1.50 dollars, a trader can buy cheap sour, ship it east, and profit from the price difference and its own processing advantage or resale premium.

## Regional variation and grade selection

Different regions produce and prefer different grades:

- **West Africa and North Sea**: Light-sweet, prized for direct processing into gasoline
- **US Gulf, Canada**: Mix of light-sweet (Texas light) and sour (Canadian heavy bitumen), depending on field
- **Middle East**: Wide range: light-sweet from Saudi Arabia (Arab Light), sour from Kuwait and Iraq
- **Latin America**: Heavy-sour dominates (Mexico Maya, Venezuela Merey) because oil reserves are dense, degraded, or from heavy reservoirs

A refinery's product slate and hydro-treater capacity determine which grades it prefers. A simple refinery without desulfurization must buy light-sweet. A complex refinery with multiple hydro-treaters can profitably process heavy-sour, widening the effective spread it can monetize.

## Sulfur regulations and future trends

Stringent sulfur caps (International Maritime Organization 0.5% sulfur limit in shipping fuel from 2020; potential future tightening of road diesel) increase demand for light-sweet crude and expand refinery desulfurization workload. This may support the sweet–sour spread over the long term.

However, transition to electric vehicles and lower transportation fuel demand could compress refining margins overall, reducing the spread's width.
