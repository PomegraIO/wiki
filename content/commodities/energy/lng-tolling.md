*An **LNG tolling** arrangement is a liquefaction service contract where a shipper (the tollee) delivers natural gas to an LNG terminal and pays a fixed fee per unit of capacity to convert it to liquid form for transport and export. Rather than owning the liquefaction plant, the tollee rents processing capacity and assumes the commodity and transportation risk, while the tolling operator (usually a terminal owner) provides the infrastructure and collects a steady margin regardless of gas or LNG prices.*

## Tolling vs. project LNG ownership

The global LNG market has evolved two main contractual models: **project LNG** and **tolling LNG**. In a project LNG arrangement (the traditional export model), the liquefaction operator or consortium owns the gas, contracts for supply, and sells the finished LNG to buyers under long-term purchase agreements. The operator captures the full margin between upstream gas cost and LNG sale price, but also absorbs all commodity risk and market downside.

Tolling inverts this structure. The tollee (typically a producer, trader, or consortium of suppliers) owns the gas at every stage. It delivers feedstock to the tolling operator's terminal, pays a capacity charge per unit liquefied, and receives the equivalent amount of LNG (minus a small shrinkage allowance). The tolling operator never owns the commodity; it merely processes it in exchange for a steady fee. This model transfers commodity risk to the tollee and provides the terminal operator with a more predictable, less volatile revenue stream.

## Why tolling attracts producers and traders

Tolling contracts have proliferated in recent years, particularly for producers with gas stranded far from established LNG hubs and traders looking to arbitrage regional price differences. 

For a smaller or regional producer, tolling avoids the massive capital burden of building a dedicated liquefaction plant. Instead of investing billions in a greenfield terminal, a producer can negotiate tolling rights at an existing facility and commercialize gas with far less balance-sheet strain. This is especially valuable in emerging LNG regions where terminal capacity is scarce or where a single country's gas volume cannot support a full-scale project LNG venture on its own.

Traders and aggregators use tolling to monetize geographically fragmented gas supplies. A trader might buy low-cost gas from one region, arrange tolling at a conveniently located terminal, and sell the LNG into a higher-priced market. The trader captures the price spread (minus the tolling fee) without ever owning liquefaction infrastructure.

For terminal operators, tolling revenue is attractive because it is largely decoupled from LNG spot prices. A downswing in LNG markets does not reduce tolling fees; they are usually fixed in the contract. This stability attracts investors and lenders and allows operators to extract value even during periods of weak LNG pricing.

## Tolling fee structures and benchmarking

Tolling fees vary widely based on terminal technology, location, and market conditions. Fees are typically quoted in two ways: per million BTU of gas input ($/MMBtu) or per tonne of LNG output ($/tonne). A common range is $2–5 per million BTU, though state-of-the-art, high-efficiency terminals may offer lower rates, and remote or constrained terminals may command premium fees.

Fees often include components for:
- **Liquefaction processing** (the core service)
- **Regasification buffer** (fuel to run the plant)
- **LNG conditioning and storage** (brief holding before export)

Some tolling contracts also specify **capacity guarantees**—minimum throughput the operator commits to meet, with penalties or credits for underperformance. Others tie fees to a **utilization rate**: a tollee pays a higher per-unit fee if it uses less than the minimum contracted capacity, creating an incentive to maximize throughput.

## Commodity and operational risk

Although tolling shifts commodity risk from operator to tollee, it does not eliminate other hazards. A tollee still faces:

- **Gas quality issues**: If feedstock contains excessive sulfur or other impurities, the terminal may reject it or charge additional conditioning fees. The tollee must ensure its gas meets terminal specifications.
- **Force majeure and downtime**: If the terminal shuts for maintenance or suffers equipment failure, the tollee cannot liquefy gas for weeks or months. Contracts typically specify force majeure clauses and sometimes include compensation or penalty-free termination windows if downtime exceeds certain thresholds.
- **Shrinkage and losses**: LNG terminals consume a fraction of the gas (typically 3–5 percent) as fuel for refrigeration compressors. The tollee absorbs this loss; it receives LNG equivalent to only 95–97 percent of feedstock delivered.
- **Price transport risk**: The tollee must arrange shipping from the tolling terminal to its end market. LNG shipping costs fluctuate, and a sudden spike in freight rates can erase the profitability of the arbitrage.

The tolling operator's primary risk is capital recovery: if the tollee defaults or market conditions render the tolling contract uneconomical, the operator may face underutilized capacity and weakened cash flow. This is why tolling contracts typically span 10–15 years and include "take-or-pay" or minimum throughput guarantees to ensure stable revenue.

## Regional tolling markets and growth

Tolling is most developed in established LNG hubs where spare capacity exists. The Australia east coast (particularly in Queensland) has tolling arrangements, as do some European regasification terminals that reverse-flow LNG back through liquefaction units. The Middle East has also expanded tolling availability as mega-terminal operators seek to maximize utilization.

Emerging LNG regions—such as East Africa or some parts of Southeast Asia—view tolling as a way to fast-track commercialization without waiting for a full project finance cycle. A producer can start exporting LNG within a few years by accessing tolling capacity at a nearby hub, rather than spending a decade developing greenfield infrastructure.
