*The **energy complex** comprises crude oil, [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/), heating oil, and refined gasoline products. These commodities exhibit high historical [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/), moving together through supply disruptions, refining constraints, and demand cycles, though the strength of that correlation shifts with market structure and regional dynamics.*

All four products share downstream demand from power generation, transportation, and industrial activity. All depend on the same production infrastructure. A hurricane shutting down [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) extraction in the Gulf of Mexico simultaneously reduces [oil](/wiki/crude-oil/), heating oil, and gasoline supplies. A polar vortex driving up [heating oil](/wiki/heating-oil/) demand in winter also boosts [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) for residential heating. Yet the correlations are neither perfect nor constant; refining bottlenecks, regional storage imbalances, and seasonal patterns create trading opportunities within the complex.

## The energy complex as a linked system

The energy complex operates as a hydrocarbon supply chain. [Crude oil](/wiki/crude-oil/) is the primary input; refineries process it into heating oil, gasoline, and other products. [Natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) is extracted separately but faces overlapping demand (power generation) and sometimes overlapping production infrastructure (associated gas from oil wells).

**Crude oil** sets the baseline. A 10% surge in [WTI](/wiki/wti-crude/) or [Brent](/wiki/brent-crude/) typically triggers increases in heating oil and gasoline. A $10/barrel move affects refinery [margins](/wiki/operating-margin/)—the spread between [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) input cost and refined product output price—often pushing heating oil and gasoline in the same direction.

**Natural gas** is less tightly coupled. It has its own supply sources (shale gas, LNG imports), independent demand drivers (power generation volatility, winter heating spikes), and storage dynamics. A very cold winter boosts both [heating oil](/wiki/heating-oil/) and [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) because both fuel home heating. But a power plant switching from [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) to [coal](/wiki/coal/) reduces [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) demand without affecting [crude oil](/wiki/crude-oil/) or heating oil.

## Historical correlations: strength and variation

Academic studies and trader experience report that [WTI](/wiki/wti-crude/) and [heating oil](/wiki/heating-oil/) historically correlate at +0.75 to +0.85—very strong. [WTI](/wiki/wti-crude/) and [gasoline](/wiki/gasoline/) correlate similarly. The [crude oil](/wiki/crude-oil/)-[natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) correlation is weaker, typically +0.40 to +0.60, and much more variable.

**Period-dependent shifts**: During the 2008 financial crisis, all energy commodities crashed together, [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) jumping to +0.95. During the 2020 COVID lockdown, [crude oil](/wiki/crude-oil/) collapsed, but [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) held up better because of strong summer cooling demand, driving correlation down briefly to +0.30.

**Seasonal patterns**: Winter months see tighter [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/)-heating oil correlation because [heating oil](/wiki/heating-oil/) demand is explicit and synchronized with [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) refining cycles. Summer months show stronger [gasoline](/wiki/gasoline/) correlation as driving season drives both [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) demand and [gasoline](/wiki/gasoline/) production.

## What drives correlation: shared shocks

**Geopolitical supply disruptions**: Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 sent [crude oil](/wiki/crude-oil/), heating oil, and gasoline upward together, as Western buyers feared Russian [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) sanctions would constrain global supply. [Natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) spiked even harder because European buyers suddenly viewed it as scarce (LNG supplies redirecting away from Europe). [Correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) between crude and [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) rose sharply to +0.80+.

**Refinery outages**: A major refinery going offline reduces heating oil and gasoline supply simultaneously, pushing both up relative to [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/). [Crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) may actually fall slightly because the refinery shutdown means less [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) will be needed, loosening supply. This creates a *negative* [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) spike between [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) and refined products—a rare pattern that traders exploit.

**Demand cycles**: Recessions reduce all energy demand equally, so [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/), heating oil, and [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) fall together. Economic booms drive all up together. These demand-side shocks create [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) in the +0.70–+0.85 range.

**Storage and inventory**: When [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) storage fills up (indicating weak demand or oversupply), [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) prices fall. Downstream, if heating oil [storage](/wiki/commodity-storage-costs/) is also full, heating oil falls with it—high [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/). But if heating oil [storage](/wiki/commodity-storage-costs/) is lean (tight supply), heating oil may not fall as much, breaking the [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/).

## Regional disconnects and arbitrage

[Correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) is weaker when regional factors dominate:

**North American vs. global**: US [crude oil](/wiki/crude-oil/) ([WTI](/wiki/wti-crude/)) is mostly priced regionally due to an export ban (lifted in 2015, but regional trading dynamics persist). [Brent](/wiki/brent-crude/), the global benchmark, prices in London and reflects world supply. If US shale production is abundant, [WTI](/wiki/wti-crude/) may fall while [Brent](/wiki/brent-crude/) stays firm. US heating oil and [gasoline](/wiki/gasoline/) track [WTI](/wiki/wti-crude/) more closely than they track [Brent](/wiki/brent-crude/), widening the [WTI](/wiki/wti-crude/)-[Brent](/wiki/brent-crude/) spread but keeping [WTI](/wiki/wti-crude/)-refined products in sync.

**Natural gas hubs**: [Henry Hub](/wiki/henry-hub/) in Louisiana sets US prices, but regional distribution hubs (Chicago, New York) sometimes decouple based on local storage and pipeline congestion. A pipeline bottleneck in New England pushes [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) higher there relative to [Henry Hub](/wiki/henry-hub/), breaking the broader energy [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/).

**LNG dynamics**: When US [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) is exported as LNG to Asia or Europe, it effectively trades at a higher price than [Henry Hub](/wiki/henry-hub/). Global LNG [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) with [crude oil](/wiki/crude-oil/) is tighter because both ship globally and both compete for end-use demand (power generation). Domestic [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) shows weaker [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/).

## Seasonal patterns in the complex

**Winter**: [Heating oil](/wiki/heating-oil/) demand spikes first, often 4–6 weeks before the coldest months. [Natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) spikes as well, but less predictably—a mild winter kills heating demand, a severe winter cuts [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) production (cold weather shuts wells). [Gasoline](/wiki/gasoline/) stays relatively flat in winter. The [heating oil](/wiki/heating-oil/)-[natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) often strengthens in the approach to winter, then weakens mid-winter if temperatures are mild.

**Summer**: [Gasoline](/wiki/gasoline/) drives complex sentiment. Driving season demand boosts both [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) and [gasoline](/wiki/gasoline/). [Natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) usage for heating drops, but power generation (air conditioning) lifts it again. [Heating oil](/wiki/heating-oil/) is dormant. Overall [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) in summer leans [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/)-[gasoline](/wiki/gasoline/) over [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/).

## Trading the energy complex: convergence and divergence

**Convergence trades**: Traders exploit temporary correlation breaks. If [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) rises 5% but heating oil rises only 2%, the heating oil-[crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) spread has widened. A trader might buy heating oil and short [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/], betting the spread narrows back to historical norms—a "crack spread" trade.

**Divergence trades**: Some traders position for decoupling. If a [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) glut hits while [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) tightens, the [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/)-[natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) might break, and traders long [crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) and short [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) profit.

**Rolling risk**: [Energy complex](/wiki/commodity-futures-rolling/) [futures](/wiki/futures-contract/) contracts roll on fixed schedules. [Crude](/wiki/crude-oil/) rolls monthly; heating oil and [gasoline](/wiki/gasoline/) roll monthly; [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) rolls monthly but can spike on unexpected weather. During roll periods, [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) among near-month contracts typically tightens (all rolling at once), then may decouple between near-month and deferred months.

## Risk management in the complex

Portfolio managers holding [natural gas](/wiki/natural-gas/) and [crude oil](/wiki/crude-oil/) together expect [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) to drift from +0.40 to +0.70 depending on market structure. Using a constant [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) assumption for [value at risk](/wiki/value-at-risk/) (VaR) or [portfolio optimization](/wiki/asset-allocation/) can lead to underestimated [volatility](/wiki/historical-volatility/) in stress periods (when [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) rises sharply). Most sophisticated energy traders use **dynamic [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) models** that adjust for seasonal regime and recent realized [volatility](/wiki/historical-volatility/).
