*The **stock market** is the network of exchanges, brokers, and market makers through which [shares](/stock/) of [public companies](/public-company/) are bought and sold. It is not one place, but a system: [stock exchanges](/stock-exchange/) (NYSE, Nasdaq, LSE, etc.) are the venues; [brokers](/broker/) are the intermediaries; investors and institutions are the participants. The [stock](/stock/) market is summarized daily by a handful of indices ([S&P 500](/alpha/), Dow Jones, Nasdaq), and it is closely watched because it reflects investor expectations about future corporate profits and economic growth. Most importantly: the [stock](/stock/) market is not the economy. A rising [stock](/stock/) market does not mean the economy is healthy, and a falling one does not mean it is broken.*

## What "the market did today" really means

When you hear "the stock market was up 2% today," what exactly is being measured?

Most commonly, it is a reference to one of three major indices:

**The S&P 500** is a market-capitalization weighted average of 500 large-cap US companies. It is the most widely cited summary of "the market" for professional investors. It includes household names (Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Tesla) and many others. About 80% of the US [stock](/stock/) market's total value is in the S&P 500.

**The Dow Jones Industrial Average** (the "Dow") is a price-weighted average of 30 large-cap US companies, often called the "blue chips." It is the oldest and most famous index, and it is arguably the worst of the three as a market summary (it is price-weighted, giving more weight to expensive [stocks](/stock/), and only covers 30 companies). But it remains the headline number in many financial news reports.

**The Nasdaq 100** or **Nasdaq Composite** tracks the Nasdaq exchange, which has a heavy technology bias (because tech companies choose to list there). It is more volatile than the S&P 500 and often moves differently. A day when tech [stocks](/stock/) soar but the rest of the market lags will show a strong Nasdaq and a weak S&P 500.

When journalists say "the market was up 2%," they usually mean the S&P 500 (or sometimes the Dow). If you own a [diversified](/diversification/) index fund tracking the S&P 500, and the index is up 2%, your fund is roughly up 2% (minus a tiny bit for the fund's expenses).

## The stock market vs. the economy

This is the most important distinction in finance: **the [stock](/stock/) market is not the economy.**

The [stock](/stock/) market is the value of the [stocks](/stock/) of [public companies](/public-company/). The economy is the production of goods and services, employment, incomes, and living standards.

These can move in opposite directions:

- In the early stages of a [recession](/recession/), [stocks](/stock/) often fall before unemployment rises. The market is forward-looking; it prices in expected future pain before the pain arrives in the data.
- In the early recovery from a [recession](/recession/), [stocks](/stock/) often rise sharply while unemployment is still high. The market is betting on future job growth.
- A strong [economy](/recession/) with rising wages and employment can still see [stocks](/stock/) fall if interest rates rise sharply (raising the discount rate applied to future profits).
- A weak [economy](/recession/) can see [stocks](/stock/) rise if the outlook for profits and [interest rates](/interest-rate/) improves.

During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the US unemployment rate spiked to 14% (the worst since the Great Depression), but the S&P 500 fell only 34% and recovered fully within months. The [stock](/stock/) market bottomed when the outlook was darkest (April 2020) but recovered as investors priced in vaccines and stimulus. The [economy](/recession/) took much longer to fully recover.

The reason is simple: [stocks](/stock/) represent a claim on future corporate profits. Investors value them based on expected future earnings and the [discount rate](/interest-rate/) applied to those earnings. The health of the [economy](/recession/) matters, but it is only one input.

## Who participates in the stock market

The [stock](/stock/) market comprises many different players:

**Retail investors** are you and me — individuals saving for retirement, investing in [index funds](/index-fund/) and individual [stocks](/stock/). Collectively, retail investors control a significant portion of the [stock](/stock/) market, though their influence on day-to-day price movements is smaller than institutional investors'.

**Institutional investors** include mutual funds, [pension funds](/mutual-fund/), [endowments](/hedge-fund/), and insurance companies. They manage trillions of dollars and are often buy-and-hold, rebalancing annually. They dominate volume and have enormous influence on prices.

**Hedge funds** are pooled investments for wealthy and institutional clients, often taking concentrated bets, using leverage, and [short selling](/short-selling/). Some are legendary traders; many underperform.

**Market makers** are firms (like Citadel, Virtu) that buy and sell [stocks](/stock/) continuously, profiting on the bid-ask spread and on small price movements. They provide liquidity and reduce spreads but are sometimes accused of front-running retail orders.

**Brokers and financial advisors** route orders, provide advice, and charge fees.

**Algorithmic traders** use computer models to predict short-term price movements and execute thousands of trades per second. High-frequency trading (HFT) is controversial; it adds liquidity but can also amplify volatility.

All of these players interact, and their collective actions set prices every moment the market is open.

## Why the stock market matters

The [stock](/stock/) market matters because:

1. **Wealth.** Trillions of dollars of household wealth, pension savings, and insurance reserves are tied up in [stocks](/stock/). When the [stock](/stock/) market falls, people feel poorer and spend less, which slows the [economy](/recession/).
2. **Capital allocation.** The [stock](/stock/) market channels savings into productive enterprises. A [stock](/stock/) that soars attracts more capital and investment. A [stock](/stock/) that crashes raises the cost of capital for that company.
3. **Expectations.** The [stock](/stock/) market is a continuous referendum on the future. It incorporates millions of individual bets and information. While it is often wrong in the short run, it has historically been a decent guide to long-term economic trends.
4. **Policy feedback.** Central banks like the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) watch the [stock](/stock/) market closely. A severe crash can trigger emergency interest rate cuts or [central bank](/central-bank/) interventions (quantitative easing, etc.).

## Common stock market terms

**Bull market:** A period of sustained [stock](/stock/) price increases, typically defined as a 20% gain from a recent low. [Bull markets](/bull-market/) are characterized by optimism, rising corporate profits, and strong employment.

**Bear market:** A period of sustained [stock](/stock/) price decline, typically defined as a 20% drop from a recent high. [Bear markets](/bear-market/) bring pessimism, profit warnings, and sometimes [recessions](/recession/).

**Correction:** A short-term [stock](/stock/) price decline, typically 10–20%. Corrections are normal and do not necessarily signal a [bear market](/bear-market/).

**Volatility:** The magnitude and speed of price swings. High volatility means prices move rapidly and unpredictably; low volatility means prices are stable. The [Volatility Index](/bear-market/) (VIX) measures this.

**Liquidity:** The ease of buying and selling a [stock](/stock/) without affecting its price. Large-cap [stocks](/stock/) are liquid (you can buy or sell millions of shares with minimal price impact); small-cap [stocks](/stock/) are less liquid.
