*A company's **market capitalization** counts all shares times the stock price, but its **float**—the shares actually available to trade—is often much smaller. That gap determines which indices will hold the stock, how easily a position can be entered or exited, and how much price swings can magnify.*

## The mathematical difference

Market capitalization is straightforward: multiply the current stock price by the total number of shares outstanding. If a company has 100 million shares and trades at $50, its market cap is $5 billion.

Float, by contrast, excludes shares that are not readily tradable. Those locked-up shares typically include:

- **Insider holdings**: shares owned by founders, executives, and board members, often subject to company policy or contractual lock-ups.
- **Restricted stock awards**: employee compensation that vests over time and cannot be immediately sold.
- **Strategic stakes**: large blocks held by parent companies, private-equity sponsors, or long-term institutional holders who signal no intent to sell.
- **Treasury stock**: shares the company has repurchased and holds rather than retired.

If that same 100-million-share company has 30 million shares held by insiders and in restricted pools, the float is only 70 million shares. The float-based market cap would be 70 million × $50 = $3.5 billion, versus $5 billion on a fully-diluted basis.

## Why float matters for index construction

Most major stock indices—including the [S&P 500](/sp-500-index/), [Nasdaq](/nasdaq/), and international benchmarks—weight holdings by **free float** rather than total market cap. This design reflects a practical reality: if a stock's shares are mostly locked up, investors cannot actually buy or replicate the index weightings at the stated proportion.

An index provider publishing 15% allocated to Company X is making a promise that roughly 15% of the "investable" stock is held. If Company X's float is only half its market cap, the index must shrink the allocation to reflect true available supply. This prevents indices from becoming unrepresentative of what investors can actually buy.

Free-float adjustment can be dramatic. A company with a dominant founder or state ownership may trade at a $10 billion total market cap but float-adjusted weight of only $3 billion. When it is added to an index, many investors are surprised to learn their intended allocation is far smaller than the raw market-cap weight would suggest.

## Impact on volatility and spreads

Smaller floats tend to produce higher [volatility](/volatility-smile/) and wider [bid-ask spreads](/bid-ask-spread/). With fewer shares available to buy or sell, demand or supply shocks can move the price sharply. A $50 million buy order in a 100-million-share float (0.05% of float) feels routine; the same order in a 10-million-share float (0.5% of float) can cause a sharp spike.

This dynamic matters acutely for large institutional investors or index funds. If an index reconstitution forces a fund to add a small-float stock, buying the necessary shares can push the price up during the transition. Conversely, an outflow can hammer the price down. These temporary but measurable distortions are called "index effects" and are best understood as a friction cost of owning small-float names.

## The IPO and gradual unlock pattern

New companies typically go public with founder stakes still intact. On the first day of trading, the IPO-allocated public float might be only 10–20% of the company. Over subsequent months, lockup agreements expire and insiders, venture investors, and early employees can sell.

As more shares hit the market, the float grows and the stock typically becomes easier to trade. Until that unlock is complete, the stock may trade with elevated volatility. Index funds watch for float thresholds—many indices have minimum-float rules (such as "at least $100 million in daily tradable market value") that exclude stocks until they have matured.

## Why it affects liquidity and short-selling

[Short-sellers](/short-selling/) and traders hunting for leverage are acutely sensitive to float. A short-seller betting against a company looks for liquid exit routes; a stock with minimal float and wide spreads is risky to exit under pressure. Regulators also impose short-selling constraints—some brokers limit short stock to names with sufficient float and volume.

Similarly, leveraged investors or [hedge funds](/hedge-fund/) sizing up a large position must account for float constraints. Buying 5% of a $10 billion float is generally straightforward; buying 5% of a $1 billion float can move the market and force the buyer to accept a worse execution price. Institutional asset allocators often avoid or underweight small-float stocks because the constraints on position sizing exceed the liquidity premium the stock offers.

## Conversion and dilution

Float can also shrink over time if a company issues restricted stock or treasury stock. Conversely, it can expand if restricted holders sell their stakes or if [preferred stock](/preferred-stock/) converts to common. Major M&A transactions, debt restructurings, or secondary offerings can all shift the float calculation overnight.

For investors and analysts, tracking float changes is a way to spot insider selling (large unlock = potential weakness) or insider confidence (an insider buying a large block on the open market despite locked-up holdings suggests conviction).
