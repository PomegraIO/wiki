---
title: "Bonus Issue"
description: "A corporate action in which a company issues new shares to existing shareholders free of charge, funded from retained earnings or reserves, proportional to their holdings."
---

*A bonus issue (also called a "bonus share" or "scrip issue" in some jurisdictions) is a distribution of free shares to existing shareholders, typically funded by capitalizing retained earnings or other reserves on the balance sheet. Unlike a [stock dividend](/wiki/stock-dividend/), which is a cash-funded dividend paid in shares, a bonus issue uses internal capital to create new shares with no cash outlay. The total value of the company is unchanged, but the number of shares increases and the price per share declines proportionally.*

## Mechanics of a bonus issue

A company's board authorizes a bonus issue (often approved by shareholders) specifying the ratio: a 1-for-2 bonus means each shareholder receives one new share for every two held. If a shareholder owns 1,000 shares before the bonus, they receive 500 new shares, bringing their total to 1,500 shares. The company's balance sheet shows a corresponding increase in equity: retained earnings or reserves are transferred to common stock.

The company does not issue new debt, nor does it accept cash from shareholders. All that changes is the number of shares outstanding and the balance sheet's equity accounts. The transfer agent handles the mechanical issuance, and shareholders receive the new shares via their brokerage account or as physical certificates.

## Why companies issue bonus shares

The stated reason is to make the share price more "attractive." If a company's share price has risen to, say, $800 per share, new retail investors may find the price daunting. A 2-for-1 bonus makes it 1,200 shares at $533 per share (approximately), reducing the per-share price and potentially attracting smaller investors.

Bonus issues are especially popular in India, Asia, and other markets where per-share price expectations differ culturally from the West. A company with strong earnings and a rising share price might issue a bonus to celebrate the achievement and increase shareholder engagement and perceived shareholding.

A secondary motivation is to improve liquidity and tradability. A higher share count with a lower per-share price can increase trading volume, reduce bid-ask spreads, and attract more retail investors.

## Balance sheet effects

A bonus issue transfers value from retained earnings (or reserves) to the common stock account. The company's total equity remains constant, but the composition shifts. A company with $100 million in retained earnings and 50 million shares outstanding ($2 of earnings per share, ignoring net income) that issues a 1-for-1 bonus now has 100 million shares outstanding and retained earnings of $0 (assuming the entire reserve was capitalized). The new EPS is $1 per share ($100 million / 100 million shares), illustrating the mechanical dilution.

This is a purely accounting adjustment. The company's assets, liabilities, and real earning power are unchanged. Yet the lower per-share price can influence retail investor behavior.

## Bonus issues versus stock dividends

A [stock dividend](/wiki/stock-dividend/) is similar but is often considered a dividend and sometimes carries different accounting or tax treatment. In the U.S., a small stock dividend (less than 25 percent of shares outstanding) is recorded at the stock's fair value and debits retained earnings. A large stock dividend (greater than 25 percent) is treated as a stock split and recorded at par value.

A bonus issue, by contrast, is always recorded at par value and framed as a capitalization of reserves. In practice, the accounting results are similar, but terminology differs by jurisdiction. In India and some Asian markets, "bonus issue" is the standard term; in the U.S., "stock dividend" is more common for the same phenomenon.

## Dividend distribution and tax implications

Shareholders receiving bonus shares in most jurisdictions do not face an immediate tax liability (the receipt is not taxable income). Instead, the shareholder's cost basis is adjusted. If a shareholder bought 100 shares at $50 per share and receives a 1-for-1 bonus, they now own 200 shares with an adjusted cost basis of $25 per share. When shares are eventually sold, the gain is computed against the adjusted basis.

Some jurisdictions (notably India) tax bonus issues differently. The issue may trigger a distribution or treated as income in certain cases, though this is rare in developed markets.

## Market and shareholder reactions

Stock market research suggests bonus issues, like [stock splits](/wiki/stock-split/), do not create value but can increase trading volume and retail participation. The share price typically adjusts downward proportionally to preserve the company's total market cap. A shareholder does not become wealthier simply because they hold more shares; the value is merely divided into smaller pieces.

Some studies find a modest short-term price appreciation after a bonus announcement (1–3 percent), possibly reflecting optimism from management or a signaling effect. But long-term performance does not appear to differ significantly between companies that issue bonuses and those that don't.

## Regulatory and accounting rules

Bonus issues are subject to corporate law and securities regulations in each jurisdiction. In the U.S., the company must have sufficient retained earnings or reserves to capitalize; issuing a bonus when the company is unprofitable or has negative equity can violate solvency laws. Shareholders must typically approve the bonus at a general meeting, and the company must file appropriate disclosures with securities regulators.
