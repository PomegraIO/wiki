*When a foreign company wants to offer [American Depositary Receipts](/adr/) to U.S. investors, the depositary bank files a **Form F-6** with the [Securities and Exchange Commission](/securities-and-exchange-commission/). This form registers the ADR programme, discloses the underlying company's jurisdiction and rights, and sets out the [depositary receipt](/adr/) fees and operational terms. Unlike other SEC forms, Form F-6 is filed by the depositary, not the foreign company itself. It is the public commitment that ADR terms are legal, the fees are transparent, and U.S. shareholders have enforceable rights.*

## Why Form F-6 exists

The [Securities Exchange Act of 1934](/securities-and-exchange-commission/) requires any security offered or traded in the U.S. public markets to be registered with the SEC unless explicitly exempt. Historically, foreign companies could not easily register their own shares because they lacked U.S. incorporation and faced foreign tax and regulatory hurdles. ADRs solved this: a U.S. depositary bank creates the receipt-based instrument, registers it, and holds the underlying foreign shares in trust. The SEC treats the ADR as the security in question and requires the depositary to file Form F-6 as proof that the programme is properly structured and transparently disclosed.

Form F-6 is unique because it is filed by the depositary, not the foreign company. The depositary asserts that it has legal authority to hold the foreign shares, that the deposit agreement is binding, and that all fees, risks, and operational procedures are properly disclosed. This arrangement avoids forcing foreign companies to navigate U.S. securities law directly, while still protecting U.S. investors through SEC oversight.

## Core contents and disclosures

A complete Form F-6 filing includes the deposit agreement—the legal contract between the depositary, the foreign company, and ADR holders—which lays out every operational rule: how dividends are converted and paid, how voting is administered, what happens if the foreign company changes its capital structure, and how the programme can be terminated. The form also itemizes all fees, including custody charges, issuance and cancellation fees, and pass-through costs for corporate actions.

The filing requires the depositary to identify the foreign company's jurisdiction, stock exchange listing(s), and outstanding share count. It must describe the foreign company's business in plain English, disclose material risks (foreign ownership restrictions, currency risk, political instability, taxation differences), and explain how ADR voting aligns with ordinary shareholder rights. A risk-disclosure section typically includes warnings about [currency volatility](/currency-volatility/), [country risk](/country-risk/), and the possibility of ADR termination.

Form F-6 also addresses the tax and accounting treatment of ADRs. It clarifies whether ADR holders are taxed as if they own the underlying shares (usually yes) and whether [dividends](/dividend/) are treated as U.S.-source or foreign-source income. For corporate-action events—[stock splits](/stock-split/), [spin-offs](/spin-off/), rights offerings—the form outlines how the depositary will handle conversion, distribution, or settlement, often cross-referencing the foreign company's corporate law and the depositary's own procedures.

## The deposit agreement as the centerpiece

The deposit agreement, attached as an exhibit to Form F-6, is the binding contract governing the ADR programme. It specifies the rights and obligations of all parties: the depositary's duty to safeguard the foreign shares, the ADR holder's rights to dividends and [voting](/voting-rights/), and the foreign company's obligations to inform the depositary of corporate actions and shareholder meetings.

The agreement typically includes an amendment clause: either party may propose changes to fees or terms, but the SEC generally requires that material changes be disclosed in an amended Form F-6 (filed as Form F-6/A) before taking effect. This prevents surprise fee increases or restrictions on voting without prior public notice. However, the amendment clause may also allow the foreign company or depositary to terminate the programme with limited notice, a power that can catch holders off-guard.

Some deposit agreements include a "residual provision" that addresses what happens to unclaimed ADRs or the underlying foreign shares if the programme terminates. This is crucial because it determines whether holders retain claims on the underlying shares or lose them to the depositary's escheat procedures.

## Form F-6/A: amendments and ongoing disclosure

After the initial Form F-6 becomes effective, any material change to the programme requires an amended filing (Form F-6/A). Common amendments include fee increases, changes to voting procedures, modifications to the deposit agreement, or the foreign company's acquisition or merger. The SEC reviews amendments under a lighter standard than the initial Form F-6, but material changes still trigger SEC review and public comment periods.

Some programmes file Form F-6/A annually to update information about the foreign company—earnings, major developments, changes in management—even if the ADR terms themselves do not change. Other programmes treat Form F-6 as a static registration, only amending when legal obligations force them to. This variation in disclosure diligence is one reason some ADRs carry outdated information on the SEC's EDGAR database.

## Effectiveness and exemptions

Form F-6 typically becomes effective 60 days after filing unless the SEC objects and requests additional disclosures. Once effective, the depositary can issue ADRs and list them on a [stock exchange](/stock-exchange/) or [over-the-counter market](/over-the-counter-market/). A few ADR programmes operate under exemptive relief from the SEC, meaning they are partly exempt from certain disclosure or registration requirements—a power the SEC grants when the foreign jurisdiction's own regulations are deemed sufficiently protective of U.S. investors. These exemptive programs file a different form (Form F-6e or a no-action letter) rather than a full Form F-6.

## Investor use of Form F-6

Most retail ADR investors never read Form F-6. It is technical, lengthy, and filed with the SEC's EDGAR database rather than promoted by brokers. However, institutional investors, analysts, and activists routinely download Form F-6 filings to audit fee structures, understand governance restrictions, and track changes to the deposit agreement. The form is freely available on the SEC website, making it an excellent resource for due diligence before investing in an unfamiliar ADR.

Form F-6 is also the basis for disputes. If a depositary unilaterally changes voting procedures or imposes undisclosed fees, holders can cite the original Form F-6 filing as evidence of what was promised. Similarly, if the SEC suspects a depositary is violating ADR holder rights, it will review the Form F-6 filing to determine whether the depositary is operating within the disclosed terms.
