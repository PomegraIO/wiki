*A **squeeze-out merger** (also called a freeze-out) is a transaction in which a controlling shareholder or group forces remaining minority shareholders to exit the company in exchange for a set cash price, eliminating their equity stake and future upside. Because the controlling party has the votes to approve the deal unilaterally, the central question is fairness: whether the offered price compensates minority shareholders for what they're losing, and whether they had any way to influence or challenge the terms.*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

Not to be confused with a [hostile takeover](/hostile-takeover/), which targets an unwilling board rather than the majority shareholder using existing control.

## Why Controlling Shareholders Pursue Squeeze-Outs

A majority shareholder might engineer a squeeze-out for several reasons. The company may be undervalued publicly (a "going-private" squeeze-out), its [public company](/public-company/) status may impose compliance costs (Sarbanes-Oxley, SEC reporting), or the controller wants 100% ownership to pursue a strategic shift minority shareholders might block. Controlling shareholders also use squeeze-outs to lock in perceived value before anticipated declines, or to convert illiquid minority equity into liquid cash for the buyout firm.

From the controller's perspective, minority shareholders represent a drag: they vote on major transactions, demand disclosures, and can sue. Eliminating them via a cash-out merger silences dissent and simplifies ownership.

## The Two-Part Fairness Standard

U.S. courts (especially Delaware courts, which hear many such cases) apply a two-pronged fairness test: **fair dealing** and **fair price**.

**Fair dealing** examines the process. Did management disclose all material facts to the minority before the vote? Was the process arms-length, or did the controlling shareholder steamroll the board? Did the company hire an independent financial advisor to evaluate the offer? Were minority shareholders given time to consider and vote? A cleaner process—independent board committee, go-shop clause, majority-of-the-minority vote requirement—strengthens the dealing inquiry.

**Fair price** assesses whether the cash offer reflects the true value of the company. Courts look to [discounted cash flow](/discounted-cash-flow-valuation/) analyses, trading prices immediately before the announcement, comparable company multiples, historical [earnings per share](/earnings-per-share/), and any expert valuations. If the deal price sits materially below defensible fundamental value, it may fail the fairness test even if the process was clean.

## The Role of Appraisal Rights

In many jurisdictions, including Delaware, minority shareholders in a squeeze-out have **appraisal rights**: the right to demand the court determine the "fair value" of their shares rather than accept the merger price. If they invoke appraisal and later win, they receive that higher court-determined value (plus interest).

Appraisal is a double-edged sword. It offers a potential remedy if the merger price is unfair, but it requires the shareholder to go to court, which is costly and time-consuming. Appraisal disputes can last years. Courts must decide what "fair value" means—some apply the merger price itself, others use a discounted cash flow or comparable-company multiple independent of the deal. The outcome is uncertain, which deters some minorities from pursuing appraisal and encourages a modest premium in the initial offer to avoid litigation risk.

## Entire Fairness vs. Business Judgment Review

If a squeeze-out is approved only by the controlling shareholder (i.e., the board is tainted by the controller's dominance), courts apply "entire fairness" review. The controlling party must prove both fair dealing and fair price. This is a heavy burden. Conversely, if the board forms a special committee of independent directors, fully discloses all material facts, and wins a majority-of-the-minority vote (i.e., a majority of non-controlling shareholders approve), the court may shift to "business judgment" review—a deferential standard that upholds the deal unless the plaintiff shows self-dealing or fraud. This structural protection incentivizes controllers to let independent parties negotiate and decide.

## Practical Example

A private equity firm owns 65% of a publicly traded software company. The stock trades at $40. The PE firm believes the company is worth $52 per share based on its five-year cash flow forecast. Rather than wait for an organic refinancing or IPO exit, the PE firm causes the board to announce a $48 per-share cash-out merger. This price is:
- A 20% premium to the $40 pre-announcement stock price (attractive on the surface).
- Below the $52 intrinsic value the controller believes (the controller captures the $4 per-share spread).
- Subject to appraisal: if a minority shareholder sues, they argue the fair value is $50–$52, not $48.

A court would examine: Did the board have a genuine special committee that negotiated hard? Was there a go-shop period? Did the company get expert valuation advice? And is $48 actually defensible given the cash flow model and comparable public companies? If the company's peers trade at 8× EBITDA and the company generates EBITDA of $6 million, fair value is at least $48 million (valuation will depend on total shares outstanding). If the company trades at $40 and the controller gets $48, the controller has captured value at the minority's expense—grounds for appraisal challenge or fairness complaint.

## Jurisdictional Differences

Delaware, home to many public corporations, has well-developed squeeze-out law favoring procedural protections (special committees, majority-of-minority votes) but tough appraisal standards. Other U.S. states vary; some offer weaker protections for minorities. European and Commonwealth jurisdictions often impose stronger shareholder protections—mandatory bid rules, minority appraisal rights at a statutory floor price, and stricter conflict-of-interest scrutiny.

## Common Defenses and Settlements

Controllers often argue the merger price reflects arms-length negotiation and comparable precedent. Minorities counter that the price undervalues growth or synergy. Many cases settle: the controller agrees to a slightly higher price (often $1–$3 per share higher) in exchange for the minority dropping appraisal claims. This avoids the cost and uncertainty of litigation.
