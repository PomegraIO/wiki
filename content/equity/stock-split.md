*A stock split is a corporate action in which a company divides each outstanding [share](/stock/) into a fixed number of new shares. In a 2-for-1 split, one share becomes two shares; in a 3-for-1 split, one becomes three. The total value of a shareholder's position is unchanged — if you owned $10,000 worth of stock before the split, you still own $10,000 worth afterward. The split is a mechanical adjustment with no economic impact, but it changes the number of shares and the per-share price.*

## How a stock split works

A company announces a 2-for-1 stock split. Before the split:

- 100 million shares outstanding.
- Stock price: $400 per share.
- Market cap: $40 billion.

After the split:

- 200 million shares outstanding (each shareholder's share count doubles).
- Stock price: $200 per share (adjusted proportionally).
- Market cap: $40 billion (unchanged).

A shareholder who owned 100 shares worth $40,000 now owns 200 shares worth $40,000. Percentage ownership (e.g., 1-in-1-million) is unchanged.

## Why companies split stock

The stated reasons are:

1. **Affordability**: High-priced stock is less accessible to retail investors. A $400 stock seems expensive compared to a $200 stock, even though it is the same company. Making the per-share price lower can widen the retail investor base.

2. **Psychological appeal**: Investors often prefer lower per-share prices (even though percentage returns are identical). This is behavioral finance and arguably irrational, but market psychology is real.

3. **Option strike prices**: After a split, [option](/equity/employee-stock-options/) and [RSU](/equity/restricted-stock-units/) strike prices are adjusted proportionally, keeping the grant value constant but making the per-share amount more intuitive.

However, most research finds **no economic benefit** to stock splits. Returns to shareholders are the same before and after. The popularity of splits is more a matter of tradition and psychology.

## Recent shifts in split behavior

Historically, splits were common when stock prices climbed. However, in recent decades, the practice has declined. Amazon and Berkshire Hathaway resisted splits for decades despite high prices. Tech companies now favor [stock dividends](/equity/stock-dividend/) or simply accept high per-share prices.

The shift reflects:

- **Index funds and institutions** are price-agnostic; they buy fractions of shares regardless of price.
- **Retail investors** can now trade fractional shares (thanks to platforms like Fidelity and Schwab).
- **High prices signal quality** to some investors.

## Stock split mechanics

A stock split is executed by:

1. The board proposes the split ratio.
2. Shareholders vote to approve it (required for most splits).
3. The company sets a record date. Shareholders as of that date receive additional shares.
4. The stock exchange adjusts the ticker price to reflect the split.
5. Options, [RSUs](/equity/restricted-stock-units/), [warrants](/equity/warrant/), and other equity are adjusted by the split ratio.

The entire process is automatic from the shareholder's perspective. You own shares one day, and the next day you own proportionally more shares at a proportionally lower price.

## 3-for-1 and other ratios

Splits come in various ratios:

- **2-for-1**: The most common; share count doubles, price halves.
- **3-for-1**: Share count triples, price drops to one-third.
- **5-for-1, 7-for-1**: Less common, used by companies with very high prices.

Apple executed a 4-for-1 split in 2020; Tesla executed a 5-for-1 split in 2020 and a 3-for-1 split in 2022. Google (Alphabet) executed a 20-for-1 split in 2022 (one of the largest splits in recent history).

## Reverse stock splits

A [reverse stock split](/equity/reverse-stock-split/) is the inverse: 1-for-2, 1-for-5, etc. One share becomes a fraction of a share; share count declines, per-share price rises.

Reverse splits are typically used when stock price has fallen very low (below exchange minimum listing requirements, usually $1 per share for NYSE). A company trading at $0.50 might execute a 1-for-10 reverse split, after which it trades at $5 and complies with exchange rules.

## Tax treatment of stock splits

Stock splits are not taxable events. You do not recognize capital gain or loss upon the split. Your cost basis in the shares is divided proportionally.

**Example**:
- You bought 100 shares at $200 = $20,000 cost basis.
- 2-for-1 split occurs.
- You now own 200 shares with a cost basis of $100 each (total $20,000).
- Upon sale at $300 per share (600 shares): gain is $200 - $300 = $100 per share, or $20,000 total gain.

The cost basis is automatically adjusted by your broker; you do not need to manually recalculate.

## Dividend adjustments for splits

When a stock splits, [dividend](/dividend/) rates are adjusted proportionally. If a stock paid $1 per share and splits 2-for-1, it now pays $0.50 per share (but you own twice as many shares, so your total dividend is unchanged).

## Market impact of stock splits

Academic research finds that stock splits have zero long-term market impact. Some research suggests temporary positive returns (the "break effect") around the split announcement, but it is tiny and attributable to market sentiment, not fundamentals.

The market price is determined by profit expectations and sentiment, not by the number of shares outstanding or the per-share price. A split does not change either of those.

## Fractional shares post-split

Before modern technology, fractional shares were a problem (you could not own 1.5 shares). The company would cash out fractional shares. Now, brokers allow fractional ownership, so if a shareholder owns an odd number of shares at split time, the broker simply keeps a fractional position.
