*A **tracking stock** is a publicly traded equity security whose returns are directly linked to the financial performance of a specific subsidiary or operating division rather than to the consolidated [parent company](/public-company/). Unlike a [spinoff](/spin-off/), the tracked entity remains legally owned by the parent, but shareholders receive a dedicated share class that mimics standalone performance.*

## Why corporations create tracking stocks

Tracking stocks emerged in the 1980s and 1990s as a hybrid alternative to [spinoffs](/spin-off/). A parent company with diverse, high-growth and stable divisions faced a classic dilemma: the market typically discounts conglomerate valuations—a so-called "conglomerate discount"—because investors struggle to value disparate businesses under one roof. An outright spinoff, however, requires regulatory approval, creates legal separation costs, and may break tax neutrality.

A tracking stock offers a third path: the subsidiary remains legally controlled by the parent, but a separate share class is issued with dividends, voting rights, and valuation metrics tethered exclusively to one division's performance. Investors in tracking stock receive exposure to a high-growth or niche business while the parent retains operational control and tax efficiency.

## How tracking stocks differ from spinoffs

The critical distinction lies in ownership and control. In a [spinoff](/spin-off/), the subsidiary becomes an independent legal entity with its own board and management; shareholders of the parent company receive an equivalent stake in the new company. A tracking stock, by contrast, does not separate the business legally. The subsidiary remains a consolidated subsidiary of the parent, and parent-company management retains formal governance. Tracking shareholders do not vote on day-to-day operations; they vote separately on certain tracking-stock-specific matters, and their economic rights flow from the division's standalone performance.

From a tax perspective, spinoffs can qualify for tax-free reorganization treatment under Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code. Tracking stocks do not receive this exemption, which makes them attractive to parent companies seeking to avoid triggering a taxable distribution to existing shareholders.

## Dividend and capital structure mechanics

Tracking stock holders receive [dividends](/dividend/) declared out of the tracked division's cashflow. Critically, these dividends are paid from the parent company's consolidated treasury, even though they are calculated as if the division operated independently. This means the parent company absorbs any variance: if the tracked division underperforms, the parent's other divisions must partially fund the tracking dividend, or the parent reduces it unilaterally.

[Capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) treatment for tracking stock sales follows standard equity rules. An investor who purchases tracking stock at $50 and sells at $70 realizes a [long-term capital gain](/long-term-capital-gain-tax/) if holding period requirements are met. However, the valuation of tracking stock—and therefore realized gains—can be volatile because it rides entirely on the division's performance, unhedged by the parent's other business lines.

Share buybacks are sometimes executed on tracking stock independently. For instance, if the parent wants to reward tracking shareholders with reduced share count, it might repurchase tracking shares while leaving the [common stock](/common-stock/) outstanding. This is a form of [selective buyback](/selective-buyback/) or divisional capital allocation.

## Governance and risks

Tracking shareholders enjoy limited voting power. They cannot initiate changes to the tracked division's strategy unilaterally, nor can they remove the tracked subsidiary's management without broader parent-company approval. This asymmetry is intentional: it preserves the parent's operational flexibility while giving investors price exposure to a specific business.

The core risk is what happens if the parent company's financial health deteriorates. Because the tracked subsidiary remains legally part of the consolidated entity, creditors of the parent have claims on the subsidiary's assets. In a severe distress scenario—bankruptcy of the parent—tracking stock holders discover that legal separation, which they thought existed economically, was never absolute. This is a genuine legal subordination relative to spinoff shareholders, who own in a truly independent entity.

Additionally, the parent company retains the right to reallocate cash within the group. If the tracked division generates excess cash but the parent decides to fund unrelated business segments or pay down group debt, tracking shareholders cannot block it. The division's apparent profitability does not automatically translate to tracking-stock dividends.

## Illustrative history and practice

General Motors issued tracking stock in the 1990s tied to its General Motors Financial Company subsidiary. AT&T issued tracking stock linked to wireless and other segments during its pivotal reorganizations. These structures appealed especially when a parent wanted to shed conglomerate-discount stigma without committing to full legal separation.

The practice has fallen out of favour in recent decades, in part because investors became more comfortable valuing diversified companies and in part because spinoffs became easier to execute tax-efficiently. However, tracking stocks remain viable in narrow situations where a parent company retains close operational coordination with a tracked division and faces genuine tax or regulatory reasons to avoid a full spinoff.

## When tracking stock matters for investors

Tracking stock appeals to investors who want pure-play exposure to a specific division without owning the parent's other operations. For instance, an investor bullish on a parent company's tech division but bearish on its legacy industrial business might prefer tracking stock over [common stock](/common-stock/).

Conversely, tracking stocks carry liquidity and concentration risk. The secondary market for tracking shares is often thinner than for the parent's main equity, and the tracking class is exposed to idiosyncratic division performance without the stabilizing effect of the parent's other businesses. An economic downturn in the tracked industry can drive valuation down sharply, whereas [common stock](/common-stock/) shareholders benefit from diversification.
