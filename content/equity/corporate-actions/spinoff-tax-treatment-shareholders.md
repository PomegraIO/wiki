*A **spin-off tax treatment** under US law can be either tax-free or taxable depending on structure and compliance with IRS rules. ## Section 355: The Tax-Free Spin-Off Framework

The Internal Revenue Code allows a parent corporation to distribute shares of a subsidiary to shareholders without immediate tax if strict conditions are met. This is codified in Section 355 and is one of the few corporate actions that can be completely tax-free.

The rationale is straightforward: shareholders already held equity in a unified company; the spin merely separates that equity into two publicly traded vehicles. Their economic interest in the underlying assets has not increased or decreased—only its legal packaging. The IRS permits deferral to avoid penalizing legitimate business reorganizations.

A **tax-free spinoff** means you do not owe capital gains tax at the moment the subsidiary shares land in your account. You have realized no gain or loss yet. The tax liability is deferred until you eventually sell either the parent or subsidiary shares.

## The Three Tests for Tax-Free Status

The IRS imposes three gatekeeping tests. Failure of any one makes the spinoff fully taxable.

**The continuity-of-business test** requires that both the parent and subsidiary continue operating the businesses they carried on before the spinoff. If a parent spins off a subsidiary and then liquidates or radically restructures the remaining business, the IRS may deny tax-free treatment. The intent is to ensure shareholders' stake in an ongoing business operation is preserved, not that a company is using a spinoff as cover for a disguised taxable sale.

**The device test** asks whether the spinoff was designed to distribute earnings and profits tax-free. This is intentionally vague but is meant to prevent companies from using spinoffs as a tax dodge. In practice, if the spinoff appears to have a genuine non-tax business purpose—such as unlocking separate valuations for two distinct business lines—the device test is easily satisfied. If the spinoff looks like a pretext to pay a hidden dividend, it fails.

**The active-trade-or-business test** requires that both parent and subsidiary operate active trades or businesses (not passive income or investment operations) immediately after the spin. A parent holding only stocks or bonds cannot be spun; both sides must be operating ventures. This prevents companies from sheltering passive income in a tax-free distribution.

These tests are technical and frequently require IRS ruling letters in advance. Public companies typically obtain comfort before announcing a spinoff.

## Cost Basis Allocation: Splitting Your Original Investment

When you receive subsidiary shares in a tax-free spinoff, your cost basis in the original parent stock is **allocated** between parent and subsidiary, not duplicated. If you bought parent stock at $100 per share and receive subsidiary shares worth $30 in a spinoff, your original basis does not remain $100 for the parent; instead, it is split.

The allocation is proportional to fair value. If the parent is worth $70 after the spinoff and the subsidiary is worth $30, and your original basis was $100, you allocate:

- Parent basis: $100 × ($70 / $100) = $70
- Subsidiary basis: $100 × ($30 / $100) = $30

Now, when you sell parent stock for $72, you have a $2 gain; selling subsidiary for $31 yields a $1 gain. The total deferred gain ($0 at spinoff) is preserved across the two positions.

The IRS does not issue a per-share basis statement for spinoffs automatically; you (or your tax software, or your tax advisor) must calculate and track it. Many brokers do allocate basis automatically in their systems, but you should verify or consult your 1099 or Schedule D carefully after a spinoff.

## When the Spinoff Becomes Taxable

Tax-free treatment can unravel in several ways.

**Failed continuity or active-trade tests** make the entire spinoff taxable. You are treated as if the parent paid you a dividend equal to the fair value of the subsidiary shares, and you owe tax on that dividend at ordinary income rates (or preferential rates if it qualifies as a [qualified dividend](/qualified-dividend/)).

**Fractional shares** are handled either by rounding down or paying cash in lieu. If you own 101 shares and the spinoff ratio creates 33.67 subsidiary shares, cash paid for the 0.67 fraction is taxable gain (or loss), calculated using your allocated basis. This is usually immaterial but can create a small tax bill.

**Subsequent restructuring** that appears to be a pretext for distribution can trigger tax retroactively. For example, if parent and subsidiary merge within two years after the spinoff, the IRS may recharacterize the spinoff as a disguised dividend. Case law and revenue procedures define safe harbors, and again, companies typically secure IRS ruling letters before proceeding.

**Cash boot** distributed alongside subsidiary shares (e.g., "receive subsidiary shares plus $5 cash") is taxable to the extent of that cash as a dividend or gain, depending on the parent's earnings and profits.

## Holding Period and Long-Term Capital Gains

One of the tax-deferral benefits is that your **holding period** in the subsidiary shares is deemed to start on the same date you purchased the original parent stock. This matters for [long-term capital gains](/long-term-capital-gain-tax/) treatment. If you bought parent stock on January 1, 2023, and it is spun on January 1, 2024, your subsidiary shares are immediately deemed to have been held for over one year (from the Jan 1, 2023 date), qualifying for long-term rates upon sale.

This tacking rule is automatic and requires no election or paperwork. It is one reason tax-free spinoffs are valuable to long-term shareholders.

## Non-US Spinoff Tax Rules

Outside the United States, spinoff treatment varies.

In the **United Kingdom**, a spinoff of subsidiary shares may qualify for **roll-over relief** under TCGA 1992, deferring gain similarly to Section 355. The mechanism is different—relief is claimed on a tax return—but the outcome is comparable.

In **Canada**, a spinoff can be tax-free under rules treating the distribution as a non-taxable return of capital or reorganization if specific conditions are met. Basis allocation applies similarly.

Consult a tax advisor in your jurisdiction; spinoff tax rules are highly localized and often require ruling letters or filings.

## Spinoffs vs Carve-Outs and Going-Private Spins

A spinoff is distinct from a **carve-out** or secondary offering, in which the parent sells new subsidiary shares to the public and retains some stake. Carve-outs are always taxable to the parent (though not necessarily to existing shareholders receiving a dividend from the carve-out proceeds).

A spinoff is also different from an **IPO** of a subsidiary where no shares are distributed to existing parents shareholders; again, no tax deferral applies to the parent shareholder base in an IPO.
