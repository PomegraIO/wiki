*An **allotment share repurchase** is the systematic buyback of shares either allocated to employees through compensation schemes or acquired gradually from public markets, designed to reduce the outstanding share count and bolster earnings per share.*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">
Related but distinct from treasury stock mechanisms and accelerated repurchase programs.
</div>

<aside class="wiki-infobox">

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Buy shares from open market or repurchase allocated shares |
| **Primary purpose** | Reduce dilution, increase EPS, offset option grants |
| **Common timing** | Systematically over months or years |
| **Tax treatment** | Capital gains on sale; no deduction for repurchasing firm |
| **Key metric** | Shares repurchased as % of average outstanding |
| **Typical actor** | Public companies with excess cash |

## Why companies use allotment repurchases instead of special dividends

A **[dividend](/wiki/dividend/)** distributes cash directly; a repurchase reduces share count and boosts per-share metrics even if total profit stays flat. Repurchases also offer optionality—the company can pause during downturns, whereas dividends carry expectations of consistency. For employees receiving [equity grants](/wiki/employee-stock-options/), repurchases neutralize the dilution that would otherwise depress EPS over time.

## The mechanics of open-market purchases

When a company conducts an allotment repurchase through regular market buys, it typically signals a predetermined budget (say, $100 million over 12 months) without a fixed share target. This flexibility lets management take advantage of low valuations—buying more shares when the [stock price](/wiki/stock/) dips relative to fundamentals. The approach contrasts sharply with a [buyback authorization](/wiki/buyback-authorization/), which sets a share range and may employ brokers to execute outside normal trading windows for tax efficiency.

## Allocated share repurchase in employee compensation

Many firms grant shares or options to employees at a discount or through [restricted stock units](/wiki/restricted-stock-units/). To offset the dilution, the company repurchases roughly equivalent shares in the open market. This practice keeps the [share count](/wiki/shares-of-stock/) stable and protects existing shareholders from having their stake watered down. Unlike a [cash merger](/wiki/cash-merger-transaction/), which combines companies, this is purely internal capital reallocation.

## Earnings per share accretion and its limits

A company with $100 million in net income and 50 million shares has $2 EPS. If it repurchases 2 million shares (reducing the count to 48 million), EPS rises to $2.08 mechanically—no new profit was earned, only spread across fewer shares. Sophisticated investors recognize this effect. If the [price-to-earnings ratio](/wiki/price-to-earnings-ratio/) contracts because growth slows, the EPS illusion evaporates. Allotment repurchases create real value only when the company buys below [intrinsic value](/wiki/intrinsic-value/).

## Regulatory guardrails and disclosure

In the US, [Rule 10b-5](/wiki/rule-10b-5/) and [Regulation SHO](/wiki/regulation-sho/) constrain when and how much a company can repurchase. Firms must announce the program upfront, set a cap, and avoid blackout periods around earnings releases. Public filings disclose cumulative repurchases, average price paid, and remaining authorization—transparency that the market uses to assess whether management trusts the stock's valuation.

## Tax and capital allocation trade-offs

Shareholders receiving a repurchase implicitly sell back to the company. Under [capital gains tax](/wiki/capital-gains-tax/) rules, [long-term capital gains](/wiki/long-term-capital-gain-tax/) rates apply to the difference between basis and the buyback price. By contrast, a [dividend](/wiki/dividend/) triggers ordinary income tax for all holders. Financially, a repurchase favors high-tax-bracket shareholders who can defer gains, while a dividend treats everyone equally. This asymmetry is why activist investors sometimes demand repurchases when valuations look cheap.
