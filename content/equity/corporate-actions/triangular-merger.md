*A **triangular merger** is a three-party acquisition structure where an acquiring company establishes a wholly-owned subsidiary (often a shell company created just for the deal), and that subsidiary [merges](/merger/) into the target company. The target's shareholders exchange their shares for stock or cash from the parent. The result: the target is absorbed, the subsidiary ceases to exist, and the parent controls the target as a legally distinct subsidiary—a structure that limits the parent's direct exposure to the target's inherited liabilities.*

For the simpler two-party form where the acquirer directly absorbs the target, see [statutory merger](/statutory-merger/). For acquisition of assets without taking on liabilities, see [acquisition](/acquisition/).

## The three-party dance

A standard [statutory merger](/statutory-merger/) is a two-party event: Company A merges into Company B, and B survives. The triangular merger adds a third party—a subsidiary of the acquirer—to achieve a specific goal: isolation.

Here is the sequence:

1. Parent company (the acquirer) creates a new, wholly-owned subsidiary. This subsidiary often has no assets, no revenue, and no history—it is a legal shell.
2. The subsidiary merges with the target company (the acquisition candidate).
3. Under the merger statute, the target is absorbed into the subsidiary. The target ceases to exist as a separate legal entity.
4. The target's shareholders exchange their shares for stock, cash, or a mix—payment provided by the parent.
5. Result: The parent owns a subsidiary (the old shell plus the target's operations), and the target shareholders have exited.

From a legal standpoint, the subsidiary survives the merger, but it is now laden with the target's assets and liabilities. The parent holds this subsidiary at arm's length. This separation is the entire point.

## Why ring-fencing matters

The moment a company acquires another through a traditional [statutory merger](/statutory-merger/), all of the target's liabilities—known and unknown—become liabilities of the acquiring company. Environmental claims, pending litigation, hidden tax disputes, product liability from years past—they all flow into the acquirer's balance sheet and potentially threaten the acquirer's assets.

A triangular merger changes this. The target's liabilities sit with the target subsidiary. The parent company is a separate legal entity. If the target is sued for asbestos exposure, the claim runs against the subsidiary (and the assets within it), not the parent. The parent's other business units and assets are shielded. This containment is especially valuable when the target operates in a high-risk sector—manufacturing, chemicals, healthcare products—or has a disputed legal history.

It is not absolute protection. Creditors sometimes "pierce the corporate veil" and go after the parent if the subsidiary is undercapitalized or the parent is seen as the real wrongdoer. But the separation is real and recognized in law. Sophisticated acquirers use triangular structures for this reason.

## Tax implications

The triangular merger can qualify as a "reorganization" under U.S. federal tax law, allowing target shareholders to defer [capital gains](/long-term-capital-gain-tax/) if the parent's stock is used as the merger consideration. In a true statutory merger, the same tax deferral is available—but the triangular structure gives the parent another advantage: flexibility in the subsidiary's structure and post-merger integration.

If the parent pays in cash rather than stock, the deal is treated as a taxable acquisition, and target shareholders recognize gains immediately. The choice of consideration (stock vs. cash) is often driven by tax strategy, not the triangular structure itself.

## Forward versus reverse triangular merger

The standard triangular merger, described above, is sometimes called a "forward" triangular merger: the parent's subsidiary merges forward into the target.

A **reverse triangular merger** flips the legal survivor: the target merges into the subsidiary, and the subsidiary (the old shell) is the legal survivor. The economic outcome is identical—the parent owns a subsidiary that operates the target's business—but the legal form differs. The reverse variant is often preferred when the target is a regulated entity (a bank, an insurance company, a utility) with licenses and permits that are easier to preserve under the target's own legal charter.

## Practical considerations

**Permit and contract continuity**: In many cases, the target's licenses, franchises, and material contracts can transfer to a subsidiary more easily than they can to a parent. Regulators and counterparties are often comfortable with a subsidiary acquisition because the target's legal identity is preserved (in a reverse triangular merger) or because the subsidiary is a clean, dedicated vehicle with no other liabilities.

**Debt and covenants**: The parent's lenders typically require that the parent itself does not incur large new debt. By housing the target as a subsidiary, the parent can keep the target's acquisition debt (and the target's legacy debt) off the parent's consolidated balance sheet—at least in some accounting treatments. However, the parent usually guarantees the subsidiary's debt, so the shield is incomplete.

**Costs**: Creating a subsidiary adds legal and administrative costs. For a small acquisition, this overhead may not be worth the liability ring-fencing. Triangular structures are typically used in large, complex, or high-risk acquisitions.

**Stock consideration and creeping ownership**: If the parent's stock is used to pay target shareholders, the triangular structure allows the parent to determine how many new shares the parent actually issues, giving the parent's board and existing shareholders more control over dilution.

## Reverse triangular merger in detail

In a reverse triangular merger:

- Parent creates subsidiary A.
- Target merges into subsidiary A.
- Subsidiary A survives (it is the legal successor to the target).
- Subsidiary A is now a subsidiary of the parent.

The distinction from a forward triangular merger is subtle but important when the target has valuable contracts or permits tied to a specific legal entity. By keeping the target's legal identity as the survivor (even though the parent owns it as a subsidiary), many regulatory and commercial relationships remain undisturbed.

For example, if a bank acquires another bank via a reverse triangular merger, the acquired bank's charter and [regulatory capital](/capital-adequacy/) ratios remain associated with the survivor entity, making the transition cleaner for regulators.

## Common pitfalls

**Appraisal rights**: Dissenting shareholders may demand a court valuation of fairness, regardless of the structure.

**Downstream liabilities**: If the parent operates other businesses and eventually merges those into the target subsidiary, liabilities can flow upward. Courts ask whether the subsidiary remains adequately capitalized.

**Loss of net operating losses**: The target's [tax losses](/tax-bracket-investor/) (which could offset future taxes) may be limited or forfeited if the ownership change is too severe, under Section 382 of the U.S. tax code.

**Third-party consents**: "Change of control" clauses may still trigger, even though the target legally survives as a subsidiary. Lenders and suppliers must be consulted before close.
