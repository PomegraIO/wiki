*A **tender offer minimum condition** is a contractual threshold that allows an acquirer to terminate a [tender offer](/tender-offer/) if the bidder receives acceptances from fewer than a specified percentage of the target company's outstanding shares. This clause protects the acquirer from being forced to complete an acquisition at an unacceptable participation rate, while also creating certainty for selling shareholders about whether the deal will close.*

## Why the Minimum Condition Exists

When an acquirer launches a public [tender offer](/tender-offer/) — inviting shareholders to sell their shares at a stated price — there is inherent uncertainty about how many shareholders will actually accept. Even if the bidder has made a public announcement and filed SEC documents setting out the offer terms, shareholders may hold back, hoping for a higher bid, or may simply ignore the offer.

The minimum condition protects the acquirer by allowing it to set a floor: "If at least 80% of you shareholders don't tender, I'm walking away." This ensures the bidder is not stuck acquiring a company with a small subset of accepting shareholders and a large block of holdout [common shareholders](/common-stock/) who remain in the company.

Without a minimum condition, an acquirer could end up owning a company with, say, only 45% of the shares tendered and 55% held by [dissenting shareholders](/common-stock/), forcing complex follow-up transactions (like a second-step [merger](/merger/)) to complete the acquisition. The minimum condition sidesteps that awkwardness.

## Typical Threshold Levels

Tender offer minimum conditions vary widely depending on the deal structure and the acquirer's objectives:

- **50% threshold**: Allows the acquirer to control the company (achieving a majority) but leaves significant minority shareholders unacquired. Common in situations where the bidder already owns shares or does not require 100% ownership immediately.
- **66% supermajority**: Gives the acquirer enough votes to approve most corporate transactions, such as a follow-on [merger](/merger/), without needing the support of dissident shareholders.
- **90% threshold**: Approaches a near-universal acquisition. In some jurisdictions, reaching 90% allows use of streamlined "squeeze-out" procedures to force out remaining minority shareholders.
- **Majority of minority clause**: In cases where the acquirer already owns a significant stake, the minimum condition may apply only to shares held by disinterested parties, not the acquirer's own holdings.

The exact threshold is chosen by the bidder and disclosed in the tender offer documents. Shareholders voting on the offer know the threshold in advance, so they understand that if acceptance falls below it, the deal terminates automatically.

## How the Condition Works in Practice

Suppose AcquireCo launches a tender offer to buy all shares of TargetCo for $50 per share, with a minimum condition of 75%. TargetCo has 10 million shares outstanding. AcquireCo states: "We will proceed with this offer only if we receive acceptances representing at least 7.5 million shares (75%)."

The offer period typically lasts 20 business days (or longer, per the bidder's terms). During that window, shareholders can tender their shares by depositing them with the offer [custodian](/custodian/). As the deadline approaches:

- If AcquireCo receives tenders for 8 million shares, the condition is satisfied. AcquireCo can proceed to purchase all tendered shares (paying the $50 price) and then use a follow-on [merger](/merger/) or [acquisition](/acquisition/) to buy the remaining 2 million shares at the same price.
- If AcquireCo receives tenders for only 6 million shares, the condition is **not** satisfied. AcquireCo withdraws the offer, shareholders' shares are returned to them, and the transaction terminates.

## Waiver and Discretion

The minimum condition is technically a condition for the benefit of the acquirer. This means the bidder can choose to **waive** the condition and proceed with the acquisition even if the threshold is not met. For example, if AcquireCo planned to require 75% acceptance but receives only 70%, it could waive the condition and move forward.

Waiving the condition is a business judgment by the acquirer — it may do so if the 70% acceptance is deemed "close enough" to proceed profitably, or if the acquirer believes it can acquire the remaining 30% through follow-on steps. The waiver must be communicated to shareholders and the SEC.

However, once the acquirer waives the condition or states that it is proceeding, it generally cannot later reverse course and cancel the offer. At that point, the offer is binding, and the acquirer must purchase all tendered shares.

## Interaction with Other Offer Conditions

Tender offers often include multiple closing conditions. Beyond the minimum condition, there may be conditions precedent such as:

- **Regulatory approval** (e.g., [antitrust clearance](/acquisition/))
- **Target board recommendation** (if the offer is initially unsolicited or hostile)
- **Third-party consents** (e.g., lender or customer approvals)
- **No material adverse change** (a MAC clause allowing the bidder to walk if the target's business deteriorates severely)

The minimum condition is distinct from these; it is a quantitative threshold on shareholder participation, not a condition tied to external approvals or target condition.

If the minimum condition is satisfied but another condition is not met (e.g., antitrust regulators block the deal), the bidder can invoke the other condition to terminate. Conversely, if the minimum is not met, the bidder does not need to satisfy other conditions; the deal is already off.

## Implications for Shareholders Tendering Shares

From a shareholder's perspective, tendering into a tender offer with a minimum condition carries a contingent outcome: a shareholder's shares may be tendered but ultimately not purchased if the minimum is not reached. The shareholder's shares are returned (or held in escrow) until the condition outcome is clear.

This is why the minimum condition is disclosed prominently in the offer documents — shareholders need to understand that accepting the offer does not guarantee they will sell their shares; it is conditional on overall participation.

In some cases, shareholders who believe the offer will fail may not tender, while those confident in success will do so early. The dynamics can create a "tipping point" where once a certain threshold is visibly approaching, late-tendering shareholders may rush to participate, knowing closure is likely.

## Why It Protects Target Shareholders Too

While the minimum condition is technically a protection for the acquirer, it also benefits target shareholders. Without it, an acquirer might complete a tender offer for a small percentage of shares, leaving the rest of the shareholders uncertain about whether the deal will ultimately close or whether a follow-on squeeze-out will occur at the same price.

The clear threshold ensures that either the deal gets enough participation to proceed, or it terminates and shareholders go back to business as usual. This creates certainty and prevents prolonged uncertainty.
