*The **book value per share** is [shareholders' equity](/shareholders-equity/) divided by the number of [shares](/common-stock/) outstanding, representing the accounting value of net assets attributable to each share. It serves as a floor or baseline valuation measure, especially useful for comparing firms in asset-heavy industries like [banking](/central-bank/) and [insurance](/auto-insurance/).*

## Basic calculation

The math is straightforward. If a company shows $500 million in [shareholders' equity](/shareholders-equity/) on its [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) and has 50 million shares outstanding, the book value per share is:

$$\text{Book Value Per Share} = \frac{\$500 \text{ million}}{50 \text{ million shares}} = \$10 \text{ per share}$$

If the stock trades at $25, the [price-to-book ratio](/price-to-book-ratio/) is 2.5—the market is willing to pay $2.50 for every $1 of [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) equity.

This calculation relies entirely on [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) values (recorded [historical cost](/historical-cost/), [depreciation](/depreciation/), [amortization](/amortization/)), not on market valuations or forward expectations. Book value is backward-looking and anchored to accounting conventions.

## What it measures

Book value per share answers the question: "If the company were liquidated today at balance sheet values, how much equity would each share receive?"

The assumption is strong: that assets could be sold at their [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) valuations and [liabilities](/balance-sheet/) paid off at recorded amounts. In reality, assets typically sell for more or less than book, and liabilities may involve penalties or discounts. But the measure provides a theoretical floor—a level below which equity appears undervalued (assuming no hidden liabilities or obsolete assets).

## Industries where it matters most

**Banks and financial institutions**: Heavily regulated firms where [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) assets (loans, securities) are the core business. Book value per share is a standard metric. Regulators set minimum [equity ratios](/tier-1-capital/) partly based on risk-weighted assets, making book value critical to lending capacity.

**Insurance companies**: [Balance sheets](/balance-sheet/) bulge with investment portfolios. Book value per share signals the size of the insurer's loss-absorption cushion and asset base available to pay claims.

**Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)**: Required to hold primarily real estate [assets](/balance-sheet/), so [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) values are material. [Price-to-book](/price-to-book-ratio/) signals whether the REIT trades at a premium (market sees value in management) or discount (suggesting distress or poor returns).

**Utilities**: Capital-intensive with long-lived [assets](/balance-sheet/). Regulators often tie allowed returns to [equity ratios](/shareholders-equity/), making book value per share a regulatory constraint.

## Where book value falls short

**Tech and software firms**: A software company might have minimal [tangible assets](/intangible-assets/) but enormous brand value and [goodwill](/goodwill/). Book value per share may be a few dollars, but [market cap](/market-capitalization/) hundreds of times higher. The [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) captures the acquisition price of past acquisitions (recorded as [goodwill](/goodwill/)) but misses internally developed [intangibles](/intangible-assets/).

**Growth companies**: A high-growth biotech or cloud-computing firm has minimal [earnings](/earnings-per-share/) today and may have book value below share price. The market prices in future growth, not current [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) value.

**Asset write-downs**: A retailer with [impaired](/depreciation/) stores, or a bank carrying distressed loans at inflated [book values](/balance-sheet/), may show high book value per share that does not reflect economic reality. [Fair value](/fair-value/) accounting rules help but cannot eliminate the gap.

**Negative book value**: Mature, dividend-paying firms sometimes return more cash to shareholders than they earn, shrinking [retained earnings](/retained-earnings/) and even turning equity negative. Book value per share can go negative, yet the stock continues trading. This signals that market valuations are detached from [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) metrics.

## Tangible book value per share

Some analysts adjust book value by stripping out intangible [assets](/balance-sheet/) like [goodwill](/goodwill/), patents, and [accumulated depreciation](/accumulated-depreciation/). The result is tangible book value per share—a more conservative floor.

For example, if book value per share is $10 but $3 is attributable to [goodwill](/goodwill/) from past acquisitions, tangible book value per share is $7. This figure is especially relevant for financials, where [goodwill](/goodwill/) from bank mergers can inflate reported equity without adding productive capacity.

## Book value and returns on equity

A company trading far above book value must earn high [return on equity](/return-on-equity/) to justify its valuation. If book value per share is $5 and the stock costs $50, investors implicitly expect the company to earn 20% or more on its equity annually. If [earnings per share](/earnings-per-share/) are only $1 (implying 2% [ROE](/return-on-equity/)), the disconnect is unsustainable and the stock is overvalued.

Conversely, a firm trading below book value is often underearning—it cannot generate acceptable returns on its [balance sheet](/balance-sheet/) assets. [Value investors](/value-investing/) hunt for stocks trading below tangible book value, betting that management will unlock hidden asset value or that the business will eventually improve.
