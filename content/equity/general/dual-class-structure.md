*A **dual-class structure** divides a company's equity into two classes of shares with vastly different voting rights and often different economic claims. Typically, Class A shares (held by founders or insiders) have 10 votes per share while Class B shares (held by public investors) have one vote per share. This lets founders retain control of the company despite owning a minority of the economic interest. It's common in tech and media (Google, Facebook, New York Times) and controversial—critics say it entrenches management and weakens governance, while proponents argue it lets founders pursue long-term strategies without quarterly-earnings pressure.*

## Why dual-class structures emerge

The founder's dilemma: after a company goes public, founders no longer own 100% of the firm. Institutional investors, employees with options, and public shareholders now own stakes. Without structural protections, a hostile bidder could accumulate shares, challenge the founder's vision, or force a sale. Early internet entrepreneurs watched mature-company CEOs pressured by quarterly earnings expectations and activist investors, and decided they didn't want that.

Dual-class structures solve this. By issuing Class B shares to the public while retaining Class A shares, founders keep voting control. They can pursue R&D-heavy, profit-sacrificing strategies (like Google's investment in moonshot projects) without fear that impatient shareholders will oust them. They can reject acquisition offers, reject activist campaigns, and run the company for the long term as they see fit.

This creates an implicit bargain with public shareholders: you get economic participation (dividend and capital-gains upside) but not governance voice. Some investors accept this; others refuse, treating dual-class shares as governance red flags.

## Governance implications

Critics argue dual-class structures weaken corporate governance. With insiders holding disproportionate votes, [board of directors](/wiki/board-of-directors/) elections are largely ceremonial—founders pick the board, shareholders can't dislodge it. Executive compensation, [related-party transactions](/wiki/related-party-disclosure/), and strategic decisions flow from founders' preferences, not shareholder pressure.

The risk is captured in the saying: "You're not buying a company, you're betting on a person." If the founder makes bad calls, minority shareholders have limited recourse. The founder can dilute shareholder value, sell to a preferred acquirer (perhaps at favorable terms to insiders), or simply make poor strategic bets. Recapitalizations can shift value from Class B to Class A.

Some founders have lived down this risk through integrity and long-term thinking (Warren Buffett's Berkshire, Sergey Brin's Google). Others have abused it, raising the cost of capital for dual-class companies and triggering regulatory pushback.

## Regulatory and exchange pushback

Nasdaq and NYSE have historically allowed dual-class structures but have tightened rules over time. As of 2021, new dual-class listings are generally discouraged. Companies with existing dual-class structures are "grandparented" but must comply with sunset provisions—some agreements require the classes to converge to one vote per share after a set period (e.g., 7 years) or upon the founder's death.

The SEC has proposed (but not finalized) rules requiring enhanced proxy disclosures for dual-class companies, making the governance tradeoff more visible to investors. Some state legislatures have considered outright bans. Foreign regulators (Europe, Canada) are more hostile, with some banning or heavily restricting dual-class voting.

## Alternatives and partial solutions

Some founders use alternative structures that preserve control without extreme voting disparities. [Staggered boards](/wiki/board-of-directors/)—where board members serve overlapping terms so only a fraction are elected each year—make hostile takeovers harder without formal voting discrimination. [Buyback authorizations](/wiki/buyback-authorization/) and [management stock options](/wiki/employee-stock-options/) can entrench insiders without explicit voting differences.

Others use super-voting structures with lower ratios: 3 votes per insider share vs. 1 per public share. Or time-decay structures where founder shares gradually convert to single-vote shares as the founder ages or steps down. These are partial compromises.

The most protective alternative for founders is simply remaining private or going to a [private equity fund](/wiki/private-equity-fund/)—no public shareholders, no governance tension. IPO dual-class structures represent an explicit choice to access public capital while minimizing governance dilution.

## Economic interests and unequal economics

Beyond voting, some dual-class structures include unequal economic rights. Perhaps Class A gets preferred dividend treatment, anti-dilution provisions, or board seats guaranteed. This makes the structure not just about control but about wealth allocation. European civil law often prohibits such extreme structures, so European dual-class firms typically have equal economic rights but unequal votes.

Acquisitions of dual-class companies create complications. Because insiders have disproportionate votes, a deal requires insider approval regardless of minority shareholder vote. This can create moral hazard: the founder might accept a lower-value deal favorable to insiders, at public shareholders' expense. Deal agreements often require a "majority of minority" vote—a majority of non-insider shareholders must approve—to protect public shareholders.

## When dual-class ends

Dual-class structures often unwind over time. Founders retire or die; heirs or trustees inherit Class A shares but may lack founder vision and market pressure for unified classes increases. Some companies have voluntarily ended dual-class structures as founders stepped back (Facebook post-2021). Others maintain structures indefinitely (Berkshire, as of 2024, still has Buffett in tight control).

On founder death or transition, the structure sometimes explicitly requires sunset. The founder's Class A shares may convert to Class B, collapsing the dual-class premium. This creates a valuation event where insiders must negotiate the transition or accept conversion. Some shareholders view founder mortality as a liquidity event—an opportunity to buy at depressed prices before the successor takes over.
