*A reverse stock split is the inverse of a [stock split](/equity/stock-split/), consolidating multiple outstanding shares into fewer shares. In a 1-for-10 reverse split, ten shares become one; the share count declines by 90% and the per-share price rises proportionally. Reverse splits are typically used by companies with declining stock prices to comply with stock exchange minimum price requirements, not as a strategic maneuver.*

## How a reverse stock split works

A company trading at $0.80 per share executes a 1-for-10 reverse stock split. Before the split:

- 1 billion shares outstanding.
- Stock price: $0.80 per share.
- Market cap: $800 million.

After the split:

- 100 million shares outstanding (each shareholder's share count declines by 90%).
- Stock price: $8.00 per share (adjusted upward).
- Market cap: $800 million (unchanged).

A shareholder who owned 1,000 shares worth $800 now owns 100 shares worth $800. Their percentage ownership is identical.

## Why reverse splits happen

Reverse splits are used for one primary reason: **compliance with stock exchange minimum price requirements**. The NYSE and NASDAQ require listed companies to maintain a minimum share price (typically $1.00 per share). A company whose stock falls below that threshold is at risk of delisting.

A reverse split is a quick fix: instantly boost the price per share back above the minimum. However, it addresses only the symptom (low price) not the disease (the company's weak performance).

Some companies reverse split to:

- **Restore liquidity**: Low-priced stocks (under $0.50) have wider bid-ask spreads, fewer buyers, and less institutional interest. A reverse split can narrow the spread and restore some liquidity.
- **Improve optics**: A stock at $0.50 "looks cheap" and attracts speculation. A stock at $10 (after 1-for-20 reverse) "looks normal" and may attract more serious investors.
- **Enable a merger**: Sometimes a reverse split is a precondition for merger consideration or for the target to remain listed post-merger.

However, reverse splits are almost universally viewed negatively by the market. They are associated with failed companies and turnarounds. A company forced to reverse split its stock has essentially admitted defeat in trying to maintain a viable price level.

## Reverse split mechanics

A reverse split is executed similarly to a forward split:

1. The board proposes the ratio.
2. Shareholders vote.
3. The record date is set.
4. Shares are consolidated in shareholder accounts.
5. The stock exchange adjusts the ticker.
6. Options, [RSUs](/equity/restricted-stock-units/), [warrants](/equity/warrant/), and equity are adjusted.

From the shareholder's perspective, it is automatic. If you owned an odd number of shares (e.g., 1,050 shares in a 1-for-10 reverse split), the company cash-settles the fractional share (105 shares remaining, 5 shares cashed out).

## Tax treatment

Reverse splits are generally not taxable events, similar to forward splits. Your cost basis is proportionally multiplied.

**Example**:
- You bought 1,000 shares at $1.00 = $1,000 cost basis.
- 1-for-10 reverse split occurs.
- You now own 100 shares with a cost basis of $10 each (total $1,000).
- If you sell at $12 per share ($1,200 total), your gain is $200.

However, cash paid for fractional shares is treated as a sale of those fractional shares. If you receive $50 in cash for fractional shares, and your cost basis in those shares was $30, you have a $20 capital gain (even though you did not actually choose to sell).

## Reverse split spirals

A company in distress sometimes executes one reverse split, but the stock price continues to decline. A few years later, another reverse split is needed. Repeated reverse splits are a hallmark of a failing company and are viewed very negatively by the market. Some companies reverse split multiple times before finally going private or folding.

## Comparison to forward split

A **forward split** (2-for-1, 3-for-1) increases share count and lowers price, typically done by growing companies with high stock prices. A **reverse split** (1-for-10, 1-for-20) decreases share count and raises price, typically done by struggling companies with low stock prices.

Forward splits are viewed as positive (the company is doing well and stock is expensive). Reverse splits are viewed negatively (the company is struggling and stock is cheap).

## Shareholder approval

Most reverse splits require shareholder approval, giving shareholders a chance to vote no. However, many shareholders rationally vote in favor because the alternative (delisting) is worse. Some shareholders vote against, viewing the reverse split as a symbol of the company's failure.

## Alternatives to reverse splits

Rather than reverse split, a company can:

- **Do nothing**: Remain listed while trading below $1 (some exchanges allow this if the company meets other requirements).
- **Go private**: Execute a take-private transaction or merger at a low valuation.
- **Merge**: Combine with another company in an all-stock deal.
- **Spin off**: Separate business units to unlock value.

Reverse splits are a purely cosmetic solution and rarely address the underlying business problems that led to the low stock price.

## Notable examples

Companies including Lehman Brothers (before collapse), RadioShack (multiple reverse splits), and Sears (multiple reverse splits) have used reverse splits as a last-ditch effort to remain listed. In most cases, the company eventually delisted or went private anyway.

In contrast, Citigroup executed a 1-for-10 reverse split in 2011 post-financial crisis and successfully recovered. Reverse splits are not always a death sentence, but they are a sign of severe distress.
