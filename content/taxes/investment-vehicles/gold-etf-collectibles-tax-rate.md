## Why Physical Gold Is Classified as a Collectible

The IRS's collectibles definition under Internal Revenue Code Section 408(m)(2) includes "any precious metal" unless it is held in specific forms exempt from the rule (most notably, bullion coins minted by the U.S. Treasury). The regulation does not distinguish between gold bars held in a safe deposit box and gold held by an ETF custodian on the shareholder's behalf. If the ETF holds actual metal, the investor's economic interest in that metal triggers collectibles status, and thus the 28% rate.

The reason for this classification is historical and policy-driven. Congress has long treated bullion, art, wine, and other collectibles differently from productive assets (stocks, bonds) in the tax code. Collectibles do not generate cash flows or earnings; they are held purely for appreciation. Moreover, the market for collectibles is less transparent and more subject to valuation manipulation than securities exchanges. A 28% tax rate reflects both the view that holding pure speculative assets merits a penalty and a practical acknowledgment that collectibles portfolios generate less information for tax compliance than security sales.

## The Contrast with Mining Stocks and Futures-Based ETFs

Not all gold investments trigger the collectibles rate. The tax treatment depends entirely on the structure of the ETF.

A gold ETF that holds shares of gold mining companies, for instance, is taxed as an ordinary [equity ETF](/equity-etf/). The investor's gain is the appreciation of the mining stock, not the appreciation of metal itself. Such gains are subject to the standard [long-term capital gains tax](/long-term-capital-gain-tax/) schedule: 15% or 20% depending on income.

Similarly, a gold ETF based on futures contracts—which track the spot price of gold but do not entail physical custody—is taxed under Section 1256 of the tax code (the "60/40 rule"): 60% is taxed as long-term gain and 40% as short-term gain, resulting in an effective blended rate of around 23%, which can be lower than the 28% collectibles rate.

The key test is substance: if the ETF's assets are actual metal bars or ingots stored in a vault, the fund units are classified as interests in a collectible. If the ETF holds mining equities or derivative contracts on metal prices, no collectibles classification applies.

## Calculating and Reporting the 28% Tax

For a shareholder who held a physically-backed gold ETF for more than one year and sold it at a profit, the long-term [capital gain](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) is subject to a flat 28% rate. This rate applies regardless of the shareholder's income bracket. There is no 0%, 15%, or 20% option; the rate is fixed.

Importantly, the 28% rate applies only to the *gain*, not the entire proceeds. If an investor bought $10,000 of a gold ETF and sold it for $12,000, the taxable gain is $2,000, and the tax is $560 (28% × $2,000). The cost basis ($10,000) is recovered tax-free.

Reporting is done on IRS Form 8949 and [Schedule D](/schedule-d/). The collectibles gain must be entered on the line specifically designated for long-term collectibles (typically line 17 of Schedule D, titled "Gains on collectibles"). The long-term capital gains lines for ordinary securities (which carry the 15%/20% rates) are separate, and must not be commingled.

If a shareholder holds multiple lots of a gold ETF purchased at different times and prices, [cost basis](/cost-basis/) tracking is essential. The IRS presumes [FIFO](/fifo/) (first-in, first-out) ordering unless the shareholder affirmatively identifies which lots are being sold. Proper identification on the trade confirmation slip or prior written statement to the broker is the only way to claim a different method (such as specific ID or average cost).

## The 28% vs. 15/20% Comparison: Practical Impact

For many investors, the 28% collectibles rate is a significant drag compared to ordinary long-term rates.

Consider an investor in the 37% ordinary income bracket (high income). Their long-term capital gains tax on ordinary securities is 20%. Their long-term capital gains tax on a physically-backed gold ETF is 28%—eight percentage points higher. On a $100,000 gain, that is $8,000 in additional federal tax.

Even for investors in the 24% ordinary income bracket (long-term rate of 15%), the 28% collectibles rate is 13 points higher. On a $100,000 gain, that is $13,000 extra.

The state tax treatment compounds the issue. Many states tax long-term capital gains at ordinary rates (ranging from 5% to 12%+), and those state rates apply to gold ETFs as well, adding another layer. The total effective rate on a gold ETF in a high-tax state can exceed 35% or 40%.

This tax efficiency gap is one reason that financial advisors often recommend mining stock ETFs or sector funds as a substitute if the investor seeks exposure to gold's performance. A mining equity ETF may deliver similar returns to physical gold with lower tax drag—though it introduces [idiosyncratic risk](/idiosyncratic-risk/) (company and operational risk) that pure metal exposure does not.

## Special Rules: Inherited Gold ETFs and Charitable Gifts

If a shareholder dies holding a physically-backed gold ETF, the [cost basis](/cost-basis/) of the position "steps up" to fair market value as of the date of death (under IRC Section 1014). The heir inherits the ETF at that stepped-up basis and owes no tax on the appreciation that occurred during the decedent's lifetime. The 28% collectibles rate applies only to gains accrued *after* the heir takes ownership.

If a shareholder donates a physically-backed gold ETF to a qualified charity, no capital gains tax is due on the appreciation. The donor receives an income tax deduction for the fair market value of the donated ETF (subject to limitations based on adjusted gross income). This is one scenario where the collectibles classification is irrelevant, since no taxable gain is triggered.

A shareholder who donates an appreciated gold ETF to a charitable remainder trust can also avoid the collectibles tax while retaining a stream of payments, though the rules are complex.
