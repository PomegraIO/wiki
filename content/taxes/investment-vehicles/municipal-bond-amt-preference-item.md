*Most **municipal bond** interest is tax-free at the federal level, but certain **private-activity bonds** create an **alternative minimum tax (AMT) preference item** that can force high-income investors to pay federal tax on their muni interest despite the ostensible exemption. Understanding which bonds trigger this hidden tax, how the AMT applies, and screening for exposure are essential for large muni holders.*

## What is a Private-Activity Bond?

Most [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/) are issued by state and local governments to finance public infrastructure—roads, schools, water systems, general government operations. Interest on these general obligation and revenue bonds is exempt from federal income tax.

**Private-activity bonds** are municipal bonds issued to finance projects that provide a private benefit, not a purely public benefit. Examples include:

- Housing developments financed via [municipal bonds](/municipal-bond/) for residential mortgages
- Student loan financing bonds
- Bonds to finance pollution-control equipment used by a private company
- Bonds to build or renovate sports stadiums or arenas leased to private teams
- Bonds to finance industrial parks or other private business facilities

These bonds are still issued by state or local authorities and technically carry a municipal designation, but the IRS considers them "private-activity" because the economic benefit flows substantially to a private entity rather than the general public.

Critically, **private-activity bonds are subject to federal income tax and create AMT preference items**. The interest is not tax-free, and holding them can trigger the alternative minimum tax.

## The Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) Framework

The AMT is a parallel tax system. The IRS calculates both your regular income tax and your alternative minimum taxable income (AMTI). If AMTI produces a higher tax under the AMT formula, you pay the AMT instead (or, more precisely, you pay regular tax plus AMT to make up the difference).

The AMT process:

1. Start with your regular [adjusted gross income](/income-statement/).
2. Add back certain "preference items"—deductions or exclusions that receive preferential tax treatment.
3. Calculate AMTI.
4. Apply the AMT exemption (roughly $85,000 for married filers, $59,000 for singles in 2024; these amounts adjust annually).
5. Calculate tentative minimum tax at a 26% or 28% rate.
6. Compare to regular income tax. Pay whichever is higher.

Private-activity municipal bond interest is one of the largest AMT preference items for individuals, because it's a large dollar amount that is nominally tax-exempt but gets added back for AMT purposes.

## How Municipal Bond AMT Preference Works

When you own a private-activity municipal bond and receive interest, that interest does **not** reduce your federal taxable income. You exclude it from line 1 of your [Schedule D](/schedule-d/) or wherever normal municipal bond interest appears on your tax return.

However, when calculating AMT, you add that same interest back in as a preference item. This means:

- For regular tax purposes: your income is reduced by the bond interest (tax benefit).
- For AMT purposes: your income is increased by the bond interest (no tax benefit).

**Example:** You earn $200,000 in W-2 wages and receive $20,000 in interest from private-activity municipal bonds.

Regular taxable income: $200,000 (the $20,000 is excluded).

AMTI: $200,000 + $20,000 preference item = $220,000.

If $220,000 AMTI, after applying AMT exemptions and the AMT rate, produces a higher tax than your regular $200,000 calculation, you're subject to AMT and owe tax on some or all of the $20,000 muni interest.

## Calculating AMT Exposure

The full calculation is complex, but the principle is straightforward. High-income investors with large private-activity muni holdings often find that the preference items push them into AMT territory.

**Simplified example:** Married filing jointly with $250,000 in income and $30,000 in private-activity muni interest.

AMTI before preference items: $250,000.
Add back private-activity muni interest: +$30,000 → $280,000 AMTI.
Apply 2024 AMT exemption (married): –$85,000 → $195,000 AMT base.
AMT at 26% (on first portion): $50,700.

If regular tax on $250,000 is, say, $48,000, then AMT of $50,700 applies, and you owe an additional $2,700 due to the muni preference.

The impact scales with both the size of your muni holdings and your overall income level.

## Identifying Private-Activity Bonds in Your Portfolio

Not every muni bond prospectus labels itself "private-activity" on the cover. However, the official statement or prospectus contains this information. A few strategies to identify exposure:

- **Read the "Official Statement" (OS).** The document will state whether the bonds are subject to the federal tax exemption under Section 103 and whether they are private-activity bonds subject to AMT.
- **Check IRS forms.** The Form 1098-T or state equivalent may list private-activity muni interest separately.
- **Use fund documentation.** If you hold a municipal bond fund or ETF, the prospectus or fact sheet will disclose the percentage of holdings that are private-activity bonds.
- **Ask your broker or fund manager.** They can provide a breakdown of your holdings by bond type.

Private-activity bonds often carry higher yields than comparable general obligation bonds, precisely because the tax exemption is unreliable for high-income investors. This yield differential compensates the market for the AMT risk.

## Screening and Portfolio Management

For investors concerned about AMT, a straightforward approach is to screen out private-activity bonds entirely and hold only general obligation and revenue bonds financed for essential public services (roads, schools, water, sewer, general government operations).

General obligation bonds are rarely subject to AMT. Revenue bonds for public utilities, ports, or colleges are typically safe. It's the housing bonds, student loan bonds, sports facility bonds, and industrial development bonds that require scrutiny.

Some muni bond funds explicitly market themselves as "AMT-free" or exclude private-activity holdings. These are suitable for high-income investors or those expecting to breach the AMT threshold.

For those already holding a substantial muni portfolio, an estimate of potential AMT exposure should factor into broader tax planning. If you're within a few thousand dollars of the AMT threshold, reducing private-activity muni holdings could push you back into regular tax territory, netting a material tax savings.

## State Tax Implications

Private-activity bonds may also be subject to state income tax, depending on the state. While interest on general obligation bonds issued by your home state is typically exempt from state tax (and sometimes federal tax), private-activity bonds may lose that exemption. Always check your state's guidance on whether the specific private-activity bond is state-tax-exempt.

## Municipal Bond Selection and Risk

The AMT preference item is not the only risk in municipal bonds. [Credit risk](/credit-risk/), [call risk](/call-risk/), and [interest-rate risk](/interest-rate-risk/) all apply. Private-activity bonds may also carry issuer-specific risks because the issuer (e.g., a housing authority or sports authority) is financially dependent on the underlying project revenues.

Balancing the yield advantage of private-activity bonds against the AMT risk and credit risk requires a clear understanding of your tax situation and risk tolerance.
