*The **Form 1099-INT** is a tax form issued by [brokers](/broker/), banks, and investment companies reporting [interest](/interest-rate/) income you earned during the year. Interest is taxed as [ordinary income](/tax-bracket-investor/) at your [marginal tax rate](/marginal-tax-rate-investor/), not at preferential [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) rates. Unlike [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/), there is no preferential treatment for [interest](/interest-rate/) income, making [bonds](/bond/) and savings accounts less tax-efficient than [stocks](/stock/).*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

For [dividend](/dividend/) income, see [Form 1099-DIV](/1099-div/). For brokerage sales, see [Form 1099-B](/1099-b/).

## What counts as [interest](/interest-rate/) income on 1099-INT

**Bank [interest](/interest-rate/)**: Savings accounts, money market accounts, CDs all report [interest](/interest-rate/) on 1099-INT.

**[Bond](/bond/) [interest](/interest-rate/)**: [Corporate bonds](/bond/), [Treasury bonds](/bond/), [municipal bonds](/bond/) (in some cases) report [interest](/interest-rate/) on 1099-INT.

**Loan [interest](/interest-rate/) received**: If you lend money to someone and charge [interest](/interest-rate/), it is [ordinary income](/tax-bracket-investor/).

**Investment account [interest](/interest-rate/)**: [Brokers](/broker/) report earned [interest](/interest-rate/) on margin accounts, dividend reinvestment plans, and other accounts.

## Tax treatment: [ordinary income](/tax-bracket-investor/)

[Interest](/interest-rate/) income is always taxed at your [marginal tax rate](/marginal-tax-rate-investor/), the same rate as wages. There is no preferential treatment like [long-term capital gains](/long-term-capital-gain-tax/) (0%-20%) or [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/) (0%-15%).

Example: You earn $120,000 in wages and $5,000 in [interest](/interest-rate/) income. Your total [taxable income](/marginal-tax-rate-investor/) is $125,000. The $5,000 [interest](/interest-rate/) is taxed at your marginal rate—likely 22% or 24%—costing $1,100-$1,200 in federal tax.

The same $5,000 in [qualified dividend](/qualified-dividend/) income would be taxed at 15%, costing $750. This is why [stocks](/stock/) with [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/) are more tax-efficient than [bonds](/bond/) with [interest](/interest-rate/).

## Multiple 1099-INTs

If you have [interest](/interest-rate/) income from multiple banks or [brokers](/broker/), you will receive multiple 1099-INT forms. You must aggregate all [interest](/interest-rate/) income on a single Schedule B on your tax return.

## [Treasury](/interest-rate/) [interest](/interest-rate/) and state tax

Federal [Treasury bonds](/bond/) produce [interest](/interest-rate/) that is exempt from state income tax. This is reported separately on the 1099-INT (Box 3 for US Savings Bond [interest](/interest-rate/)). When you report [Treasury](/interest-rate/) [interest](/interest-rate/) on your state return, you may be able to exclude it.

Corporate [bonds](/bond/) and other [interest](/interest-rate/) are subject to both federal and state tax.

## Series I Savings Bond [interest](/interest-rate/)

Series I Savings Bonds do not issue 1099-INT until you redeem them. The [interest](/interest-rate/) accrues and is taxed in the year of redemption. This is a way to defer [interest](/interest-rate/) taxation: buy a bond today, report no [interest](/interest-rate/) until you cash it years later.

## Timing and accrued [interest](/interest-rate/)

The 1099-INT reports [interest](/interest-rate/) paid or credited during the calendar year, not [interest](/interest-rate/) earned. If you buy a [bond](/bond/) and it accrues [interest](/interest-rate/) but does not pay until January of the next year, the 1099-INT for the purchase year will not include that [interest](/interest-rate/).

## Estimated tax payments

If you receive substantial [interest](/interest-rate/) income (and little or no tax withholding), you may need to make estimated tax payments quarterly to avoid underpayment penalties.

## No tax withholding on [interest](/interest-rate/)

Banks and [brokers](/broker/) generally do not withhold tax from [interest](/interest-rate/) income (unlike wages, which are subject to payroll withholding). You are responsible for reporting the [interest](/interest-rate/) and paying the tax when due.

This can be a surprise for retirees living on [interest](/interest-rate/) income who expected automatic withholding.

## Contrast with [dividends](/dividend/) and [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/)

| Income type | Tax rate | Preferential rates | Tax efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[Interest](/interest-rate/)** | Ordinary (up to 37%) | None | Poor |
| **[Ordinary dividends](/ordinary-dividend/)** | Ordinary (up to 37%) | None | Poor |
| **[Qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/)** | 0%-20% | Yes | Good |
| **[Long-term capital gains](/long-term-capital-gain-tax/)** | 0%-20% | Yes | Good |

This difference drives much of [tax-efficient investing strategy](/tax-bracket-investor/): favor [stocks](/stock/) (which pay [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/)) over [bonds](/bond/) (which pay [interest](/interest-rate/)) in taxable accounts.
