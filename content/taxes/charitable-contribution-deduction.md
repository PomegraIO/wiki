*The **charitable contribution deduction** allows taxpayers to deduct donations to qualified charities, nonprofits, and educational institutions. You must [itemize deductions](/itemized-deduction-investor/) to claim them. Donations of cash are generally deductible at 50% of adjusted gross income; appreciated securities receive higher limits (60%) if held long-term. Wealthy investors often use appreciated [stocks](/stock/) and [donor-advised funds](/charitable-contribution-deduction/) to maximize deductions while optimizing [capital gains taxes](/capital-gains-tax-investor/).*

## How the deduction works

You donate $50,000 in [stock](/stock/) to a qualified charity. Your [adjusted gross income](/tax-bracket-investor/) is $100,000.

The deduction limit for appreciated securities is 30% [AGI](/tax-bracket-investor/) = $30,000. You can deduct $30,000 this year; the remaining $20,000 carries forward to the next year (up to 5 years forward).

At your [marginal rate](/marginal-tax-rate-investor/) of 24%, the $30,000 deduction saves $7,200 in federal tax.

Moreover, because you donated the [stock](/stock/) (not sold it), you avoided [capital gains tax](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) on the appreciation. If the [stock](/stock/) had appreciated $30,000, [capital gains tax](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) would have cost $4,500 (at 15%). Total benefit: $7,200 deduction + $4,500 gain avoided = **$11,700 total tax benefit** on a $50,000 gift.

## Appreciated vs. cash donations

**Cash**: Deductible at up to 50% [AGI](/tax-bracket-investor/). Simpler to administer.

**Appreciated securities** (held >1 year): Deductible at 30% [AGI](/tax-bracket-investor/) for capital gain property. However, if the appreciated securities were held ≤1 year (short-term), they are deductible at 50% [AGI](/tax-bracket-investor/) (same as cash) but you can deduct the cost basis, not fair value.

The appreciated securities path is more tax-efficient: you avoid [capital gains tax](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) and receive a deduction.

## Qualified charities

To deduct, the charity must be a qualified organization:
- 501(c)(3) nonprofit
- Educational institution
- Church or religious organization
- Hospital or medical research organization
- Qualified public charity

Donations to political campaigns, nonprofits without 501(c)(3) status, or foreign charities are not deductible.

The IRS maintains a list of qualified charities; check before donating.

## Carryforward of excess donations

If your [charitable contributions](/charitable-contribution-deduction/) exceed the [AGI](/tax-bracket-investor/) limit, you carry the excess forward up to 5 years. Each year, the carried-forward amount is deductible subject to the annual limit.

Example: Donate $100,000 this year. [AGI](/tax-bracket-investor/) = $150,000. Limit = 50% × $150,000 = $75,000. Deduct $75,000 this year; carry forward $25,000 to next year.

Next year, [AGI](/tax-bracket-investor/) = $160,000. Limit = $80,000. Carryforward $25,000 fits; deduct the full amount.

## Donor-advised fund

A **donor-advised fund** (DAF) is a charitable account where you donate funds or appreciated securities, receive an immediate deduction, but can recommend distributions to charities over years.

Advantages:
- Immediate deduction even if you donate later
- Avoid [capital gains tax](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) on appreciated securities
- Simplify charitable giving (one donation, multiple charities)

A DAF at Fidelity, Schwab, or Vanguard is commonly used by investors.

## Bunching donations for itemization

If your donations are just below the [itemization threshold](/itemized-deduction-investor/), you can "bunch" donations: donate $30,000 in year 1 (itemize), then $0 in year 2 (take [standard deduction](/standard-deduction-investor/)). A DAF makes bunching easy.

## Qualified charitable distributions for retirees

If you are 70½+ with a traditional IRA, you can transfer up to $100,000 per year to a qualified charity **without** recognizing [income](/tax-bracket-investor/). This is a qualified charitable distribution, and it is not deductible (you already got the deduction via [IRA contributions](/hedge-fund/)), but it avoids [income tax](/tax-bracket-investor/) on distributions.

## Donating appreciated securities: a clean example

You bought Apple [stock](/stock/) for $10,000 (basis). It is now worth $50,000. You want to give $50,000 to charity.

**Option A: Sell, then give cash**
- Sell [stock](/stock/) for $50,000: [capital gain](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) = $40,000
- [Capital gains tax](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) at 15% = $6,000
- Donate $44,000 in cash (you sold for $50,000, paid $6,000 tax, left with $44,000)
- Deduction: $44,000
- Total tax benefit: roughly $10,000

**Option B: Donate the [stock](/stock/) directly**
- Donate $50,000 in appreciated [stock](/stock/)
- No [capital gains tax](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) (charity is exempt)
- Deduction: up to $30,000 this year ([AGI](/tax-bracket-investor/) limit), carry forward
- Total tax benefit: roughly $18,000 (deduction over 2-3 years + avoided gains)

Donating appreciated securities is far more tax-efficient.

## Fraud and substantiation

Always get a receipt from the charity. For donations >$250, you need written acknowledgment from the charity. Donations are a common audit area; keep records of fair value (for securities, the price on donation date).
