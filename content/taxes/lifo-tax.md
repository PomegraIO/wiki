*The **LIFO** method (Last In, First Out) is a [cost basis](/cost-basis/) approach where the newest [tax lot](/tax-lot/) is sold first. LIFO can be more tax-efficient than [FIFO](/fifo-tax/) when recent purchases are at higher prices, allowing you to realise smaller gains. It is most useful during volatile or rising markets, though it requires explicit election and careful documentation.*

## How LIFO works

Suppose you own 200 [shares](/stock/) acquired in two [lots](/tax-lot/):
- **Lot 1** (January 2010): 100 [shares](/stock/) at $50 = $5,000 cost
- **Lot 2** (January 2024): 100 [shares](/stock/) at $150 = $15,000 cost

Current price: $200 per share. You want to sell 100 [shares](/stock/).

Under LIFO, you sell **Lot 2 first** (the newest). Your gain is $200 - $150 = $50 per share × 100 = **$5,000 gain**.

Under [FIFO](/fifo-tax/), you would sell Lot 1, generating a gain of $150 × 100 = $15,000. LIFO saves you $10,000 in taxable income—a significant advantage.

## When LIFO shines

LIFO is most beneficial in three scenarios:

**Rising markets**. When prices generally trend upward, newer [lots](/tax-lot/) are at higher prices. Selling them first generates smaller gains.

**Dollar-cost averaging**. If you steadily buy into a position over years, older [lots](/tax-lot/) will be at lower prices; selling newer [lots](/tax-lot/) (at higher prices) minimises gains.

**Market corrections**. In a downturn, if you hold [lots](/tax-lot/) purchased at all different prices, LIFO lets you use recent losses to offset old gains. When a [stock](/stock/) rallies after a crash, you can sell recent [lots](/tax-lot/) at near-recovery prices while preserving old, low-cost [lots](/tax-lot/).

## When LIFO is poor

**Falling markets**. If newer [lots](/tax-lot/) are at lower prices than old [lots](/tax-lot/) (because you bought at the peak), LIFO forces you to sell losers or breakeven positions, realising the smallest gains but not harvesting the largest losses.

**Stable or declining holdings**. If you own a [stock](/stock/) that has fallen or flatlined and you bought at various prices, LIFO offers no advantage.

## Election and requirements

LIFO is not automatic. You must affirmatively elect it with your [broker](/broker/), usually via written request. Once elected, it applies to all transactions in that security within that account for that tax year and future years. Switching methods or accounts requires IRS Form 3115 (Application for Change in Accounting Method), which can be burdensome.

**Important caveat**: LIFO is not available for [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/). It is used mainly for individual [stocks](/stock/) and some commodities. Check with your [broker](/broker/) if you are unsure whether LIFO is available for your holding.

## Comparison to FIFO and specific identification

| Method | Simplicity | Flexibility | Tax Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[FIFO](/fifo-tax/)** | High | None (default) | Often poor |
| **[Specific identification](/specific-identification-basis/)** | Medium | Excellent | Excellent |
| **LIFO** | High | Medium | Often good |
| **[Average cost](/average-cost-basis/)** | Very high | None | Moderate |

LIFO is a good middle ground: it is automatic once elected, offering more flexibility than FIFO without requiring a written instruction at each sale. However, [specific identification](/specific-identification-basis/) offers the greatest tax efficiency if you are willing to manage it.

## Accounting and inventory

LIFO is an inventory accounting method borrowed from inventory valuation. In inflationary periods, LIFO can provide real tax savings for businesses. For individual securities, the benefit is more limited but still meaningful.
