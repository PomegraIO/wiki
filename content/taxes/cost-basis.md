*Your **cost basis** is the adjusted purchase price of an investment, used to calculate your [capital gain](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) when you sell. The amount you paid for an asset minus any adjustments (dividends, splits, return of capital) is the starting point; the difference between your sale price and your cost basis is your taxable gain. The method you use to calculate basis can make a six-figure difference in your tax bill.*

## How basis works

When you buy a [stock](/stock/) for $100 and sell it for $150, your [capital gain](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) is $50. That $100 purchase price is your cost basis. If you bought it for $100 and sell it for $80, your loss is $20.

Cost basis is not static. If you buy a [stock](/stock/) for $100 and it pays a $10 [dividend](/dividend/) that you reinvest, your new basis becomes $110 (assuming the dividend was not a [return of capital](/ordinary-dividend/)). If the [stock](/stock/) splits 2-for-1, your basis per share is halved. These adjustments keep your basis aligned with economic reality.

## Adjustments to basis

Several events increase or decrease your cost basis:

**Reinvested dividends**. When you reinvest [dividend](/dividend/) payments to buy more [shares](/stock/), your basis in those new shares is the [dividend](/dividend/) amount you paid; and your basis in your original [shares](/stock/) is unchanged.

**Stock splits**. If a [stock](/stock/) splits 2-for-1, your basis per share is halved (but your total basis in the position remains the same).

**Return of capital**. Some distributions, especially from REITs or natural resource partnerships, are not [dividends](/dividend/) but returns of your capital. These reduce your cost basis; if the return exceeds your basis, the excess is a [capital gain](/capital-gains-tax-investor/).

**Stock dividends**. If you receive new [shares](/stock/) as a [dividend](/dividend/) (rare), your basis is adjusted across all your [shares](/stock/).

**Merger or reorganization**. If your holding is acquired or merged, your basis typically carries forward (carryover basis).

## Choosing a method

When you own multiple [lots](/tax-lot/) of the same [stock](/stock/) at different prices—bought at $50, then at $80, then at $120—selling triggers a question: which lot am I selling? The answer determines your gain.

- **[Specific identification](/specific-identification-basis/)**. You explicitly choose which lot to sell, usually the highest-cost one (to minimize gain). This requires broker instruction and documentation.
- **[FIFO (First In, First Out)](/fifo-tax/)**. You are deemed to sell the oldest lot first. Often the worst choice if you bought at lower prices long ago.
- **[LIFO (Last In, First Out)](/lifo-tax/)**. You are deemed to sell the newest lot first. Useful if recent purchases were at higher prices.
- **[Average cost](/average-cost-basis/)**. You average all your costs across all lots and sell at that average. Simple but not always tax-optimal.

Most brokers default to FIFO, which is often not tax-efficient. The IRS requires you to specify your method, and you must use it consistently for a given [stock](/stock/). Switching methods requires IRS approval.

## Importance of documentation

Your [broker](/broker/) tracks your cost basis and reports it to the IRS on [Form 8949](/form-8949/). For securities acquired after 2011 in a US account, brokers are required to report your basis. For older securities or inherited accounts, you may need to provide your own cost basis documentation. Keep records of all purchases, [dividends](/dividend/), and adjustments.

## Inherited basis: step-up

When you inherit an investment, you do not take on the decedent's cost basis. Instead, you receive a [step-up in basis](/step-up-in-basis/)—your new basis is the fair market value on the date of death. This is an enormous tax advantage: if someone held a [stock](/stock/) worth $1,000 that had cost them $100, you inherit it at a $1,000 basis, erasing all embedded gains. You could sell it immediately with no tax.

## Recordkeeping and life events

Cost basis tracking is critical. Use your broker's cost-basis reports; do not guess. If you transfer an account or change brokers, confirm that your cost basis transfers accurately. If you sell an inherited asset, confirm you used the stepped-up basis, not the original basis.
