*The **gift tax filing requirement** is the obligation to report gifts that exceed the [annual exclusion](/wiki/annual-exclusion-gift-tax/) to the IRS, typically on Form 709, and the effect that reporting has on lifetime gift and [estate tax](/wiki/estate-tax/) exemption. Most donors never owe gift tax, but the filing requirement can trigger even when tax is zero.*

## When Form 709 must be filed

The gift tax filing requirement is straightforward in structure but requires careful application. If a taxpayer gives more than the [annual exclusion](/wiki/annual-exclusion-gift-tax/) amount to any individual in a calendar year, Form 709 must be filed. For 2024, the annual exclusion is $18,000. A gift of $20,000 to one child triggers a filing requirement; a gift of $17,500 to ten children does not.

The annual exclusion applies per recipient, per year. Married couples who file jointly can each give $18,000 per recipient — a total of $36,000 to each child in 2024 — without filing. If only one spouse gives, and the amount exceeds $18,000, Form 709 is required.

The filing requirement exists even when no tax is owed. A taxpayer with a large lifetime exemption (the federal lifetime amount was $13.61 million per person in 2024) can report gifts on Form 709 that consume exemption but incur zero tax liability. The form serves as the official record that the gift was made and the exemption was used.

## How the annual exclusion and lifetime exemption interact

The distinction between the annual exclusion and the [lifetime exemption](/wiki/lifetime-exemption-amount/) is fundamental to gift tax filing obligations. The annual exclusion is a per-recipient, per-year allowance that vanishes if unused — a taxpayer cannot carry forward unused annual exclusions or gift $36,000 to a child in year one and $0 in year two to "save" the second year's exclusion.

The lifetime exemption, by contrast, is cumulative. Every dollar of taxable gifts (those exceeding annual exclusions) in excess of the lifetime exemption in any year reduces the remaining lifetime exemption by that amount. If a taxpayer files Form 709 reporting a $100,000 gift to a child in 2024, the $18,000 annual exclusion is consumed, and the remaining $82,000 is applied against the lifetime exemption. Assuming the taxpayer has the full $13.61 million available, they now have $13.528 million remaining.

This relationship makes Form 709 a critical estate planning document. The IRS uses copies on file to verify that the [estate tax](/wiki/estate-tax/) return (Form 706, filed by the executor after death) is accurately reporting the lifetime gifts that must be included in the taxable estate under [step-up in basis](/wiki/step-up-in-basis/) rules and adjusted gross estate calculations.

## Form 709 structure and line-by-line obligation

Form 709 is issued in two parts: Part 1 covers gifts of present interests (typically, outright gifts), and Part 2 addresses gifts of future interests (gifts in trust or conditional gifts). Most donors file Part 1 only.

Part 1 requires donors to list each gift: the recipient's name and address, the date of the gift, the description of the property, and the value at the time of the gift. For securities, the value is usually the closing price on the date of the gift; for real estate or other hard assets, a qualified appraisal may be required if the value exceeds certain thresholds.

The form then calculates: total gifts in the year, minus annual exclusions, yielding taxable gifts for the year. If the taxpayer has made prior-year gifts and filed prior Form 709s, the form must reference the prior-year exemption used, recalculating the running lifetime exemption balance.

## Valuation and discounts

Proper valuation is the most contentious aspect of gift tax filing. The IRS does not care what the donor claims a gift is worth — it is bound by [fair market value](/wiki/fair-value/), the price at which property would sell between a willing buyer and seller, neither under pressure.

For publicly traded securities, valuation is mechanical: the closing price on the date of gift, or an average of opening and closing prices if that is more reasonable. For closely held businesses or partnership interests, valuers often apply discounts for lack of marketability (DLOM) or lack of control (DLOC). A 40% discount for a minority interest in a closely held business can be aggressive; the IRS scrutinizes these aggressively.

The IRS can audit gift tax returns up to three years after filing, or indefinitely if the return understates the value of a gift by more than 25%. Understating value is a red flag; donors and their advisors must be conservative and document valuations carefully.

## Married couple elections and splitting gifts

Married couples can elect gift-splitting on Form 709, which allows each spouse to be treated as having made half of the gifts reported by either spouse. This election must be filed on Form 709; the election is effective only if both spouses file, even if only one made the gifts.

Splitting allows a couple to double the annual exclusion ($36,000 per child in 2024) and is nearly universal practice. It is particularly valuable because the election does not require the non-gifting spouse to spend down their own exemption; the IRS treats the entire gift as split automatically.

The election can be revoked on a subsequent amended Form 709, if filed before the statute of limitations expires. However, once a couple files Form 709 claiming split gifts, the IRS expects consistency in future years.

## Failing to file Form 709

The consequences of failing to file when required depend on whether tax was actually owed. If a taxpayer made reportable gifts but had sufficient lifetime exemption to cover them, and they failed to file Form 709, the IRS may impose a penalty (typically up to 5% per month of failure, capped at 25% of the tax owed, or a negligence penalty of 20% if the return was filed late). If tax was owed and the return was not filed, the penalty and interest compound significantly.

More importantly, a gift that was never reported on Form 709 is not documented against the lifetime exemption. If the taxpayer dies and the executor files Form 706, they must either report the unreported gift (triggering estate tax liability) or exclude it from the estate (inviting an IRS audit and penalties).

## State gift taxes and coordination

Five U.S. states impose separate gift taxes: Connecticut, Delaware, Louisiana, North Carolina, and Tennessee. Federal gift tax filing (Form 709) does not automatically satisfy state requirements. A donor in Connecticut must file Connecticut's gift return even if federal Form 709 is filed. State annual exclusions and exemptions are often lower than federal amounts, requiring separate planning.

Canada and other countries impose different gift tax regimes; U.S. citizens abroad must coordinate their filing obligations across jurisdictions.
