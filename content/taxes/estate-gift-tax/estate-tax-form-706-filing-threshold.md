*The **Form 706** estate tax return is required when a decedent's gross estate exceeds the federal exemption threshold — currently over $13 million per person — but estates smaller than that may also need to file to preserve the estate-tax exemption amount for the surviving spouse, a maneuver called portability. Filing deadlines and thresholds shift with law changes, so the answer to "must I file?" depends on both size and marital status.*

## The Federal Exemption and Filing Requirement

The primary rule for **Form 706** filing is straightforward: if your taxable estate at death exceeds the federal exemption amount, you must file. For 2024, that threshold is $13.61 million per individual. Married couples can combine exemptions to $27.22 million if both estates are properly planned and the surviving spouse survives to claim [portability](/estate-tax/).

But "gross estate" is larger than most people expect. It includes:
- Probate assets (real estate, securities, bank accounts).
- Life insurance death benefits (even policies the decedent no longer owned).
- Retirement accounts with named beneficiaries.
- Certain gifts made within 3 years of death (for life insurance and gifts in contemplation of death).
- Durable powers of attorney assets and revocable trusts.

A seemingly modest estate — a home worth $800,000, a [401(k)](/401k-plan/) with $2 million, a life insurance policy for $5 million, and investment accounts totaling $3 million — totals $10.8 million and may not require filing. But if the life insurance jumps to $7 million, the threshold is crossed, and Form 706 becomes mandatory.

## Portability and Below-Threshold Filing

The **portability election** allows a surviving spouse to inherit the deceased spouse's unused exemption, effectively doubling the exemption for the survivor's future [estate-tax](/estate-tax/) liability. This is enormously valuable in blended families or when one spouse dies with a much smaller estate.

To claim portability, the estate **must file Form 706**, even if the decedent's gross estate falls below the filing threshold. The IRS treats the filing as an election; a return not filed means portability is lost forever.

Example: A widow with a $2 million estate remarries. Her first husband's estate was $5 million (below the 2024 threshold) but included no Form 706. The $8.61 million of unused exemption is now gone and cannot be recovered, costing the widow's heirs substantial federal tax. Had Form 706 been filed when the first husband died, that exemption would have flowed to her.

Because of portability, many estates below the exemption threshold still file Form 706 as a precautionary measure — the cost of filing (typically $2,000–$5,000 in professional fees) is small insurance against losing millions in tax exemption.

## Deadlines and Extensions

Form 706 is due **nine months after the decedent's death**. The IRS grants automatic extensions for an additional six months (15 months total from death) if a timely request is made. Extensions are routine and widely granted.

Missing the deadline — even by a day — forfeits portability. The IRS's position is strict: no retroactive relief for late portability elections. This makes the nine-month window a hard deadline for estates seeking to preserve unused exemptions.

## Generation-Skipping Tax and Form 709

Some estates below the exemption threshold must still file Form 706 if the decedent made generation-skipping transfers (gifts or bequests that skip a generation, typically to grandchildren). Form 709 (gift tax return) information can be incorporated into Form 706, but the return is required to allocate and report GST exemptions.

Failure to allocate GST exemptions on a timely return can trigger harsh deemed-allocation rules, making Form 706 filing necessary even when no federal estate tax is owed.

## State Estate and Inheritance Taxes

Roughly 17 U.S. states impose separate estate or inheritance taxes with much lower thresholds than the federal exemption. New York's threshold is $6.94 million (2024); Massachusetts, Oregon, and Washington State are roughly similar. Connecticut tops out at $12.92 million, and Illinois has no estate tax.

A decedent may file no federal Form 706 (because the estate is below $13.61 million) but still be required to file a state return. Each state has its own rules, filing deadlines, and portability provisions (if any). The interaction between federal and state requirements is a common source of confusion.

## Timeline and Practical Steps

**Within days of death:**
- Gather asset inventories and current valuations.
- Identify life insurance policies and beneficiaries.
- Locate deeds, brokerage statements, and retirement account documentation.

**Within 3–6 months:**
- Engage an estate attorney and tax professional.
- Calculate gross estate value.
- Determine whether filing is required federally and in any relevant states.

**Within 9 months:**
- File Form 706 (if required or elected) with the IRS.
- File state returns as needed.
- Claim extensions if additional time is needed for valuations or documents.

Many estates benefit from professional appraisals of real estate, closely held businesses, or art; these can take 4–8 weeks and should be started early.

## Common Mistakes

- **Not filing for portability.** The single most expensive mistake: losing hundreds of thousands in unused exemption because no Form 706 was filed.
- **Undervaluing assets.** The IRS scrutinizes estate valuations. Inflated values erode the exemption; understated values invite audit and penalties.
- **Missing state deadlines.** Some states impose earlier or different deadlines than the federal nine-month rule.
- **Failing to account for gifts.** Lifetime gifts above the annual exclusion ($18,000 per recipient in 2024) use exemption and must be reported, even if the estate is below threshold.
