*A **charitable bequest deduction** is the right to exclude the full fair-market value of assets left to qualifying charities from the taxable estate, reducing federal [estate tax](/estate-tax/) dollar-for-dollar. Any amount transferred outright to a qualified charitable organization—whether in a will, trust, or during life—is simply subtracted from the taxable estate before applying the estate [tax bracket](/tax-bracket-investor/). This deduction is unlimited, making charitable bequests the primary estate tax planning tool for donors with large portfolios and philanthropic intent.*

## Eligibility and Qualifying Organizations

The charitable bequest deduction applies only to transfers to organizations that the IRS recognizes as qualifying charities. These include 501(c)(3) nonprofits (educational, religious, scientific, medical institutions), certain veterans' organizations under IRC §501(c)(19), certain fraternal societies under §501(c)(8) that use gifts exclusively for charitable purposes, and government entities (states, counties, municipalities) that hold the gift for exclusively public purposes.

Gifts to private individuals, for-profit entities, or religious organizations with restrictions that run counter to public policy do not qualify. For example, a bequest to a private foundation can trigger the charitable deduction, but if the donor is a substantial contributor and exerts control, the IRS may scrutinize whether the foundation's distributions are sufficiently charitable. Virtually all mainstream 501(c)(3) organizations—universities, museums, hospitals, public charities—will satisfy the requirement.

## How the Deduction Works in Estate Tax Calculation

The [estate tax](/estate-tax/) is calculated on the net taxable estate after applicable deductions. The formula is:

**Gross Estate** (all assets at death) − **Deductions** (including charitable bequests, funeral/admin costs, debts) = **Taxable Estate** × **Applicable rate** = **Estate Tax**

If an estate has a gross value of $15 million and the decedent leaves $5 million to a qualified charity via bequest, the deduction reduces the taxable estate by $5 million, leaving $10 million subject to tax. Because the federal estate-tax exemption is $13.61 million per person in 2024 (and scheduled to drop significantly in 2026), a $5 million charitable bequest might reduce the estate's tax liability from substantial to zero or minimal.

The deduction is taken dollar-for-dollar at full fair-market value, not a percentage or partial reduction. This means a $1 million bequest to charity reduces the estate by exactly $1 million.

## Timing: Lifetime and Testamentary Gifts

The charitable bequest deduction applies to both lifetime donations and bequests in a will or trust. If a donor transfers property to a qualified charity during life, the transfer is a completed gift that reduces the taxable estate. If the transfer occurs via will or revocable trust, the deduction is claimed on the estate tax return (Form 706) at the time of death.

Lifetime charitable gifts also benefit from the annual gift-tax exclusion (currently $18,000 per donor per recipient in 2024) and the lifetime gifting exemption. Bequests in a will or irrevocable trust avoid lifetime gift-tax consequences entirely because the transfer is testamentary—no gift tax applies, only estate tax, and the charitable deduction erases the estate-tax consequence for the amount transferred.

This structural difference means bequests are often simpler from a compliance perspective: a donor names a charity as beneficiary in their will, and the estate deduction follows automatically without any separate gift-tax return filing.

## Form 706 Filing and Documentation

When a decedent's gross estate exceeds the exemption threshold (or when charitable deductions or other factors trigger a filing requirement), the executor must file Form 706, the federal estate tax return, within nine months of death (with possible extensions). Schedule O of Form 706 is dedicated to reporting charitable, educational, and religious gifts. Executors must list:

- The name and address of each qualified recipient
- The character of the property transferred (cash, securities, real estate, etc.)
- The date of the transfer
- The fair-market value as of the date of death (or the alternate valuation date)

Form 706 instructions specify that the IRS wants enough detail to verify that the recipient is indeed a qualifying organization. Providing a Tax ID number (EIN) for the organization is required.

## Valuation and Appraisals

For the charitable deduction to apply, the property value must be accurate as of the date of death (or, if elected, the alternate valuation date six months later). For liquid assets (cash, publicly traded stock), valuation is straightforward—use the closing price on the date of death. For illiquid or hard-to-value property (real estate, art, private company interests), the estate must obtain a qualified appraisal performed by a qualified appraiser.

The IRS scrutinizes valuations on Schedule O because overstating fair-market value directly overstates the deduction and thus understates tax liability. If an appraiser values a piece of real estate at $10 million to the estate's benefit (reducing taxable estate) but the IRS challenges it as $6 million, the disallowed deduction of $4 million flows through to the taxable estate and increases the estate tax owed plus potential penalties.

## Interaction with Marital and Other Deductions

The charitable bequest deduction is independent of the [marital deduction](/estate-tax/), which allows unlimited transfers between spouses without tax. An estate can use both: leave some property to a spouse (marital deduction) and some to charity (charitable deduction), with each reducing the taxable estate separately.

An executor must also account for the charitable deduction in relation to debts, expenses, and state-law elective shares. If a decedent's state allows a surviving spouse to elect a forced share (against a will that leaves everything to charity), the election will reduce the amount available to the charity and thus reduce the charitable deduction to the amount actually received.

## Charitable Remainder Trusts and Income Interests

Not all charitable transfers are outright. A **Charitable Remainder Trust** (CRT) can split ownership: the donor (or beneficiary) receives an income stream for life or a term of years, and the remainder goes to charity. The donor receives a charitable deduction for the present value of the charity's remainder interest, calculated using IRS discount-rate tables. This allows a donor to achieve both lifetime income and a current deduction, though the deduction is a fraction of the full value.

Similarly, a donor can give a life estate (living residence rights) to family while leaving the remainder to charity. The charitable deduction applies to the remainder value only, not the life estate portion. These split-interest gifts require careful planning and compliance with IRC §2702 valuation rules.

## State Estate Tax and Portability

Some states impose their own estate taxes, with lower exemptions than the federal threshold. A charitable bequest deduction is typically available under state law as well, following similar rules. The federal deduction does not automatically extend to state returns, so executors must file Form 706 and also file parallel state estate tax returns (e.g., in Massachusetts, New York, Connecticut).

The federal portable-exemption election (which allows a surviving spouse to use a decedent's unused exemption) does not interact negatively with charitable deductions—both mechanisms coexist. If one spouse dies and leaves assets to charity, the deduction applies; the surviving spouse can still port the first spouse's unused exemption for future transfers.
