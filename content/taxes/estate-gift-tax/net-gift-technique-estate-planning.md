*A **net gift** is a transfer of property where the recipient agrees to pay the [gift tax](/gift-tax/) attributable to the transfer. By having the recipient cover the tax liability, the donor effectively reduces the size of the taxable gift and removes value from the taxable estate without consuming as much lifetime exemption—making it a mathematically efficient gifting strategy for high-net-worth individuals.*

## How the net gift reduces the taxable amount

A traditional gift works like this: you give your daughter a $1 million house. The $1 million is the taxable gift. It uses $1 million of your lifetime exemption (assuming no other gifts that year).

A net gift works differently: you give your daughter the $1 million house, and she promises to pay the federal gift tax on that transfer. At a 40% tax rate (if you're over exemption), that tax would be $400,000. The taxable gift is then calculated as: gift minus the tax the recipient pays, or $1 million minus $400,000 = $600,000. By shifting the tax obligation to the recipient, you've reduced the taxable gift and freed up $400,000 of your exemption.

The math works recursively. Because the recipient paying the tax is itself a reduction in the taxable gift, the actual computation is: gift value minus [gift tax rate × (gift value minus tax)]. For a $1 million transfer at a 40% rate, the taxable gift is approximately $625,000 and the tax owed is about $250,000. This is more favorable than a traditional gift but requires careful calculation.

## The exemption advantage and leverage

The key appeal of a net gift is exemption leverage. If your lifetime exemption is limited or approaching exhaustion, every dollar of exemption saved has value. A net gift extracts value from your estate while using less of your exemption than a traditional gift of the same property.

This is especially powerful when gifting appreciating assets. If you transfer a business or real estate expected to grow significantly, a net gift locks in today's value for gift-tax purposes. All future appreciation accrues to the recipient outside your taxable estate. Meanwhile, the recipient's obligation to pay the gift tax is now a liability (not an asset) from your perspective, further reducing your gross estate.

For a high-net-worth person near or over the exemption threshold, net gifts offer a way to shift substantial wealth while preserving exemption for other planning strategies or for the surviving spouse.

## Risk to the donor: the non-paying recipient

The critical vulnerability is that the recipient must actually pay the tax. If your daughter agrees to pay the $250,000 gift tax on the net gift but doesn't, the IRS can pursue you (the donor) for payment. You remain liable if the recipient's promise isn't met.

This is why net gifts work best with competent, trustworthy recipients—typically adult children or spouses. Some planners require the recipient to actually pay over the funds for the tax before the deed or property transfer is finalized, or to use a promissory note or escrow to ensure funds are set aside.

## Income tax basis complications

One grey area in net-gift planning is the recipient's basis in the property. The IRS position (from *Diedrich v. Commissioner*, 1982) is that the recipient's adjusted basis is the gift amount reduced by any tax paid. So if the house is worth $1 million and the recipient pays $250,000 in gift tax, the recipient's basis is $750,000 for [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) purposes. When she later sells at $1.2 million, her gain is $450,000, not $200,000.

In effect, some of the income-tax benefit that would normally arise from a gift—the stepped-up basis at death—is lost in a lifetime net gift. The tax burden shifts from the donor (who gets lifetime exemption relief) to the recipient (who faces capital gains tax on the appreciation). This is fine if the assets will be held long-term or if the recipient is in a low tax bracket, but it's worth modeling.

## Who bears the capital gains tax?

In a traditional gift of appreciating property, the recipient inherits the donor's original [cost basis](/cost-basis/). If the donor bought at $500,000 and the house is now $1 million, the recipient gets a basis of $500,000 and can sell at $1 million tax-free (under the basis step-up at death). But in a net gift during life, the recipient takes a reduced basis and owes capital gains tax on the appreciation.

This trade-off—less gift tax now, more capital gains tax later—is acceptable when the recipient's capital gains rate is low (long-term holding, low income) or when the property is unlikely to be sold. For highly appreciated assets destined to stay in the family, traditional gifts or irrevocable trusts may be more tax-efficient.

## Net gifts of closely held business interests

Net gifts are common in family-business transfer planning. A parent might gift a 30% stake in a corporation to a child, with the child paying the gift tax. The business interest is removed from the parent's estate, and only the net gift amount (not the full value) counts against exemption.

This is especially useful for S-corporations or partnerships, where the recipient can eventually step into the parent's role as manager while the parent's estate shrinks. The valuation of the business interest is critical: if undervalued for gift-tax purposes, the IRS may challenge the value and assess additional gift tax on the difference.

## Estate planning coordination

Net gifts fit into a broader estate plan alongside other techniques. A client might use:

- **Annual exclusion gifts** (up to $18,000 per person per year, 2024) of liquid assets or growth stocks with no gift tax.
- **Net gifts** of larger appreciated properties or business interests, using exemption efficiently.
- **[Irrevocable life insurance trusts](/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust/)** to fund life insurance outside the estate and pay estate taxes.
- **[Spousal lifetime access trusts](/spousal-lifetime-access-trust/)** (SLATs) for further leveraging and freezing value.

Each tool serves a different goal; net gifts are most useful when the donor wants to move specific assets out of the estate while preserving exemption.

## Tax payment timeline and execution

The recipient must pay the gift tax by the due date of the gift tax return (April 15 following the year of the gift). If the return is filed late, penalties and interest accrue. The donor should ensure the recipient understands this deadline and has the liquidity to pay.

In practice, many net gifts are structured with a written agreement that specifies the property transferred, the gift tax due, and the recipient's obligation to pay by the due date. Some include an indemnity clause, protecting the donor if the recipient defaults. This documentation is crucial for the IRS if the return is examined.
