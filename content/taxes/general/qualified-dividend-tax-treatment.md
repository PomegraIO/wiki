*A **qualified dividend** is a cash distribution from a corporation that meets strict IRS holding-period and payer criteria, entitling the recipient to long-term capital gains tax rates (typically 15% or 20%) rather than ordinary income rates. This preferential treatment—a key advantage of dividend investing—applies only to dividends from U.S. corporations or approved foreign firms, and only when the shareholder has held the stock long enough around the payment date.*

## How the holding period works

The IRS does not simply require you to own the stock on the payment date. Instead, you must hold the stock (or a "similar or identical" security) for at least 60 days during a symmetrical 121-day window. This window runs from 60 days before the ex-dividend date to 60 days after it. The ex-dividend date—typically the day before the record date—matters because it marks when new buyers are no longer entitled to the pending dividend.

This rule blocks a common tax-evasion tactic: buying a stock a few days before the dividend payment, collecting the distribution, and immediately selling at a loss. The 60-day requirement forces genuine holding. Importantly, if you sell the stock, borrow against it, or short a substantially identical security during the window, the holding-period clock resets or breaks entirely. Dividends on hedged positions usually lose their qualified status.

## Why corporations and funds matter for qualification

Not all corporations issue qualified dividends. **Publicly traded U.S. corporations**—those filing with the SEC—automatically qualify, assuming the holding period is met. Foreign corporations normally do not, but there are narrow exceptions: dividends qualify if the foreign firm is a resident of a country with which the U.S. has an income tax treaty, or if its stock is actively traded on a U.S. exchange.

**[Real estate investment trusts](/real-estate-investment-trust/)** (REITs) rarely distribute qualified dividends; most REIT distributions are ordinary income or return of capital. Similarly, dividends from **master limited partnerships** and most other flow-through entities are ordinary income, even if the underlying business is profitable. Dividends from [mutual funds](/mutual-fund/) holding foreign securities often fail the qualification test if the foreign holdings exceed certain thresholds. Many small stocks, especially those not actively traded, yield non-qualified dividends.

## The tax benefit in context

The preferential rate matters enormously. A taxpayer in the top ordinary income bracket (37% federal) may pay only 20% on qualified dividends. For a $1,000 qualified dividend, the tax savings compared to ordinary treatment can exceed $300 in federal tax alone, before state considerations. This is why [dividend-yield](/dividend-yield/) strategies and [income funds](/income-fund/) have long appealed to wealthy taxable investors.

However, the benefit is not automatic. You must affirmatively report which dividends are qualified on [Schedule D](/schedule-d/) or Form 1040. The Form 1099-DIV you receive from your broker typically identifies ordinary and qualified dividends separately, but errors occur. Brokers may misclassify foreign dividends or fail to apply holding-period adjustments. It falls to you—or your tax preparer—to audit these designations, especially if you engaged in frequent trading around dividend dates.

## Interplay with wash-sale rules and short selling

The [wash-sale](/wash-sale/) rules interact with dividend qualification in subtle ways. Technically, a wash sale (selling at a loss and repurchasing within 30 days) does not automatically disqualify a dividend. However, if you're actively trading in and out of a stock, you're unlikely to satisfy the 60-day holding requirement anyway. Similarly, if you short a stock immediately before the ex-dividend date and cover the short within 60 days after, the IRS views this as a hedge, and the dividend on your long position ceases to qualify.

These overlaps explain why dividend investors often adopt a buy-and-hold mindset. Frequent trading destroys qualification; stability preserves it. For active traders or those using sophisticated [hedging strategies](/covered-call/), the tax advantage evaporates.

## Strategic implications

The preferential rate structure influences asset allocation. In a taxable account, a 4% qualified dividend yield may be more after-tax attractive than a 5% taxable bond yield. Conversely, in retirement accounts—where [401(k) plans](/401k-plan/) and [IRAs](/traditional-ira/) shelter all gains from current tax—the distinction is irrelevant. These accounts hold substantial bond allocations and [bond ETFs](/bond-etf/) whose non-qualified distributions would cost you nothing in retirement settings.

Qualified dividends also complicate the decision to hold [growth stocks](/growth-fund/) versus [value stocks](/value-fund/). Value portfolios, heavy in mature dividend payers, benefit more from the preferential rate. For very long investment horizons, [capital appreciation](/asset-allocation/) may still dominate, but the tax efficiency of qualified dividends tips the scales toward value and income strategies in taxable accounts.

## Common pitfalls and planning

Many investors fail to claim the benefit because they don't track the holding period carefully. A single large transaction—a [merger](/merger/) or [acquisition](/acquisition/) of a stock you own—can reset the clock. Some funds experience rapid holding-period failures because corporate actions trigger internal holdings that "reset the counter."

For [qualified dividend](/qualified-dividend-tax-treatment/) planning, clarity wins. If you're building a taxable portfolio, confirm the qualified-dividend pedigree of every major holding. If you're in a low tax bracket in a particular year—say, a year with net losses—ordinary-income treatment on some dividends may not sting as much, and you can focus your holding-period energy on high-yield, high-bracket-sensitive positions.

The distinction between ordinary and qualified dividends remains one of the sturdier tax preferences in the code, resilient through multiple reforms. As long as capital gains remain favoured, qualified dividends will be a pillar of after-tax investing strategy.
