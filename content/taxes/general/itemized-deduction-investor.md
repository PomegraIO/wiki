*The **itemized deduction** option allows taxpayers to deduct specific expenses instead of taking the [standard deduction](/standard-deduction-investor/). Eligible items include [charitable contributions](/charitable-contribution-deduction/), mortgage [interest](/interest-rate/), state and local taxes (capped at $10,000), and medical expenses. You must choose: [itemize](/itemized-deduction-investor/) *or* take the [standard deduction](/standard-deduction-investor/), whichever is larger. For most middle-income investors, the [standard deduction](/standard-deduction-investor/) is larger, but wealthy investors with large [charitable contributions](/charitable-contribution-deduction/) or expensive homes may benefit from [itemizing](/itemized-deduction-investor/).*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

To decide whether to itemize, compare your total itemized deductions to the [standard deduction](/standard-deduction-investor/) amount. Use whichever is larger.

## What can be itemized

**[Charitable contributions](/charitable-contribution-deduction/)**. Donations to qualified charities, nonprofits, and public institutions. Generally capped at 50%-60% of [adjusted gross income](/tax-bracket-investor/) depending on asset type.

**Mortgage [interest](/interest-rate/)**. [Interest](/interest-rate/) paid on [mortgages](/interest-rate/) for a primary residence and one vacation home (up to $750,000 of loan value).

**State and local taxes**. Income taxes, sales taxes (choose one), and property taxes. **Capped at $10,000 per year** (per taxpayer, for married filing jointly). This cap significantly limits itemization for high-tax-state residents.

**Medical expenses**. Medical, dental, and prescription expenses exceeding 7.5% of [adjusted gross income](/tax-bracket-investor/).

**Casualty losses**. Uninsured losses from theft, fire, or disaster (rare).

## What cannot be itemized

- Fees to a tax preparer
- [Investment advisory fees](/k-1-investor/)
- Personal expenses (car maintenance, groceries)
- Nondeductible [investment losses](/wash-sale/) (beyond [capital loss limits](/capital-gains-tax-investor/))

## The $10,000 state/local tax cap

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2017) capped state and local tax deductions at $10,000. For high-income earners in high-tax states, this is a major limit on itemization.

Example: Married couple, $200,000 income, in California (13.3% tax ≈ $26,600 state tax, plus property tax). Pre-2017, they could deduct $26,600+. Post-2017, they can deduct only $10,000 total. This alone can wipe out the itemization benefit.

## How to decide: itemize vs. standard deduction

1. **Estimate [charitable contributions](/charitable-contribution-deduction/)**: If you donate $15,000+ annually, itemization likely wins.
2. **Add other deductible expenses**: Mortgage [interest](/interest-rate/) + state/local tax (up to $10,000) + medical (if significant).
3. **Compare total to [standard deduction](/standard-deduction-investor/)**: If total > standard, itemize. Otherwise, take standard.

Example (2024, single):
- [Charitable contributions](/charitable-contribution-deduction/): $12,000
- Mortgage [interest](/interest-rate/): $8,000
- State/local tax: $8,000 (capped at $10,000, but using $8,000)
- **Total: $28,000 vs. [standard deduction](/standard-deduction-investor/) $14,600**
- **Itemize** (saves $28,000 - $14,600 = $13,400 deduction, ~$3,200 in tax at 24% rate)

## Charitable bunching

For investors who want to itemize but are near the threshold, "bunching" [charitable contributions](/charitable-contribution-deduction/) is a strategy: contribute $30,000 in year 1 (itemize), then $0 in year 2 (take [standard deduction](/standard-deduction-investor/)). This maximizes deduction over two years compared to spreading small annual donations.

A [donor-advised fund](/charitable-contribution-deduction/) makes bunching easy: donate large sums to the fund (deductible immediately), then disperse to charities over years.

## Impact on [tax bracket](/tax-bracket-investor/)

Itemized deductions reduce [taxable income](/tax-bracket-investor/), potentially lowering your [tax bracket](/tax-bracket-investor/) and thus your [marginal rate](/marginal-tax-rate-investor/). This is especially valuable near bracket boundaries.

## Business vs. personal deductions

Investors should not confuse business deductions (for self-employed activities, rental property) with itemized personal deductions. [K-1 income](/k-1-investor/) from partnerships already deducts business expenses at the entity level, so you do not double-deduct on Schedule A.

## Form and reporting

Itemized deductions are reported on **Schedule A** (Itemized Deductions), filed with Form 1040. Your tax software guides you through the choices.

## Limitations on [charitable contributions](/charitable-contribution-deduction/)

Charitable deductions are subject to percentage limitations (50%-60% of [AGI](/tax-bracket-investor/)) depending on the type of property donated. Cash gifts are less limited than appreciated securities.

## Interaction with [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/)

Itemized deductions reduce [taxable income](/tax-bracket-investor/) equally whether from wages or [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/). So a large [capital gain](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) can push you into a higher [tax bracket](/tax-bracket-investor/) even if you itemize. Plan [capital gains realization](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) alongside [charitable contributions](/charitable-contribution-deduction/).
