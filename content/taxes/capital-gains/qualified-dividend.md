*A **qualified dividend** is a [dividend](/dividend/) payment from a US or qualifying foreign [corporation](/public-company/) that is taxed at long-term [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) rates (0%, 15%, or 20%) rather than ordinary [income tax](/tax-bracket-investor/) rates. Most [dividends](/dividend/) from US stocks held in a typical brokerage account qualify, making them far more tax-efficient than ordinary dividends or [interest](/1099-int/).*

For dividends that do not qualify for preferential treatment, see [ordinary dividend](/ordinary-dividend/). For the mechanics of dividend taxation, see [dividend](/dividend/).

## Who pays qualified dividends

Most major US [corporations](/public-company/)—those in the S&P 500, for instance—pay qualified dividends. The dividend must be issued by a US corporation or a foreign corporation whose [stock](/stock/) is traded on a US exchange. A very small subset of [stocks](/stock/)—REITs, foreign stocks held directly, and most corporate bonds—pay ordinary dividends instead.

When you own [stock](/stock/) through an [ETF](/etf/) or [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/), the fund passes through the character of the [dividend](/dividend/) it receives. If the underlying [stock](/stock/) paid a qualified [dividend](/dividend/), your share of it is also qualified.

## The holding period requirement

To qualify for preferential treatment, you must hold the [stock](/stock/) for at least 60 days in the 120-day window surrounding the ex-dividend date—the date on which ownership officially splits. Buy a [stock](/stock/) today and collect a [dividend](/dividend/) tomorrow without ever holding for 60 days, and that [dividend](/dividend/) is ordinary, not qualified, and taxed at higher rates.

This requirement prevents investors from collecting [dividends](/dividend/) without bearing market risk. If you hold for only one day, you do not get the preferential treatment; you must be a committed shareholder for at least two months around the [dividend](/dividend/) date.

## Tax rates and brackets

Like long-term [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/), qualified [dividends](/dividend/) are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your income:

- **0% rate**: single filers up to ~$47,000 income; married up to ~$94,000 (2024).
- **15% rate**: single filers up to ~$518,000; married up to ~$1.03 million.
- **20% rate**: above those thresholds, plus 3.8% [net investment income tax](/net-investment-income-tax/).

This is dramatically more favorable than ordinary [dividend](/dividend/) income, which is taxed at rates up to 37%.

## Why the difference: qualified vs. ordinary

The tax code distinguishes qualified and ordinary [dividends](/dividend/) to incentivize corporate ownership. Qualified [dividends](/dividend/) are a form of "double taxation relief"—the [corporation](/public-company/) paid corporate tax on earnings before paying the [dividend](/dividend/), so Congress lets shareholders pay a lower rate. [Dividends](/dividend/) from REITs, partnerships, and other pass-through entities are ordinary because the underlying income may not have borne corporate tax.

## Reporting

Brokers report qualified [dividends](/dividend/) separately on [1099-DIV](/1099-div/) forms in Box 1b (qualified [dividends](/dividend/)) vs. Box 1a (ordinary [dividends](/dividend/)). Your tax software uses this distinction to apply the correct rate when you prepare your return.

## Interaction with capital gains

Qualified [dividends](/dividend/) are stacked on top of your other income to determine which tax bracket applies. A gain of $100,000 in long-term [capital gains](/capital-gains-tax-investor/) plus $10,000 in qualified [dividends](/dividend/) may push you from the 15% bracket to the 20% bracket, affecting both. This is one reason to consider [tax-loss harvesting](/wash-sale/)—offsetting gains can keep you in a lower bracket.
