*An **ordinary dividend** is a [dividend](/dividend/) payment that does not meet the requirements for [qualified dividend](/qualified-dividend/) treatment and is therefore taxed at ordinary [income tax](/tax-bracket-investor/) rates, up to 37% federally. Ordinary [dividends](/dividend/) are less tax-efficient than qualified [dividends](/dividend/) but are still common, particularly from REITs, partnerships, and foreign [stocks](/stock/).*

For [dividends](/dividend/) taxed at preferential long-term rates, see [qualified dividend](/qualified-dividend/). For the broader concept of dividend income, see [dividend](/dividend/).

## Why some dividends don't qualify

Not all [dividends](/dividend/) receive preferential tax treatment. The tax code is structured to incentivize investment in US corporations that pay corporate tax. [Dividends](/dividend/) that do not qualify for preferential rates include:

**REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts)**. REITs are required by law to distribute 90% of taxable income to shareholders. Because REITs do not pay corporate-level tax, their [dividends](/dividend/) cannot receive the "double-tax relief" of preferential rates. REIT [dividends](/dividend/) are always ordinary income.

**Partnerships and S-corps**. [K-1 income](/k-1-investor/) from partnerships and S-corporations is passed through to partners and shareholders as ordinary income, then often further distributed as [dividends](/dividend/).

**Foreign [stocks](/stock/)**. [Dividends](/dividend/) from foreign [stocks](/stock/) not listed on a US exchange are ordinary [dividends](/dividend/), even if the foreign company is equivalent to a large US [corporation](/public-company/).

**Preferred [stock](/stock/) and hybrid securities**. Some preferred [shares](/stock/) and other exotic securities pay dividends that do not qualify.

**Brokers holding [stock](/stock/) for short periods**. If you hold a [stock](/stock/) for fewer than 60 days surrounding the ex-dividend date, the [dividend](/dividend/) loses its qualified character and becomes ordinary.

## Tax rate and impact

Ordinary [dividends](/dividend/) are added to your ordinary income and taxed at your [marginal tax rate](/marginal-tax-rate-investor/). An investor in the 32% [bracket](/tax-bracket-investor/) pays 32% on ordinary [dividends](/dividend/)—more than double the 15% long-term rate for [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/).

Over time, this difference compounds. $10,000 in annual REIT [dividends](/dividend/) taxed at 32% costs $3,200 per year in tax; the same amount as [qualified dividends](/qualified-dividend/) taxed at 15% costs only $1,500—a difference of $1,700 per year, or $17,000 per decade.

## When ordinary dividends are unavoidable

Investors seeking income often cannot avoid ordinary [dividends](/dividend/). REITs, for instance, are held primarily for their high [dividend](/dividend/) yields, which are taxed as ordinary income. A portfolio heavy in REITs will have a higher tax bill than a portfolio of [stocks](/stock/) paying qualified [dividends](/dividend/). This is a trade-off: REITs often offer inflation protection and diversification benefits that offset the tax drag.

## Interaction with tax brackets

Ordinary [dividends](/dividend/) stack on top of your other income. If you earn $100,000 in wages, you are in the 24% [bracket](/tax-bracket-investor/). If you then receive $20,000 in ordinary [dividends](/dividend/), your total income becomes $120,000, and those [dividends](/dividend/) are taxed at 24% (or higher if they push you into the next bracket).

This means that taking on additional ordinary [dividend](/dividend/) income can have a cascading effect: it not only gets taxed at your marginal rate, but it can push you higher into the brackets, increasing your rate on other income.

## Reporting

Brokers report ordinary [dividends](/dividend/) in Box 1a of [1099-DIV](/1099-div/) forms (as opposed to Box 1b for qualified [dividends](/qualified-dividend/)). [K-1 income](/k-1-investor/) from partnerships and pass-through entities appears on [K-1](/k-1-investor/) forms instead.
