## Why Treasury Interest Gets State Tax Shelter

Federal law explicitly forbids states from taxing interest paid by direct U.S. obligations. The reasoning is straightforward: states cannot impede the federal government's borrowing costs. A state income tax on Treasury interest would effectively penalize anyone living in that state who funds federal debt, creating a hidden cost to the U.S. Treasury's operations.

This exemption is **automatic** — you do not need special registration or approval. The moment interest accrues on a Treasury bond or bill, it carries the state tax shield. The catch is identification: the IRS and Form 1099-INT do not automatically separate state-exempt Treasury interest from other taxable interest, so the burden falls on you to sort it out.

## What Interest Is Covered

Form 1099-INT reports all interest from an issuer combined. If you hold multiple Treasury securities, a brokerage will lump the interest together. The rule applies to:

- **Treasury bills** (short-term, issued at discount)
- **Treasury notes** (2–10 years)
- **Treasury bonds** (long-term)
- **Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS)**
- **Series EE, I, and other savings bonds** issued by the Treasury

The state tax exclusion does *not* apply to:

- State and local bonds (which have their own federal exemption)
- Federal agency bonds (such as Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac securities), even though they are quasi-federal
- [Mortgage-backed securities](/mortgage-backed-security/) issued by federal agencies
- Interest on loans secured by Treasury bonds
- Dividend income from bond funds or [ETFs](/etf/)

## Reporting on Schedule B

If you received over $1,500 in taxable interest during the year, you must file **Schedule B (Form 1040)** and attach it to your [federal tax return](/schedule-d/). Schedule B has a dedicated line for this distinction.

On **Schedule B, line 1**, you list all your interest income. The form then asks you to separate it: **line 1a** is all interest, and **line 1b** is the state tax-exempt portion (Treasury interest). You subtract line 1b from line 1a to arrive at the taxable federal interest reported on your main return.

This split does two things:

1. It allows the IRS to verify that you are not fabricating a non-existent exclusion.
2. It signals to state tax authorities that a portion of your box 1 interest should not be taxed at the state level.

Each state's tax form may then reference this figure, or you may need to fill in a state-specific schedule to claim the exclusion at filing time. Some states integrate the Schedule B line 1b figure directly; others require a separate line item or worksheet.

## How to Calculate the Amount

The tricky part: Form 1099-INT box 1 bundles all interest from the issuer, regardless of source. If a brokerage holds your Treasury bonds *and* other bonds or savings accounts, the 1099-INT will list the combined total.

To calculate the excluded portion:

1. **Gather statements** from your brokerage or Treasury Direct account for the calendar year.
2. **Extract the interest paid on Treasury securities only.** Most brokerages itemize this clearly.
3. **Add up that amount.** This is your line 1b figure for Schedule B.
4. **Check the issuer code** on the 1099-INT: if the issuer is the U.S. Department of the Treasury, the entire box 1 amount on that form qualifies (assuming no other income is commingled on the same statement).

Many investors receive multiple 1099-INT forms — one for each account or financial institution. The Treasury forms will have the Treasury Department listed as the issuer. Non-Treasury forms will not. Aggregate only the Treasury ones.

## State-Specific Twist

Although federal law mandates the exemption, some states require affirmative steps to claim it. A few states:

- Offer a checkbox or line on their income tax form for federal obligation interest
- Ask for proof (a copy of the statement showing the Treasury security)
- Accept the Schedule B line 1b figure as sufficient documentation

Texas, Florida, and several other states with no income tax do not apply the exemption (because they tax nothing). High-tax states like New York and California are strict about the exclusion if you itemize it: they cross-reference your federal return and expect consistency.

File your **state return** with the same Schedule B reference and the same line 1b amount as you reported federally. Inconsistency can trigger state audits.

## Common Pitfalls

**Excluding agency bonds mistakenly.** Treasury bonds are untaxable at the state level; Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and Federal Home Loan Bank securities are not, even though they are federal or quasi-federal. Check the issuer carefully.

**Forgetting to adjust your state return.** Some taxpayers file Schedule B for the federal return, claim the exclusion there, but then report the full box 1 amount on their state return. The state will disallow the exclusion if you do not explicitly claim it on the state form.

**Confusing Treasury bonds with Treasury bond funds.** A [bond ETF](/bond-etf/) or [mutual fund](/mutual-fund/) that holds Treasury securities is not itself a Treasury security. The dividends or distributions the fund pays are not state-exempt; only interest on the underlying Treasury bonds is. The fund will report its dividends on a different form.

## The Broader Context

This exclusion is part of a broader principle: federal taxing authority trumps state authority when the two conflict. Just as states cannot tax federal employees' salaries or federal agency buildings, they cannot tax interest paid by federal borrowing. The mechanism — Schedule B line 1b — is the IRS's way of letting taxpayers self-report this boundary.

Understanding the split between box 1a and box 1b also clarifies [cost-of-equity](/cost-of-equity/) and yield calculations. When you compute the after-tax return on a Treasury bond, the state tax rate drops to zero, making Treasuries relatively more attractive for high-income earners in high-tax states compared to corporate [bonds](/bond/) with the same [coupon rate](/coupon-rate/).
