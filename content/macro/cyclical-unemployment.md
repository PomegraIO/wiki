*Cyclical unemployment is [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) caused by weak aggregate demand — it rises sharply in [recessions](/recession) and falls as the [business cycle](/business-cycle) expands. Unlike [structural](/structural-unemployment) or [frictional](/frictional-unemployment) unemployment, which persist even in booming economies, cyclical unemployment is zero (by definition) when the economy is at [potential GDP](/potential-gdp).*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

Cyclical unemployment = Actual [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) − [Natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment). It reflects the [output gap](/output-gap): when output is below [potential](/potential-gdp), cyclical unemployment is positive.

## Cyclical unemployment in the business cycle

The [business cycle](/business-cycle) has a clear cyclical unemployment pattern:

**Expansion (recovery):**
- Output grows above [potential](/potential-gdp).
- Firms hire aggressively to meet rising demand.
- Cyclical unemployment falls toward zero.
- [Inflation](/inflation) may accelerate as spare capacity shrinks.

**Peak (boom):**
- Output reaches full capacity.
- Unemployment is at (or below) the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment).
- Cyclical unemployment is zero.
- [Inflation](/inflation) pressures are high.

**Contraction (recession):**
- Output falls below [potential](/potential-gdp).
- Firms lay off workers to match lower demand.
- Cyclical unemployment spikes, sometimes by 3–5+ percentage points.
- [Inflation](/inflation) falls ([disinflation](/disinflation)) as demand weakens.

**Trough (bottom of recession):**
- Output is far below [potential](/potential-gdp).
- Cyclical unemployment is at its peak.
- Job-finding is difficult; unemployment duration extends.

## Cyclical unemployment and the output gap

Cyclical unemployment is mechanically linked to the [output gap](/output-gap) through the Phillips curve:

**[Inflation](/inflation) = f([Output gap](/output-gap)) + Expected [inflation](/inflation)**

When output is below [potential](/potential-gdp) (negative [output gap](/output-gap)), there is positive cyclical unemployment, and [inflation](/inflation) decelerates. When output is above [potential](/potential-gdp), cyclical unemployment is negative (unemployment below natural rate), and [inflation](/inflation) accelerates.

The mechanism: weak demand means firms need fewer workers. Workers are laid off or not hired. As unemployment rises above the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment), workers have less bargaining power, wage growth slows, and firms hold price increases — [inflation](/inflation) falls.

## Great Recession example

The 2007-2009 Great Recession illustrates cyclical unemployment starkly:

- **Pre-recession (2007):** Unemployment was 4.6%, [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment) was ~5%, cyclical unemployment was near zero.
- **Peak of recession (Oct 2009):** Unemployment hit 10.0%, [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment) was ~5.5%, cyclical unemployment was +4.5 percentage points.
- **Recovery (2013-14):** Unemployment fell toward 6%, and cyclical unemployment shrank.
- **Late expansion (2018-19):** Unemployment fell to 3.5%, cyclical unemployment became slightly negative (unemployment below natural rate).

This cycle is typical. The depth of cyclical unemployment depends on the severity of the recession.

## COVID-19 shock: a different pattern

COVID was unusual: cyclical unemployment spiked to 14.8% in April 2020 — the highest on record — due to immediate demand destruction from lockdowns. But the recovery was rapid:

- April 2020: Unemployment 14.8%, cyclical unemployment ~10%.
- Dec 2020: Unemployment 6.7%, cyclical unemployment ~3%.
- Jan 2022: Unemployment 3.9%, cyclical unemployment near zero.

Why so fast? Massive fiscal and monetary stimulus, plus workers rehiring as restrictions eased. This is much faster than typical recessions.

## Policy responses to cyclical unemployment

Cyclical unemployment is what monetary and fiscal policy targets:

**Monetary policy ([Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve)):**
- Cut [interest rates](/interest-rate) when cyclical unemployment rises.
- Lower rates encourage borrowing and investment, boosting demand.
- Once demand recovers, cyclical unemployment falls.

**Fiscal policy (Congress):**
- Increase government spending or tax cuts when cyclical unemployment is high.
- Stimulus directly boosts aggregate demand, putting workers back to work.
- Once demand recovers, reduce stimulus to avoid [inflation](/inflation).

The debate: How aggressive should stimulus be? Too little leaves cyclical unemployment high; too much triggers [inflation](/inflation).

## Okun's law

The empirical relationship between cyclical unemployment and output is Okun's law:

**Change in Cyclical Unemployment ≈ −0.5 × (Output growth − [Potential growth](/potential-gdp))**

If [potential GDP](/potential-gdp) grows 2% but actual GDP grows 4%, output growth is 2% above trend, and cyclical unemployment falls by ~1%. This relationship, named after economist Arthur Okun, is robust empirically.

## Distinguishing cyclical from structural unemployment

This distinction is crucial but hard to measure in real time:

| **Type** | **Cause** | **Persistence** | **Fix** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cyclical** | Weak demand | Temporary (months-years) | Stimulus |
| **Structural** | Skills/location mismatch | Persistent (years-decades) | Retraining, relocation |

During deep recessions, [frictional](/frictional-unemployment) unemployment can become [structural](/structural-unemployment) — long-term unemployment destroys skills, employers stop hiring workers with gaps, and mismatches widen. A cyclical shock can have scarring effects.

## Post-pandemic debate

Whether the 2020-21 [inflation](/inflation) surge was cyclical unemployment falling too fast, or supply shocks, or both, remains contentious:

- **Narrative 1:** The Fed cut rates too aggressively; stimulus was excessive. Cyclical unemployment fell below the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment), causing demand-pull [inflation](/inflation).
- **Narrative 2:** Supply chains broke down; oil prices spiked; labor force participation fell structurally. [Inflation](/inflation) was not demand-driven but supply-driven.
- **Reality:** Both played roles. The Fed likely misjudged the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment) (thought it was lower than it was), but supply shocks were also severe.
