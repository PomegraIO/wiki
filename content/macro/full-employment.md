*Full employment does not mean zero [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/). Rather, it is the state when the economy has no [cyclical slack](/cyclical-unemployment/) — when actual [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) equals the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/), the [output gap](/output-gap/) is zero, and [inflation](/inflation/) is stable. In full employment, remaining [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) is purely [frictional](/frictional-unemployment/) and [structural](/structural-unemployment/).*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

Full employment typically corresponds to an [unemployment rate](/unemployment-rate/) of 4–4.5% in the US, not 0%. The remaining [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) reflects normal job search and sectoral mismatches.

## Full employment is not zero unemployment

This is the crucial conceptual point. Even in full employment, [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) exists:

**[Frictional unemployment](/frictional-unemployment/)** (1–1.5%):
- Workers between jobs while searching.
- Firms recruiting for positions.
- This is healthy; it reflects worker mobility and good matching.

**[Structural unemployment](/structural-unemployment/)** (1.5–3%):
- Skills mismatches (software job open, coal miner available).
- Geographic mismatches (Silicon Valley booming, Rust Belt depressed).
- This persists even in booms.

Together, these are the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/), typically 4–4.5%.

## Full employment in the business cycle

Full employment occurs at the **peak** of the [business cycle](/business-cycle/) — the point where the economy has recovered from recession, cyclical slack is gone, and further expansion will begin to overheat:

- **Before peak:** Economy is in expansion, unemployment is falling toward the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/), [cyclical unemployment](/cyclical-unemployment/) is positive.
- **At peak:** [Unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) hits the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/), [cyclical unemployment](/cyclical-unemployment/) is zero, [inflation](/inflation/) is stable.
- **After peak:** [Unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) falls below the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/), [cyclical unemployment](/cyclical-unemployment/) becomes negative, [inflation](/inflation/) accelerates.

A sophisticated policymaker would tighten policy at full employment to prevent overheating. A naive one might keep stimulus flowing, pushing [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) below [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/) and triggering [inflation](/inflation/).

## Identifying full employment in real time

The challenge: the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/) is unobservable. You cannot directly see when [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) reaches it. You must infer it from:

- **Unemployment level:** If [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) stops falling despite economic growth, you have probably reached full employment.
- **Wage growth acceleration:** If wage growth accelerates while [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) is stable, the economy has probably passed full employment (cyclical unemployment is now negative).
- **[Inflation](/inflation/) acceleration:** If [inflation](/inflation/) begins rising without supply shocks, cyclical slack has probably been absorbed.
- **Labor force participation:** If participation is not increasing, the economy may be at full employment (limited supply-side room for expansion).

In 2018-19, most policymakers believed the US economy had reached and passed full employment, yet [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) fell to 3.5% without much [inflation](/inflation/). This raised questions: Was the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/) actually lower? Or were measurement issues at play?

## Full employment and inflation

The relationship is via the Phillips curve. At full employment, [inflation](/inflation/) is stable at the rate of expected [inflation](/inflation/). Push [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) below the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/), and [inflation](/inflation/) accelerates. Allow [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) to rise above the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/), and [inflation](/inflation/) decelerates.

**At full employment (unemployment = [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/)):**
- [Cyclical unemployment](/cyclical-unemployment/) is zero.
- The [output gap](/output-gap/) is zero.
- Wage and price pressures are balanced.
- [Inflation](/inflation/) is stable.

## The Federal Reserve's dual mandate

The US [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) has a dual mandate: price stability (2% [inflation](/inflation/)) and maximum employment (full employment). These sometimes conflict:

- **Below full employment:** The Fed can cut [interest rates](/interest-rate/) to boost employment without [inflation](/inflation/) risk.
- **At full employment:** The Fed faces a trade-off. Further stimulus will both boost employment (temporarily) and raise [inflation](/inflation/).
- **Above full employment:** The Fed should tighten policy to prevent [inflation](/inflation/), even if it costs jobs.

Navigating this trade-off is a major source of monetary policy debate.

## Historical examples

**2019:** The US labor market appeared to be at full employment:
- [Unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) was 3.5%, a 50-year low.
- Wage growth was accelerating.
- [Inflation](/inflation/) was stable near the Fed's 2% target (neither accelerating nor decelerating).
- The Fed had raised rates to near-neutral levels.

**2020-21:** COVID disrupted everything, then stimulus pushed the economy past full employment:
- [Unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) fell to 3.5% again, but below-natural levels.
- Wage growth accelerated to 4–5% annually.
- [Inflation](/inflation/) rose from 2% to 8% by mid-2022.
- The Fed had to raise rates aggressively.

Whether the Fed should have identified full employment earlier and tightened more gradually remains debated.

## Full employment and inclusive growth

A policy debate: Is traditional full employment (4–4.5% [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/)) sufficient, or should policymakers aim for "inclusive" full employment where even marginalized groups approach lower unemployment rates?

- **Traditional view:** Full employment is defined by the [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/). Lower is unsustainable.
- **Progressive view:** The [natural rate](/natural-rate-of-unemployment/) is not fixed. Targeted job creation and aggressive full-employment policy can reduce structural [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) for minorities and disadvantaged groups, lowering the true natural rate.

Evidence is mixed. The 2018-19 tightness did help lower unemployment among Black and Hispanic workers, though gaps persisted.
