*Headline inflation includes all items in the [Consumer Price Index](/consumer-price-index/), especially volatile food and energy prices. It is what households experience directly at the grocery store and gas pump and is widely cited in media and policy discussions, but economists often prefer [core inflation](/core-inflation/) for policy analysis because headline inflation can be distorted by temporary commodity shocks.*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

Headline [inflation](/inflation/) = [Core inflation](/core-inflation/) + Food and energy [inflation](/inflation/). In commodity-spike periods, headline can exceed core by 2–3 percentage points.

## Why headline matters despite being volatile

Despite being more volatile, headline [inflation](/inflation/) matters because:

1. **It is what households experience.** People notice gas and food prices; they are unavoidable costs.
2. **It affects real income.** A household earning $60k sees its real purchasing power decline by 9% if headline [inflation](/inflation/) is 9%.
3. **It affects inflation expectations.** If headline [inflation](/inflation/) stays elevated, workers and firms expect sustained [inflation](/inflation/), pushing up wage and price demands.

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) is concerned that high headline [inflation](/inflation/) will become self-fulfilling — workers demand higher wages, firms raise prices, and expectations spiral upward.

## The food and energy component

Food and energy are ~30% of the [CPI](/consumer-price-index/) basket:

- **Energy** — Gasoline, heating oil, electricity. Globally traded commodities; prices driven by geopolitics, OPEC decisions, weather.
- **Food** — Groceries and restaurant meals. Driven by crop conditions, commodity prices, transportation costs.

Both are pass-through items:

- **Energy shocks are immediate.** Oil price spikes at the well → gasoline prices spike at the pump within weeks.
- **Food shocks take longer.** Crop failure today → higher wholesale prices in months → higher grocery prices later.

## Headline inflation dynamics 2020-2024

**2020:** Headline [inflation](/inflation/) fell from 2.3% to 1.2% as COVID demand destruction hit. Energy prices even went negative.

**2021:** Headline [inflation](/inflation/) rose to 4.7% as oil prices recovered from lows. Not yet "high," but rising.

**2022:** Headline [inflation](/inflation/) spiked to 9.1% (June) as Russia-Ukraine war disrupted oil and grain markets. Energy [inflation](/inflation/) peaked near 40% year-over-year.

**2023:** Headline [inflation](/inflation/) fell sharply to 4–5% as energy prices normalized. Fell further in late 2023 toward 3%.

**2024-26:** Headline [inflation](/inflation/) slowly declining toward Fed's implicit target of ~2%.

## Relationship to core inflation

The relationship between headline and [core inflation](/core-inflation/) reveals the source of price pressures:

- **If headline > core by >1.5 points:** Likely a commodity shock; [core inflation](/core-inflation/) is more stable underlying price pressure.
- **If headline ≈ core:** Demand is broad-based; no major commodity shock masking underlying pressures.
- **If headline < core (rare):** Energy/food prices are falling, but other [inflation](/inflation/) is persistent.

## Markets and headline inflation

Markets react more strongly to headline [inflation](/inflation/) than [core inflation](/core-inflation/):

- A headline [inflation](/inflation/) print of 8% will weaken stocks and bonds sharply.
- A [core inflation](/core-inflation/) print of 3.5% with headline of 2% (oil falling) may strengthen stocks (hope for rate cuts).

This is because headline hits household wallets directly, affecting consumer confidence and demand.

## Comparison with other countries

Headline [inflation](/inflation/) sensitivity varies globally:

- **US:** Energy and food are important but less life-or-death for most households; gasoline is ~3% of budget.
- **Emerging markets:** Food and energy can be 40–50% of household budgets. A food or energy shock is devastating.

This is why emerging market central banks sometimes track food and energy [inflation](/inflation/) more closely.

## The Fed's approach to headline inflation

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) official target is 2% inflation, but the Fed leaves room for headline volatility:

- If headline spikes to 6% due to oil but [core inflation](/core-inflation/) is 2.5%, the Fed likely will not raise rates; the spike is temporary.
- If headline spikes to 6% and [core inflation](/core-inflation/) is also rising (to 4%+), the Fed will tighten aggressively to contain it.

The key distinction is whether the shock is temporary ([core inflation](/core-inflation/) stable) or becoming embedded ([core inflation](/core-inflation/) rising).

## Implications for savers and investors

High headline [inflation](/inflation/) erodes real savings:

**Real return = Nominal return − Headline [inflation](/inflation/)**

If your savings account earns 0.5% and headline [inflation](/inflation/) is 3%, you are losing 2.5% in real purchasing power annually. This drives savers toward [inflation](/inflation/)-protected securities ([TIPS](/tips/)) or assets with pricing power (real estate, commodities, equities).
