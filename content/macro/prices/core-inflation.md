*Core inflation excludes the most volatile components of the [Consumer Price Index](/consumer-price-index/) — food and energy — to reveal the underlying [inflation](/inflation/) trend. Policymakers, especially the [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/), focus on core [inflation](/inflation/) because headline [inflation](/inflation/) can be distorted by temporary commodity shocks that are not driven by demand-side pressures.*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

Core [inflation](/inflation/) = Headline [inflation](/inflation/) − (Food and energy [inflation](/inflation/)). It is typically 0.5–1.5 points below headline in commodity-spike periods, and nearly identical during stable commodity phases.

</div>

## Why exclude food and energy

Food and energy prices are volatile and often driven by supply shocks unrelated to monetary policy or demand:

- **Oil spikes due to geopolitical events** (OPEC cuts, war in Middle East) — not directly related to US economic strength.
- **Crop failures** (drought in Ukraine, floods in India) → food prices spike — again, a supply shock, not demand.
- **Weather patterns** (warm winter lowers heating costs, hot summer lowers AC costs) cause monthly swings.

These shocks pass through to [inflation](/inflation/) quickly but often reverse just as quickly. A policymaker raising rates because oil spiked might be overreacting if the spike is temporary.

## Core inflation and monetary policy

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) uses core inflation to distinguish:

**Temporary shocks → leave monetary policy alone**
- Oil spikes from supply disruption.
- Food prices spike from poor harvest.
- These tend to reverse, and tightening is counterproductive.

**Persistent demand pressure → tighten policy**
- Demand outstrips supply consistently.
- Wage growth accelerates.
- Pricing power spreads across goods and services.
- This requires higher [interest rates](/interest-rate/) to slow demand.

By focusing on core, the Fed avoids overreacting to temporary commodity shocks.

## Food and energy vs. other "volatile" items

Some critics argue the food/energy exclusion is too narrow. Other items are also volatile:

- **Airfares** — highly cyclical with fuel and demand.
- **Used car prices** — spiked 40%+ in 2021-22, then crashed.
- **Rent** — less volatile but still fluctuates with housing demand.

Alternative measures like [trimmed-mean CPI](/trimmed-mean-cpi/) exclude the top and bottom 10% of price movements each month, capturing more nuance.

## Core inflation trends post-2008

- **2010-2019:** Core [inflation](/inflation/) was typically 1.5–2%, below the Fed's 2% target.
- **2020:** Core [inflation](/inflation/) fell to 1.2–1.5% (demand-side weakness post-COVID).
- **2021:** Core [inflation](/inflation/) rose from 1.5% to 3.5% as demand rebounded and supply lagged.
- **2022:** Core [inflation](/inflation/) peaked at 6.6% as wage growth accelerated.
- **2023:** Core [inflation](/inflation/) moderated toward 3% as Fed tightened.
- **2024-26:** Core [inflation](/inflation/) slowly declining toward 2% target.

## Core versus headline: examples

**2022 Oil spike:**
- Headline [inflation](/inflation/) peaked at 9.1% (June 2022) due to energy.
- Core [inflation](/inflation/) peaked at 6.6% (Sept 2022) — still high, but less dramatic.
- The 2.5-point difference reflected the oil spike.

**Late 2023 stabilization:**
- Oil prices stabilized; headline [inflation](/inflation/) fell below core.
- Core stayed elevated due to sticky services [inflation](/inflation/) (rent, healthcare).
- This indicated supply-side healing but persistent demand-side pressures.

## Sticky prices and core inflation

Core [inflation](/inflation/) is dominated by "sticky prices" — rents, healthcare, insurance — items that do not adjust instantly when demand changes:

- Rent adjusts annually; [inflation](/inflation/) in rents lags demand changes by 6–12 months.
- Healthcare prices are negotiated; wage-driven [inflation](/inflation/) takes time to pass through.
- Insurance premiums adjust at renewal.

Because these are sticky, core [inflation](/inflation/) is more persistent than headline [inflation](/inflation/). It is harder to cool via tightening because the stickiness delays the adjustment.

## Relationship to [sticky-price CPI](/sticky-price-cpi/)

A more sophisticated measure is [sticky-price CPI](/sticky-price-cpi/), which tracks only items with infrequent price changes:

- [Sticky-price CPI](/sticky-price-cpi/) is often 0.5–1 point higher than core [inflation](/inflation/).
- It turns faster than core [inflation](/inflation/) in response to demand shocks.
- Some economists argue it is a better leading indicator of future core [inflation](/inflation/).

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve/) monitors multiple measures — core [CPI](/consumer-price-index/), core PCE, [sticky-price CPI](/sticky-price-cpi/), [trimmed-mean CPI](/trimmed-mean-cpi/) — to triangulate true underlying [inflation](/inflation/).
