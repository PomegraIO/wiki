*The **average hourly earnings** (AHE) is the mean wage per worker hour, including overtime pay and bonuses, published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It measures the total cost of labor on a per-hour basis and serves as a leading indicator of wage inflation and worker compensation trends.*

## Why AHE matters to inflation forecasts

Average hourly earnings growth is one of the Federal Reserve's most closely watched labor indicators. When AHE rises faster than inflation, workers gain real purchasing power—a sign of labor market tightness. When it lags inflation, workers lose ground. The Fed uses AHE to gauge whether [wage-driven inflation](/wiki/wage-growth-expectations/) is building and whether [monetary policy](/wiki/monetary-policy/) should tighten further.

## The three components of AHE

The BLS calculates AHE by summing straight-time wages, overtime premiums, and shift differentials—then dividing total payroll by hours worked. This includes:

- **Base hourly rates**: the stated wage
- **Overtime pay**: time-and-a-half or double-time hours
- **Bonuses and shift premiums**: lump-sum or variable pay folded into the hourly average

This breadth makes AHE more volatile than simple wage rates. When overtime surges during a production boom, AHE jumps even if base rates are flat. When hours are cut, AHE can fall even if hourly rates rise.

## Nominal versus real AHE

The headline AHE number is nominal—expressed in current dollars. To assess whether workers are actually gaining purchasing power, economists compare it to [core inflation](/wiki/core-inflation/) or the [Consumer Price Index](/wiki/consumer-price-index/). Real AHE is the inflation-adjusted series; when it stalls or declines, workers face wage pressure despite headline pay raises.

## Industry variance and composition effects

AHE masks wide differences across sectors. Professional services workers often earn significantly more per hour than retail or hospitality workers. When [employment composition](/wiki/employment-population-ratio/) shifts—say, a recession sheds high-wage manufacturing jobs—aggregate AHE may fall even if wages in each industry rise. Conversely, a boom that adds low-wage service jobs can suppress AHE growth despite tight labor markets.

## The relationship to labor productivity and COLA

Economists benchmark AHE against [labor productivity](/wiki/labor-productivity/) to assess whether wage growth is sustainable. If AHE outpaces productivity for years, firms face margin pressure; if it lags, workers lose ground. Union contracts and Social Security [cost-of-living adjustments](/wiki/cost-of-living/) often reference or correlate with AHE trends, amplifying its policy impact.

## Watching AHE for recession signals

A sustained slowdown in AHE growth—especially in real terms—can signal approaching weakness in [aggregate demand](/wiki/consumption-function/). Conversely, AHE acceleration during late-cycle expansion often prompts the Fed to consider tighter [monetary policy](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/) to prevent wage-price spirals. Market analysts scrutinize month-to-month changes and year-over-year rates for early recession warnings.
