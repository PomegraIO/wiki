*Disinflation is a situation where [inflation](/inflation) slows — prices still rise, but at a decreasing rate. It is distinct from [deflation](/deflation), where prices actually fall. The US has experienced [disinflation](/disinflation) regularly: the sharp drop from 14% [inflation](/inflation) in 1980 to 3% by 1985, and again from the 2021-22 surge to 2.5–3% by 2026.*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

[Disinflation](/disinflation) typically occurs when [central banks](/central-bank) tighten monetary policy in response to rising [inflation](/inflation). It can also occur naturally during [recessions](/recession) due to weak demand.

## Disinflation versus deflation

A crucial distinction:

| **Concept** | **What Happens to Prices** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|
| **[Inflation](/inflation)** | Rising | 4% annual [inflation](/inflation): prices rise 4% year-over-year |
| **[Disinflation](/disinflation)** | Still rising, but slower | [Inflation](/inflation) falls from 4% to 2%: prices rise 2% instead of 4% |
| **[Deflation](/deflation)** | Falling | −2% [inflation](/inflation): prices fall 2% year-over-year |

During [disinflation](/disinflation), the [CPI](/consumer-price-index) index is still rising, but the year-over-year growth rate is falling. This is the most common scenario in modern economies.

## Causes of disinflation

**Policy-induced disinflation:**
- [Central bank](/central-bank) raises [interest rates](/interest-rate) to cool demand.
- Higher borrowing costs reduce spending and investment.
- Demand falls → firms cut prices → [inflation](/inflation) decelerates.
- The classic example: Fed Chair Paul Volcker's rate hikes in 1980-82 to break [inflation](/inflation).

**Demand-side disinflation:**
- [Recession](/recession) occurs; [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) rises.
- Weak demand puts downward pressure on prices.
- [Inflation](/inflation) naturally slows.

**Supply-side benefits (rare):**
- [Productivity](/productivity) growth accelerates, lowering cost-push [inflation](/inflation).
- Oil and commodity prices fall.
- [Inflation](/inflation) slows even as demand is healthy.

## The Volcker disinflation (1980-1985)

The most famous US [disinflation](/disinflation) came under Fed Chair Paul Volcker:

- **1980:** [Inflation](/inflation) peaked at 14.8% — the highest in post-WWII history.
- **1981-82:** Volcker raised the federal funds rate to 20% (nominal) to break [inflation](/inflation) expectations.
- **1983-85:** [Inflation](/inflation) fell from 12% to 3% — dramatic [disinflation](/disinflation).
- **Cost:** Severe recession; [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) rose to 10.8%.

The Volcker disinflation crushed [inflation](/inflation) expectations so thoroughly that [inflation](/inflation) remained low for the next 30 years. But it required massive short-term pain.

## The Phillips curve and disinflation

The Phillips curve predicts that [disinflation](/disinflation) requires a temporary output loss:

**Output loss = ([Inflation](/inflation) reduction) / (Sacrifice ratio)**

The "sacrifice ratio" is how much output (and thus [unemployment](/unemployment-rate)) must be lost to reduce [inflation](/inflation) by 1%. Estimates range from 2–5, meaning 1% [inflation](/inflation) reduction costs 2–5% of [potential GDP](/potential-gdp) in lost output.

The Volcker disinflation had a sacrifice ratio of ~5–6, meaning reducing [inflation](/inflation) by 11.8 percentage points cost roughly 2 years of severe recession.

## Disinflation and real interest rates

[Disinflation](/disinflation) raises real [interest rates](/interest-rate):

**Real rate = Nominal rate − Expected [inflation](/inflation)**

As expected [inflation](/inflation) falls, real rates rise even if nominal rates are unchanged. This can squeeze debtors (mortgages get more expensive in real terms) and hurt equities (lower [inflation](/inflation) expectations can raise discount rates, lowering [stock](/stock) valuations).

## Disinflation and debt

Unlike [deflation](/deflation), moderate [disinflation](/disinflation) is not catastrophic for debtors:

- With [disinflation](/disinflation), your nominal income still rises (just more slowly).
- The real burden of debt does increase somewhat, but not as sharply as in [deflation](/deflation).
- Default rates rise somewhat but not dramatically.

Example: If [inflation](/inflation) falls from 4% to 2%, wage growth falls from 4% to 2%, but wages don't go negative (as they would in [deflation](/deflation)).

## Recent disinflation: 2021-2026

The post-pandemic period saw sharp [disinflation](/disinflation):

- **2022:** [Inflation](/inflation) peaked at 9.1% (headline) and 6.6% (core).
- **2024:** [Inflation](/inflation) fell to 3–4%.
- **2026:** [Inflation](/inflation) moderating toward 2–2.5%.

This [disinflation](/disinflation) occurred with relatively modest [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) rise (from 3.5% to 4.2–4.3%), suggesting a low sacrifice ratio. Some credit better inflation expectations anchoring and supply-side improvements.

## Disinflation and financial markets

[Disinflation](/disinflation) has complex effects on markets:

**Initially:** Equities often struggle as real rates rise and [earnings](/earnings-per-share) decelerate.

**Later:** If [disinflation](/disinflation) brings [inflation](/inflation) to target without severe recession, equities can rebound (lower risk premium).

**Bonds:** Bonds initially gain value in [disinflation](/disinflation) (existing bond prices rise as yields fall), but the benefit diminishes as [disinflation](/disinflation) ends.

## Policy debates during disinflation

**Dovish view:** "The Fed should cut rates now to prevent recession."
- [Disinflation](/disinflation) is working; further pain is unnecessary.

**Hawkish view:** "The Fed should hold steady until [inflation](/inflation) reaches 2%."
- [Disinflation](/disinflation) could reverse if confidence is lost.

The 2023-25 period saw this debate: as [inflation](/inflation) fell, debate intensified over whether rate cuts were appropriate or premature.
