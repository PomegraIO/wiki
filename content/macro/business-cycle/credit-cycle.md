*The [credit cycle](/credit-cycle/) describes the self-reinforcing pattern where rising [asset](/asset-allocation/) values and economic optimism lead lenders to loosen [credit](/credit-risk/) standards and extend more debt, which in turn further inflates asset prices until the cycle reverses abruptly. Unlike [real business cycles](/real-business-cycle-theory/) driven by productivity shocks, credit cycles are endogenous—the system generates boom and bust from its own feedback mechanisms.*

## The boom: collateral, confidence, and leverage

In the boom phase of the [credit cycle](/credit-cycle/), rising [asset prices](/asset-allocation/)—whether [real estate](/residential-real-estate/), [stocks](/stock/), or commodities—increase the value of collateral that borrowers can pledge. A homeowner whose house has appreciated by 20 per cent can borrow more using that increased equity. A firm with rising market value can issue more [debt](/debt-financing/). The lender's perspective shifts too: if the borrower defaults, the collateral is worth more, so [credit risk](/credit-risk/) appears lower.

This generates a positive feedback loop. As banks [lower credit standards](/credit-rating/), they compete for market share by relaxing [underwriting](/credit-rating/) requirements—accepting lower down payments, weaker [cash flow](/cash-conversion-cycle/) verification, looser [debt-to-income](/debt-to-equity-ratio/) ratios. More borrowers qualify for credit. They spend and invest, driving demand, pushing asset prices higher still. The boom appears self-confirming: prices rise, lending expands, demand rises, prices rise further. Lenders feel vindicated by the improving data. Economic growth accelerates, [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) falls, and [default rates](/default-rate/) drop. The perception of [risk](/tail-risk/) narrows sharply.

Confidence becomes self-reinforcing. Investors and borrowers extrapolate recent returns and assume the good times will continue. Lenders, competing for market share and driven by [performance fees](/performance-fee/), take on riskier borrowers and larger [leverage ratios](/leverage-ratio-forex/). Sophisticated market participants build strategies betting on continued price appreciation. The financial system becomes increasingly indebted and fragile, though the fragility is hidden by rising collateral values and low observed [default rates](/default-rate/). The system is vulnerable to any shock that reverses confidence or liquidity.

## The mechanics of the downturn

The reversal of a [credit cycle](/credit-cycle/) is often sudden and nonlinear. A trigger—rising [interest rates](/interest-rate/), a [geopolitical](/capital-flows/) shock, unexpected [inflation](/inflation/), or even a loss of confidence for purely psychological reasons—causes prices to stop rising. Once prices flatten, the logic of the boom inverts.

Lenders reassess [collateral values](/fair-value/). A house that stopped appreciating is worth less in real terms; borrowers' equity shrinks. The buffer that made [credit](/credit-risk/) seem safe vanishes. [Default rates](/default-rate/) begin to rise, validating the renewed caution. Suddenly, the data that seemed supportive—high leverage, dependent on continued price appreciation—looks dangerous. Lenders withdraw [credit](/credit-risk/), raising [interest rates](/interest-rate/) on new loans or cutting exposure. Marginal borrowers who relied on refinancing or new borrowing face pressure. Some defaults occur. Lenders, burned by losses, become extremely conservative, moving to a new regime of tight [credit](/credit-risk/) conditions.

The bust phase of the [credit cycle](/credit-cycle/) is typically sharper than the boom. Borrowers who leveraged to buy assets now face margin calls or refinancing crises. Those who borrowed to fund operations face [cash flow](/cash-conversion-cycle/) pressure as [interest](/interest-rate/) expense rises. They sell assets to service debt, pushing prices down further. The feedback loop runs in reverse: falling prices → lower collateral → tighter [credit](/credit-risk/) → asset sales → falling prices. [Unemployment](/unemployment-rate/) rises, incomes fall, and [default rates](/default-rate/) spike. The economy slides into [recession](/recession/).

## Why real business cycle theory misses credit cycles

[Real business cycle](/real-business-cycle-theory/) theory attributes cycles to exogenous shocks—technology disruptions or productivity changes. But many [credit cycles](/credit-cycle/) appear to have little connection to fundamental productivity. The 2008 financial crisis, for instance, was preceded by years of strong nominal growth but no obvious productivity acceleration. Instead, credit expansion and leverage drove demand and asset prices. When [credit](/credit-risk/) tightened, growth collapsed not because productivity fell, but because borrowers could no longer fund spending.

Similarly, the [real estate](/residential-real-estate/) booms in Ireland and Spain before 2008 generated rapid growth measured in [GDP](/business-cycle/), but much of that was [investment](/equity-financing/) in structures with dubious long-term returns. The boom masked the reality that excessive [credit](/credit-risk/) was being channelled into low-productivity uses. When the [credit cycle](/credit-cycle/) reversed, the true weakness of the productive base was revealed. This distinction—between nominal growth driven by [credit](/credit-risk/) inflation versus real growth driven by productivity improvement—is essential to understanding modern [recessions](/recession/).

## The role of financial institutions and regulation

The [credit cycle](/credit-cycle/) is amplified by the structure of financial markets and the behaviour of banks. Banks borrow short-term (via deposits and [bond](/bond/) markets) and lend long-term. In a boom, they feel pressure to expand balance sheets to compete and profit. Regulators' [capital adequacy](/capital-adequacy/) rules set a floor on [bank](/federal-deposit-insurance-corporation/) solvency, but in a long boom with low [default rates](/default-rate/), capital ratios can drift upward relative to measured [risk](/tail-risk/), encouraging [leverage](/leveraged-etf/). When [credit](/credit-risk/) conditions tighten, banks face rapid deposit outflows or [bond](/bond/) market freezes, forcing fire-sales of assets and [credit](/credit-risk/) rationing.

After 2008, regulators introduced stricter [capital](/capital-adequacy/) and liquidity requirements ([Basel III](/capital-adequacy/) rules, stress tests) to reduce the amplitude of [credit cycles](/credit-cycle/). But the cycle has not been eliminated—it has migrated partly to [shadow banking](/hedge-fund/) (private equity, hedge funds, non-bank lenders) where [regulation](/dodd-frank-act/) is lighter. Understanding and managing [credit cycles](/credit-cycle/) remains a central challenge for policymakers aiming for financial stability.
