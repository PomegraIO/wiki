*The [political business cycle](/political-business-cycle/) describes how electoral incentives lead incumbent governments to manipulate [fiscal policy](/fiscal-consolidation/) and [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) to generate favourable economic conditions before elections, at the cost of [inflation](/inflation/) and instability afterward. Rather than pursuing optimal long-term policy, policymakers strategically time stimulus to engineer pre-election booms.*

## The electoral incentive structure

The political business cycle rests on a simple premise: voters have short memories and reward recent economic performance. A government that delivers growth in the quarter before an election will likely secure re-election, even if it inherited a strong economy or will leave a mess for the next administration. Conversely, a government that inherits a trough and endures years of painful adjustment before recovery will not be credited for the eventual upswing if it occurs after the election.

Rational politicians respond to these incentives. An incumbent with control over [fiscal policy](/fiscal-consolidation/) can increase [government spending](/discretionary-spending/), cut taxes, or both ahead of an election. A [central bank](/central-bank/) either under political pressure or sharing electoral motives can cut [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and expand [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/). These measures boost demand, lower unemployment, and create a visible boom in the year of the election. Voters, seeing unemployment falling and wages rising, vote to re-elect the incumbent.

The catch: the boom is unsustainable. The expansion was driven by temporary stimulus, not fundamental productivity improvements. Once the election is over, [inflation](/inflation/) begins to rise, either openly or as [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) becomes more accommodative than [central banks](/central-bank/) intended. The new or returning government then tightens policy—raising [interest rates](/interest-rate/) and cutting spending—to manage [inflation](/inflation/). The economy slows, unemployment rises again, but this pain is endured in the off-election years when voters are less attentive. By the time the next election approaches, the cycle begins anew.

## Contrasting political and real business cycles

The [political business cycle](/political-business-cycle/) is fundamentally at odds with [real business cycle](/real-business-cycle-theory/) theory. RBC holds that [cycles](/business-cycle/) reflect rational, efficient responses to productivity shocks, and that [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) cannot persistently mislead rational agents. The political cycle, by contrast, assumes policymakers *can* mislead voters and create temporary booms that voters interpret as competence rather than stimulus.

This requires that voters suffer from what economists call "political myopia"—they overweight recent conditions and underweight information about underlying [inflation](/inflation/) or unsustainability. It also assumes rational expectations are incomplete; voters do not fully anticipate that pre-election stimulus will be paid for in [inflation](/inflation/) later. Over time, as voters and markets become more sophisticated, they should see through this pattern, prune their expectations, and cease to reward it. Yet the pattern has proven remarkably persistent across democracies.

## Empirical evidence and debate

The original framework predicted observable, predictable cycles aligned with election calendars. In practice, the evidence is mixed. Some countries and time periods show clear evidence of electoral effects on growth and unemployment. In the United States, pre-election growth acceleration and post-election slowdowns have been documented, though the magnitude is often small. In some developing democracies, where institutions are weaker, the pattern is more pronounced.

However, the effect is not mechanical. Governments face constraints: [budget deficits](/budget-deficit/) become unsustainable, [bond](/bond/) markets may refuse to finance excessive borrowing, and [inflation](/inflation/) can spiral quickly if not managed. Credible, independent [central banks](/central-bank/) also limit electoral manipulation of [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/). Central bankers (particularly in the eurozone and many other advanced economies) explicitly resist pre-election pressure to cut [interest rates](/interest-rate/), arguing that their mandate is [price stability](/inflation/), not employment or growth.

Another complication: not all pre-election booms are manufactured. Sometimes the economy naturally expands before an election because of lucky supply-side improvements or [capital flows](/capital-flows/). And sometimes elected politicians simply face too many constraints—rising [inflation](/inflation/), [budget deficits](/budget-deficit/), or loss of [credit](/credit-risk/) market access—to engineer a pre-election boom, even if they want to.

## The role of transparency and credibility

Modern democracies have built institutions designed to reduce electoral manipulation of [macroeconomic](/monetary-policy/) policy. [Central banks](/central-bank/) in most advanced economies operate with substantial independence, insulating [monetary policy](/monetary-policy/) from direct electoral pressure. Budget rules, multi-year spending plans, and fiscal frameworks limit ad hoc spending increases. Statistical agencies publish comprehensive [inflation](/inflation/), [unemployment](/unemployment-rate/), and growth data in real-time, making it harder for governments to hide underlying conditions.

These safeguards have weakened the political cycle. Yet incentives persist. A government can still time the *announcement* of spending, the distribution of fiscal benefits (tax credits, transfers, public works), or permissive stances toward [credit cycles](/credit-cycle/) to maximise pre-election visibility. And in democracies with weaker institutions—limited [central bank](/central-bank/) independence, poor statistical transparency, or discretionary [budget](/budgeting-methods/) control—the pattern can be strong.
