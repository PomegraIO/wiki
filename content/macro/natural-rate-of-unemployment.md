*The natural rate of unemployment is the [unemployment rate](/unemployment-rate) that prevails when the [output gap](/output-gap) is zero — when the economy is at full capacity and [inflation](/inflation) is stable. It is not zero because unemployment always includes [frictional](/frictional-unemployment) (job search) and [structural](/structural-unemployment) (skills mismatch) components.*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

The natural rate is nearly identical to [NAIRU](/nairu) (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment). The natural rate is the unemployment rate at which [inflation](/inflation) does not accelerate or decelerate.

## Why unemployment is never zero

Even in a perfectly functioning economy, unemployment always exists. Two types comprise the natural rate:

**[Frictional unemployment](/frictional-unemployment)** (typically 1–2%):
- Workers between jobs during normal job search.
- Firms searching for workers with right skills.
- Geographic mismatches (job in one city, worker in another).
- This is healthy — it reflects worker mobility and optimization.

**[Structural unemployment](/structural-unemployment)** (typically 1.5–3%):
- Skills mismatch — job requires skills worker lacks.
- Geographic mismatch — jobs in Sun Belt, workers in Rust Belt.
- Technological displacement — coal mining skills useless when switching to solar.
- This is persistent and harder to fix than frictional unemployment.

Together, these typically total 4–4.5%, though they vary over time.

## [Cyclical unemployment](/cyclical-unemployment)

Unemployment above the natural rate is [cyclical unemployment](/cyclical-unemployment) — the result of weak demand. It arises during [recessions](/recession) and gradually falls as the [business cycle](/business-cycle) expands. The [output gap](/output-gap) is negative when unemployment is above the natural rate.

Example: If the natural rate is 4.5% and actual [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) is 6.5%, then [cyclical unemployment](/cyclical-unemployment) is 2.0 percentage points, and the [output gap](/output-gap) is negative.

## Estimating the natural rate

Because the natural rate is unobservable, economists estimate it using:

1. **Survey-based methods:** Economists are asked to estimate; typical answer is 4–4.5%.

2. **Backward-looking methods:** Fit a Phillips curve to historical data and estimate the unemployment rate at which [inflation](/inflation) is stable.

3. **Time-varying methods:** Assume the natural rate changes over time and use a Kalman filter or similar technique to estimate it period-by-period.

4. **Demographic decomposition:** Calculate what the natural rate would be given current demographics and labor market institutions.

These methods often disagree, and uncertainty is substantial — estimates typically have confidence bands of ±0.5%.

## Time-varying natural rate

The natural rate is not constant. It likely shifted over time in the US:

- **1960s:** ~4.0%
- **1970s-80s:** ~5.5–6.0% (high due to stagflation, demographics)
- **1990s-2000s:** ~4.5% (declining due to lower inflation expectations, aging demographics)
- **2010s:** ~4.0–4.5%
- **2020s:** ~4.0% (possibly lower due to labor market tightness)

The decline from 5.5% to 4% reflects:
- **Lower inflation expectations** — anchored by credible central banks.
- **Demographic aging** — older workers have lower unemployment rates.
- **Labor market deregulation** — easier firing/hiring, lower structural unemployment.
- **Information technology** — faster job search, lower frictional unemployment.

## Policy implications

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve) uses the natural rate to guide monetary policy:

- **If [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) < natural rate:** The economy is overheating, [inflation](/inflation) will accelerate, the Fed should raise rates.
- **If [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) > natural rate:** The economy is slack, [inflation](/inflation) will decelerate, the Fed should cut rates.
- **If [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) = natural rate:** The economy is at full capacity, [inflation](/inflation) is stable, policy is neutral.

The challenge: small errors in estimating the natural rate lead to large policy mistakes. If the Fed thinks the natural rate is 4.5% when it is actually 3.8%, the Fed will keep rates too high and cause unnecessary unemployment.

## The 2010s debate

The debate over the natural rate was central to post-crisis monetary policy:

- **Pessimists** argued the natural rate had risen to 5.5% or higher due to long-term unemployment destroying skills.
- **Optimists** argued it was 4% or lower, and the Fed could push [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) much lower without [inflation](/inflation).

Events favored the optimists: [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) fell to 3.5% in 2019 without [inflation](/inflation) accelerating much. But the rapid [inflation](/inflation) of 2021-22 raised questions: had the Fed kept [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) too low? Or were supply shocks the culprit?

## Natural rate and participation

An important wrinkle: [labor force participation](/labor-force-participation-rate) is not constant. If participation declines, the official natural rate (as a percentage of the shrinking labor force) can stay low even as the [employment-population ratio](/employment-population-ratio) rises. Some economists argue that [employment-population ratio](/employment-population-ratio) is more informative than [unemployment](/unemployment-rate) for assessing slack.
