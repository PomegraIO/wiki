*Sticky-price CPI measures [inflation](/inflation) in items with infrequent price changes — rents, healthcare, insurance, subscriptions — that tend to adjust slowly even when economic conditions change. It is often more persistent than [core inflation](/core-inflation) and can be a better leading indicator of future [inflation](/inflation) pressures.*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

Sticky-price CPI typically runs 0.5–1.0 percentage points above [core CPI](/core-inflation) because items with sticky prices tend to have persistent underlying demand pressures.

## What are sticky prices?

Sticky prices are items that do not adjust frequently:

- **Rent:** Adjusted annually at lease renewal.
- **Healthcare:** Negotiated; adjusted annually or when contracts renew.
- **Insurance:** Premiums adjusted at renewal (often annually).
- **Subscription services:** Price changes are discrete, not continuous.
- **Tuition:** Set annually.
- **Wages:** Adjusted annually or with promotions.

In contrast, flexible prices change frequently:

- **Gasoline:** Reflects global oil market daily.
- **Airline fares:** Adjust hourly based on demand.
- **Produce:** Prices change with seasons and supply.

## Why stickiness matters

Sticky-price items matter for [inflation](/inflation) dynamics:

1. **They drive expectations.** Rent adjustment negotiations are painful; workers demand wage increases to keep pace. This cascades.
2. **They are persistent.** Once [inflation](/inflation) enters a sticky-price item (rent rises 5%), it tends to stay elevated (rent rises 5% again next year).
3. **They account for much spending.** Housing is ~40% of [CPI](/consumer-price-index); healthcare is ~8%; together they are nearly 50%.

## Sticky-price CPI as an inflation indicator

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve) increasingly watches sticky-price CPI because:

- It **leads** [core inflation](/core-inflation) — sticky prices turn before core as wage pressures build.
- It is **more persistent** — less subject to commodity shock distortions.
- It **reflects demand pressure** — when the economy is tight, wage growth drives sticky-price [inflation](/inflation) first.

In 2021-22, sticky-price CPI was the canary in the coal mine: it spiked before core [CPI](/consumer-price-index), correctly signaling persistent [inflation](/inflation).

## Relationship to flexible prices

Over time, sticky and flexible prices should move together. But in the short run, they diverge:

**Commodity boom (2021-22):**
- Flexible prices (gasoline, food) spike immediately.
- Headline [inflation](/inflation) surges to 9% (oil shock).
- Sticky prices lag; take 6–12 months to adjust.
- Core [inflation](/core-inflation) rises but more slowly.
- Sticky-price [inflation](/inflation) rises slowly, then persistently.

**Commodity crash:**
- Headline [inflation](/inflation) falls immediately.
- Sticky prices stay elevated; they are "ratcheting" (they go up, then stay up, then go up again).
- Sticky-price [inflation](/inflation) remains high even when headline falls.

## Housing and sticky-price inflation

Rent accounts for ~33% of the sticky-price basket. Rent [inflation](/inflation) is crucial:

**2021-22:** Rent [inflation](/inflation) spiked from 1.5% to 9% as the pandemic drove migration to sunbelt states and remote work raised housing demand. This cascaded into wage demands.

**2023:** Rent [inflation](/inflation) began moderating as supply increased (more apartment construction), but the deceleration was slow.

The lag between tight housing markets and rent adjustment is 6–12 months, which is why housing [inflation](/inflation) is such a persistent feature.

## Healthcare in sticky-price inflation

Healthcare [inflation](/inflation) is sticky: once hospital and drug prices rise, they rarely fall. They may decelerate, but they do not reverse. This makes healthcare [inflation](/inflation) a structural long-run [inflation](/inflation) problem, not cyclical.

Post-pandemic, healthcare [inflation](/inflation) accelerated above [CPI](/consumer-price-index) growth, reflecting labor shortages and demand recovery. This is expected to persist.

## Fed's expanding focus on sticky prices

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve) under Jerome Powell has emphasized sticky-price [inflation](/inflation) in speeches and testimonies:

- **2021-22:** The Fed cited sticky prices as evidence that [inflation](/inflation) was becoming persistent, justifying aggressive rate hikes.
- **2023-24:** The Fed watched sticky-price [inflation](/inflation) as it decelerated, looking for confirmation that [inflation](/inflation) was returning to target.

This shift reflects a recognition that headline and [core inflation](/core-inflation) can be misleading when commodity shocks are large.

## Sticky-price CPI divergence from core

Sticky-price CPI often diverges from [core CPI](/core-inflation):

- **When demand is tight:** Sticky-price [inflation](/inflation) accelerates as wages rise, passing through to rents, healthcare, and services. [Core inflation](/core-inflation) may lag if there is a commodity offset.
- **When demand is weak:** Sticky-price [inflation](/inflation) persists (ratcheting) while [core inflation](/core-inflation) falls. This makes monetary policy harder — the economy is slack, but [inflation](/inflation) remains elevated.

The 2023-24 period showed this: [core CPI](/core-inflation) fell toward 3% while sticky-price measures remained near 4%, keeping the Fed vigilant about risks.
