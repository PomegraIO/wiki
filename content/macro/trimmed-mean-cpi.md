*Trimmed-mean CPI is calculated by removing items with the most extreme price changes (highest and lowest 10–20%) each month, then averaging the remainder. This approach aims to capture underlying [inflation](/inflation) trends better than [core inflation](/core-inflation) by filtering out both temporary commodity shocks and unusual demand spikes.*

<div class="wiki-hatnote">

Trimmed-mean CPI usually falls between headline and [core inflation](/core-inflation), and often predicts [core inflation](/core-inflation) movements with a 1–2 month lead.

## How trimmed-mean works

Each month, statisticians:

1. **Collect price changes** for all ~80,000 items in the [CPI](/consumer-price-index) basket.
2. **Remove the top 10–20%** of largest price increases.
3. **Remove the bottom 10–20%** of largest price decreases.
4. **Average the remaining ~60–70%** of items.

The items trimmed vary month-to-month. In an oil-shock month, gasoline (big price increase) is trimmed out. In a deflationary month, items with steep discounts are trimmed. This rotation means trimmed-mean is less distorted by any single shock.

## Why trim?

Trimming serves multiple purposes:

1. **Removes outliers.** A sudden 50% surge in used-car prices (2021-22) is removed, preventing outsized headline impact.
2. **Captures underlying demand.** If half of items are raising prices 2–3% due to demand, trimmed-mean will show this even if gasoline is spiking 15%.
3. **Smooths month-to-month noise.** Reduces the statistical noise that makes month-to-month [CPI](/consumer-price-index) hard to interpret.

## Comparison with core inflation

| **Metric** | **Approach** | **Strength** | **Weakness** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Headline** | All items | What households pay | Commodity noise |
| **[Core](/core-inflation)** | Ex-food, energy | Removes known volatility | Fixed exclusion; misses other spikes |
| **Trimmed-mean** | Remove extremes | Adaptive to current shocks | Complex; less intuitive |

Trimmed-mean is more sophisticated but less familiar to the public. [Core inflation](/core-inflation) is simpler but sometimes includes items that should be excluded.

## Trimmed-mean as a leading indicator

Research shows trimmed-mean CPI **predicts [core CPI](/core-inflation) inflation 1–2 months ahead**:

- When trimmed-mean spikes, [core CPI](/core-inflation) usually follows.
- When trimmed-mean moderates, [core CPI](/core-inflation) usually moderates.

This makes trimmed-mean valuable for real-time assessment of whether [inflation](/inflation) pressures are building or fading.

## 2021-22 example

**June 2021:**
- Headline [inflation](/inflation): 5.4%
- [Core inflation](/core-inflation): 4.5%
- Trimmed-mean: 3.8%
- Interpretation: Gasoline and food were spiking, but underlying demand was more moderate.

**November 2021:**
- Headline [inflation](/inflation): 6.8%
- [Core inflation](/core-inflation): 4.9%
- Trimmed-mean: 4.2%
- Interpretation: Commodity shock was masking a rising underlying trend.

**June 2022:**
- Headline [inflation](/inflation): 9.1%
- [Core inflation](/core-inflation): 5.9%
- Trimmed-mean: 5.0%
- Interpretation: Persistent underlying demand-driven [inflation](/inflation) was becoming clear.

By fall 2022, [core CPI](/core-inflation) had risen to 6.5%, confirming what trimmed-mean had signaled months earlier.

## Fed's adoption of trimmed-mean

The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve) has increasingly cited trimmed-mean CPI in statements:

- **2021-22:** Emphasized trimmed-mean as evidence of persistent [inflation](/inflation).
- **2023-24:** Tracked trimmed-mean deceleration to assess progress toward 2% target.

This reflects the Fed's recognition that both headline and [core](/core-inflation) measures have limitations.

## Variants of trimming

The Cleveland Federal Reserve calculates trimmed-mean removing the top and bottom 16.4% of items (roughly one standard deviation). Other institutions use different percentages (10%, 20%). The choice matters:

- **Aggressive trimming (20%):** Smooths more; very stable but lags by several months.
- **Light trimming (10%):** More responsive but retains some noise.

Most analysts use the Cleveland Fed's 16.4% version for consistency.

## Relationship to sticky-price CPI

Trimmed-mean and [sticky-price CPI](/sticky-price-cpi) are conceptually different:

- **Trimmed-mean:** Adaptive trimming based on price movements; fluid set of items.
- **[Sticky-price](/sticky-price-cpi):** Fixed set of items with infrequent price changes; structural approach.

They often move together but can diverge. In 2023, [sticky-price CPI](/sticky-price-cpi) remained elevated while trimmed-mean fell faster, suggesting sticky-price items (rent, healthcare) were where [inflation](/inflation) persistence lay.

## Limitations

Trimmed-mean is less intuitive than [core inflation](/core-inflation):

- What is being trimmed varies month-to-month, making narrative storytelling harder.
- The methodology is complex; few people outside academia understand it.
- It is not an official Fed target (though watched closely).

For these reasons, [core inflation](/core-inflation) remains the primary policy metric despite trimmed-mean's statistical merits.
