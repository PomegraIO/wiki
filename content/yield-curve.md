*A **yield curve** is a graph showing the relationship between [bond](/bond) yields and maturities. The x-axis is time (maturity, from three months to 30 years); the y-axis is [yield](/dividend) (the annual interest rate). Normally, longer-dated bonds offer higher yields because investors demand compensation for the risk of locking up money for a long time (longer duration, inflation risk). When this normal relationship inverts—when short-term [yields](/dividend) exceed long-term [yields](/dividend)—it often signals economic stress and has historically preceded [recessions](/recession). The yield curve is one of the most watched and least understood charts in finance.*

## The normal yield curve and why it slopes upward

Under normal conditions, the yield curve slopes upward: a three-month Treasury bill yields 2%, a ten-year Treasury note yields 3%, and a 30-year Treasury bond yields 3.5%. Longer = higher [yield](/dividend).

Why? **Time and uncertainty.** If you lend money for three months, you know what will happen to it. If you lend for 30 years, you face enormous uncertainty: inflation could erode your purchasing power, interest rates could rise (making your low-yielding [bond](/bond) worth less if you have to sell before maturity), or the borrower could default (though US government default is near-zero). Investors demand higher yields to compensate for this uncertainty.

This compensation for maturity risk is called the **term premium**. It is built into the normal yield curve. Investors who buy longer-dated [bonds](/bond) expect to earn more than those who buy short-term [bonds](/bond), and they do.

## Reading the curve: the shape

The shape of the yield curve changes with economic conditions:

**A steep curve** (long [yields](/dividend) well above short [yields](/dividend), e.g., short=2%, long=4%) typically signals economic optimism. The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve) has cut short-term [rates](/interest-rate) to stimulate the [economy](/recession), but long-term [yields](/dividend) are elevated because investors expect future inflation as growth accelerates. A steep curve is common early in an economic recovery.

**A flat curve** (short and long [yields](/dividend) converge, e.g., all ~3%) signals economic transition. The market is uncertain. Short [rates](/interest-rate) may be elevated (the Fed is fighting inflation), but long [yields](/dividend) are not much higher, suggesting the market expects eventual disinflation or weak growth.

**An inverted curve** (short [yields](/dividend) > long [yields](/dividend), e.g., short=5%, long=4%) is historically rare and economically ominous. It signals that investors are so worried about near-term risk that they are willing to lock in lower long-term yields. They are essentially saying: "I expect a [recession](/recession), [interest rates](/interest-rate) will fall, and I want to lock in today's rate before they do." An inversion has preceded most US [recessions](/recession).

## The inversion-recession link

The most famous feature of the yield curve is the **inversion-recession relationship.** Since 1970, nearly every US [recession](/recession) has been preceded by an inverted yield curve. The lead time has typically been six to 18 months: the curve inverts first; the [recession](/recession) arrives later.

This relationship is not mechanical or guaranteed. A few inversions have not been followed by [recessions](/recession), and a few [recessions](/recession) have occurred without a clear prior inversion. But the correlation is strong enough that the yield curve is used as a leading indicator.

Why does it work? An inverted curve signals that the market expects weak growth and falling [interest rates](/interest-rate) in the future. The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve) typically inverts the curve by keeping short-term [rates](/interest-rate) elevated while long-term [rates](/dividend) fall (as investors expect future rate cuts). The Fed raises rates to fight inflation, but if this tightening is too aggressive, it slows growth, inflation falls, the Fed cuts rates, and a [recession](/recession) may follow.

## The spread as a signal

Often, yield-curve analysis focuses on a single number: the **spread** between long and short rates. The "2–10 spread" is the difference between the ten-year Treasury [yield](/dividend) and the two-year Treasury [yield](/dividend). When the 2–10 spread is negative (inverted), it is a recession warning.

The spread is simple to track and is widely reported. A positive spread signals normalcy; a negative spread signals stress. As the spread narrows and approaches zero, the market is signaling uncertainty; once it inverts, it signals heightened recession risk.

## Treasury curve vs. corporate curve

The **Treasury yield curve** is the government [bond](/bond) curve (Treasuries are backed by the US government and are effectively zero-default-risk). It is the most-watched curve and is the baseline for all other [bond](/bond) pricing.

The **corporate [bond](/bond) curve** is the yield curve for private companies' [bonds](/bond). Corporate [yields](/dividend) are always higher than Treasuries (to compensate for default risk), and the corporate curve also inverts sometimes. A widening spread between corporate and Treasury [yields](/dividend) (the "credit spread") signals rising default risk and often coincides with a [recession](/recession). Conversely, a narrowing spread signals confidence.

## What the yield curve does not tell you

The yield curve is powerful but easily misunderstood:

**It is not a perfect recession predictor.** A few inversions have not been followed by [recessions](/recession), and the lead time is variable. Investors sometimes invert the curve in error, only to recover before [recession](/recession) arrives.

**It reflects many forces.** An inverted curve can signal recession risk, but it can also reflect very low inflation expectations, a [flight to safety](/stock-market) (investors buying long [bonds](/bond) regardless of recession risk), or even policy distortions. The [Federal Reserve](/federal-reserve) itself has inverted the curve in the past by holding short rates artificially low, buying long [bonds](/bond), or both.

**It says nothing about timing.** Even if the curve inverts and a [recession](/recession) arrives, it could be 18 months away. Investors who act on an inversion immediately often exit equities too early and miss subsequent gains.

**It is not a valuation tool.** The yield curve tells you about [interest rates](/interest-rate) and recession risk, but not whether [stocks](/stock) are cheap or expensive. A steep curve can occur with [stocks](/stock) at any [price-to-earnings ratio](/price-to-earnings-ratio).

## Using the yield curve wisely

Serious investors monitor the yield curve as one data point among many:

- **For macro outlook:** Is the curve steep (recovery signal), flat (transition signal), or inverted (recession warning)?
- **For portfolio positioning:** A flattening or inverted curve might prompt a shift away from equities toward [bonds](/bond), or a reduction in leverage.
- **For credit decisions:** A widening corporate-Treasury spread warns of rising default risk; a narrowing spread suggests confidence.
- **For [interest rate](/interest-rate) strategy:** An inverted curve that is likely to normalize suggests potential losses for long-duration [bond](/bond) positions.

But the curve should not be your only signal. Growth data, unemployment, corporate earnings, and [inflation](/inflation) expectations matter too. The best investors pair the yield curve with a broader set of economic and market indicators.
