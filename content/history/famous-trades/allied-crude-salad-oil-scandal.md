*The **Allied Crude Salad Oil Scandal** was a massive commodity fraud that erupted in 1963 and nearly brought down American Express. A warehouse operator named Tino De Angelis filled storage tanks with seawater while issuing fake warehouse receipts claiming salad oil was inside. When the fraud unraveled, losses exceeded $150 million (roughly $1.3 billion in today's money), markets crashed, and the American financial system nearly fractured. It remains a textbook example of how abstract trust in paperwork can collapse when the underlying asset is pure fiction.*

## The Setup: Trust Without Verification

In the early 1960s, American commodity traders—especially in vegetable oils—relied on a simple system. If you wanted to buy soybean oil or salad oil for future delivery, a producer or trader would store it in a licensed warehouse. The warehouse would issue a receipt (usually certified by a bank or inspection company), and that receipt became tradable like currency. You could buy, sell, and eventually take delivery based entirely on the receipt. No one needed to physically inspect the oil; the receipt *was* the oil.

This system worked well when warehouses were honest. But Tino De Angelis, a New Jersey-based trader and warehouse operator, saw an opportunity.

De Angelis controlled a warehouse complex in New Jersey. Beginning in the late 1950s, he began issuing warehouse receipts for far more salad oil than actually existed. The tanks appeared full to inspectors—he used simple tricks, like filling the bottoms with seawater and floating oil on top, or misrepresenting tank volumes. Banks accepted the receipts and lent against them. Traders accepted the receipts and bought and sold them in futures markets. By the early 1960s, De Angelis had issued receipts claiming he held over 1 billion pounds of vegetable oil.

The actual inventory was a fraction of that.

## How the Fraud Scaled

De Angelis's scheme worked because the incentives aligned to avoid scrutiny:

- **Banks wanted the interest**: They lent money against the receipts at profitable rates
- **Traders wanted the supply**: Oil was scarce in spot markets; the receipts promised inventory when physical supply was tight
- **Exchange officials trusted the system**: The American Commodity Exchange (which later merged to become COMEX) assumed warehouse certifications were binding
- **American Express assumed liability**: AMEX guaranteed the authenticity of warehouse receipts, making them nearly as good as cash

De Angelis borrowed heavily against these fake receipts, using the proceeds to buy real salad oil futures contracts. He then tried to drive futures prices higher, betting he could profit before the fraud was discovered. For a time, it worked—he was listed among the top commodity traders in America.

But oil prices softened in late 1963. De Angelis's strategy began to crack. His lenders and trading counterparties demanded delivery of physical oil. That was when warehouse inspections revealed the truth: most of the tanks were empty or filled with water.

## The Unraveling

On November 19, 1963, the swindle exploded. Inspectors found that De Angelis's warehouse held nowhere near the claimed 1 billion pounds. The market panicked. Salad oil futures—which had rallied on the belief that supply was constrained—crashed.

Traders who had bought De Angelis's fake receipts and hedged in futures markets faced a double loss: the receipts were worthless, and their hedges against spot prices had blown up. Counterparties holding derivative positions against the fraud discovered that their counterparties couldn't pay.

The damage radiated outward. American Express, which had guaranteed the warehouse receipts, faced claims of $150 million or more. For context, AMEX's annual earnings at the time were roughly $40 million. The company's credibility and capital base were on the line.

## The Near-Collapse of American Express

The scandal threatened American Express's solvency. Customers and trading partners lost faith in the company's guarantee. The firm's stock plummeted. Had AMEX failed, it would have cascaded through banking, insurance, and trading networks—the financial system of 1963 was heavily interconnected.

The government intervened. The Federal Reserve coordinated a bailout and guarantee program. Major banks agreed to shore up AMEX with liquidity lines. The crisis was contained, but it was close.

AMEX survived, but the near-death experience reshaped the company's risk culture and ultimately contributed to its shift away from commodity trading toward credit card and travel services—the businesses that would define AMEX for decades.

## Market Consequences and Reform

The scandal devastated the salad oil futures market. Prices that had rallied on the false supply constraints collapsed. Hedges that traders thought were safe turned worthless. Confidence in futures contracts and warehouse certificates evaporated overnight.

Regulatory reforms followed:

- **Stricter warehouse inspections**: Independent inspectors were required more frequently and rigorously
- **Segregation of customer funds**: Clearer rules about what could and couldn't be held against warehouse receipts
- **Disclosure rules**: More transparency about inventory composition and warehouse operations
- **Exchange surveillance**: Commodity exchanges tightened monitoring of large positions and unusual trading

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission, created in 1974 (over a decade later), was partly a response to the awareness that commodity markets needed federal oversight. The salad oil scandal showed that unregulated warehouse systems and commodity futures trading could hide enormous fraud.

## Why It Mattered Beyond Commodity Markets

The scandal revealed a fundamental weakness in any financial system based on paperwork and trust: when the underlying asset is fictional, the entire chain of transactions—futures hedges, bank loans, trader positions—becomes unstable. A futures contract is only as good as the physical commodity it claims to represent.

More broadly, it exposed the concentration risk of single-point guarantees. AMEX's guarantee was only as strong as AMEX's capital, which was being consumed by the fraud itself. No single institution's promise, no matter how blue-chip, can substitute for proper verification of the underlying asset.

## The Lesson for Modern Finance

The salad oil scandal seems quaint in an age of digital verification, barcode scanning, and satellite imagery. But its essence repeats. In 2008, mortgage-backed securities failed partly because the underlying mortgages were misrepresented. In commodity markets, storage fraud still surfaces—gold stored in vaults that don't actually hold it, or metal stored elsewhere than claimed. The medium changes; the risk remains: paperwork, trust, and verification gaps.
