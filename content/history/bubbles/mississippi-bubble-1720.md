*The **Mississippi Bubble of 1720** was France's first great financial crash, triggered by John Law's Mississippi Company—a speculative venture backed by inflated paper money and impossible claims about North American wealth. In fewer than three years, the scheme went from state-sanctioned glory to complete collapse, wiping out fortunes and discrediting paper currency across Europe for a generation.*

## John Law and the Banque Royale

In 1717, France was bankrupt. Decades of war under Louis XIV had depleted the royal treasury, and the state owed creditors enormous sums. John Law, a Scottish mathematician and entrepreneur living in exile in Paris, proposed a solution: establish a note-issuing bank (the Banque Royale, or Royal Bank) that would increase the money supply, lower interest rates, and stimulate commerce. The idea had merit in principle—increased liquidity can spur economic activity—but Law's execution rested on a dangerous assumption: that paper money could expand indefinitely without a solid foundation of real assets or genuine economic growth.

Law convinced the French crown that his system would work. In 1717, the Banque Royale was chartered. It began issuing paper notes backed ostensibly by royal revenue and gold reserves. Crucially, Law held the contract to collect taxes and the right to issue the notes. This gave him control over both the money supply and the primary mechanism for validating those notes. The French king, desperate to stabilize finances, granted Law a near-monopoly on French commerce and colonial trade.

## The Mississippi Company Scheme

In 1718, Law expanded his vision. He formed the Compagnie des Indes (Mississippi Company), granted control of all French colonial trade and territory in North America, including the Mississippi River valley. The company was meant to extract vast wealth from these territories—gold, silver, furs, and agricultural products that would flow back to France and validate the paper money already in circulation.

The critical flaw: those territorial claims and the wealth they promised were largely imaginary. The Mississippi Valley was sparsely settled, underdeveloped, and far more difficult to exploit than Law's marketing suggested. Yet Law promoted the Mississippi Company with fervent propaganda. He issued stock and offered shares to the public, claiming that colonial resources would soon generate enormous profits.

Stock prices soared. In 1719, Mississippi Company shares were trading at 500 livres. By September 1719, they had risen to 5,000 livres. By January 1720, prices approached 10,000 livres—a rise of nearly 2,000% in about six months. At its peak, Mississippi Company stock briefly reached valuations equivalent to 20% of all French national wealth.

## The Mechanics of the Bubble

Two factors amplified the bubble. First, Law's Banque Royale kept issuing paper notes to finance speculation and purchases of Mississippi stock. The more paper entered circulation, the more it fueled demand for the stock. Money and stock price were locked in a feedback loop.

Second, the French crown bought heavily into the scheme. The state used Banque Royale notes to purchase Mississippi stock, further validating the currency and signaling royal confidence. Foreign investors, impressed by official endorsement, poured money into Paris. By some estimates, the Banque Royale quadrupled the money supply between 1717 and 1720—a monetary explosion with no underlying economic growth to support it.

Wealthy and middle-class investors speculated recklessly. Fortunes were made quickly on paper. The Dutch tulip mania fifty years earlier had involved a luxury bulb; the Mississippi bubble was far more consequential because it entangled state finance, currency, and colonial ambitions. Failure would be systemic.

## The Collapse

By mid-1720, reality began to intrude. Reports from Mississippi colonies revealed that no gold or silver riches had materialized. The wealth was a fantasy. At the same time, the sheer quantity of Banque Royale notes in circulation started to erode confidence in the paper itself. If the underlying assets (colonial wealth, royal revenue, gold reserves) didn't exist, why should paper notes be accepted as valuable?

In August 1720, Law raised interest rates in a desperate bid to contain the paper supply. The move signaled panic. Investors rushed to exchange their notes and shares for real goods or precious metals. Mississippi stock collapsed. In just weeks, prices fell from 10,000 livres to a few hundred. Fortunes evaporated. Creditors and investors who had borrowed heavily to buy stock faced ruin.

Law, facing mob fury, fled France. The Banque Royale was wound down. The French crown repudiated much of the paper currency—declaring that many notes issued late in the bubble would not be honored at full value. Those holding the notes took massive losses.

## Economic Consequences

The Mississippi Bubble's collapse left France's finances in worse shape than before. Rather than curing insolvency, Law's scheme had deepened it. The crown's reputation for honoring obligations was damaged. International creditors grew cautious about lending to France. Trust in paper money was shattered.

For the next 100+ years, paper currency was treated with suspicion across Europe. When nations issued notes, they were backed, at least nominally, by specie (gold or silver) held in reserve—a constraint that Law's system had abandoned. The gold standard, or close variants, became the rule precisely because Law's experiment had shown the dangers of unchecked paper expansion.

Within France, the Mississippi Bubble became synonymous with financial ruin and irrational speculation. French literature, memoirs, and satirical prints referenced the bubble for decades as the epitome of foolishness and greed. The experience influenced French finance for a century.

## Historical Significance and Echoes

The Mississippi Bubble was the first great paper-money crisis and one of the first episodes where a speculative asset bubble destroyed public trust in an entire monetary system. It preceded the 1929 stock market crash by more than 200 years, yet the pattern was recognizable: inflated asset prices, widespread borrowing to buy, official encouragement, a sudden loss of confidence, and mass bankruptcies.

The bubble also illustrates the danger of coupling unlimited credit expansion with speculative assets disconnected from real value. Modern economists point to the Mississippi Bubble when cautioning against rapid monetary expansion not anchored in underlying productivity or real assets. Central banks monitor monetary aggregates and asset prices precisely because of lessons drawn from episodes like this one.

The Mississippi Bubble also exposed a structural problem: when the same person or entity controls both the money supply (the Banque Royale) and the primary speculative asset (Mississippi stock), conflicts of interest are inevitable and catastrophic. The bank can inflate the currency to prop up the asset, but eventually the gap between paper and reality becomes unsustainable. Modern financial regulation, including separation of central banking from commercial ventures, owes something to the failures Law's monopoly revealed.
