*The **Sarbanes-Oxley Act** (SOX), enacted in July 2002, was the most comprehensive overhaul of US [corporate governance](/wiki/board-of-directors/) and [securities regulation](/wiki/securities-and-exchange-commission/) since the [Securities Act of 1933](/wiki/securities-act-of-1933/). Triggered by the spectacular [accounting fraud](/wiki/enron-accounting-fraud/) at [Enron](/wiki/enron-scandal/) and [WorldCom](/wiki/worldcom-scandal/), SOX mandated that [CEOs](/wiki/ceo-compensation/) personally certify financial [statements](/wiki/income-statement/), prohibited [auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/) from providing [consulting services](/wiki/consulting/), and created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) to regulate [auditing](/wiki/audit-opinion/). While praised for restoring investor confidence, SOX's compliance costs became a burden on small [public companies](/wiki/public-company/) and raised questions about its [cost-benefit](/wiki/cost-of-carry/) trade-off.*

## The disasters that prompted reform

In December 2001, [Enron](/wiki/enron-scandal/), once a $111 billion [market cap](/wiki/market-capitalization/) energy trading giant, imploded. The company had hidden $1.3 billion in [liabilities](/wiki/liability/) using [special purpose entities](/wiki/special-purpose-acquisition/) and shell companies, with [auditor](/wiki/audit-opinion/) Arthur Andersen signing off despite red flags. In a week, [Enron](/wiki/enron-scandal/) went from respectable to bankrupt, wiping out $74 billion in [shareholder value](/wiki/equity-financing/) and destroying 5,600 jobs. Employees lost life savings; [pension funds](/wiki/pension-obligation/) wrote off billions; the public was enraged.

The Enron scandal revealed systemic rot. [Auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/) had not been independent—they earned massive [fees](/wiki/management-fee/) from Enron for [consulting](/wiki/consulting/) work (Arthur Andersen earned $27 million/year from Enron, of which only $25 million was for [audit](/wiki/audit-opinion/)). [Boards](/wiki/board-of-directors/) had been asleep, with [audit committees](/wiki/audit-committee/) rubber-stamping [management](/wiki/compensation-committee/). [Financial statements](/wiki/income-statement/) had been opaque, using aggressive [accounting](/wiki/accrual-accounting/) choices that technically complied with [GAAP](/wiki/generally-accepted-accounting-principles/) but masked the company's true condition.

In mid-2002, [WorldCom](/wiki/worldcom-scandal/), a $100+ billion [telecom](/wiki/telecommunications/) [company](/wiki/public-company/), revealed it had overstated [income](/wiki/earnings-per-share/) by $3.9 billion via capitalized [operating expenses](/wiki/operating-expense/) that should have been expensed. Again, [auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/) at [Arthur Andersen](/wiki/enron-scandal/) missed or ignored the fraud. The public and Congress demanded action.

## The four pillars of Sarbanes-Oxley

**Section 302: CEO and CFO Certification**. CEOs and Chief Financial Officers must personally certify the accuracy of [quarterly and annual financial statements](/wiki/earnings-per-share/). If [statements](/wiki/income-statement/) are later found to be materially inaccurate and certification was made without reasonable care, executives face criminal penalties. This created personal accountability where none existed before.

**Section 404: Internal Control Assessment**. [Public companies](/wiki/public-company/) must implement and audit [internal controls](/wiki/internal-control-assessment/) over [financial reporting](/wiki/financial-information-exchange/). [Management](/wiki/compensation-committee/) must attest to the effectiveness of these controls; [external auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/) must separately audit the controls. This codified best practices (segregation of duties, authorization matrices, [audit trails](/wiki/consolidated-audit-trail/)) that good [companies](/wiki/public-company/) followed, but many had ignored.

**Section 301: Audit Committee Independence**. [Audit committees](/wiki/audit-committee/) of [public company boards](/wiki/board-of-directors/) must be [independent](/wiki/central-bank-independence/) of [management](/wiki/compensation-committee/), with at least one financial expert. They directly oversee [auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/) and [management's](/wiki/compensation-committee/) [financial reporting](/wiki/financial-information-exchange/).

**Section 201: Auditor Independence**. [Auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/) are prohibited from providing [consulting](/wiki/consulting/), tax, or valuation services to [audit clients](/wiki/audit-opinion/). This breaks the conflicted incentive where [auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/) earn far more from [consulting](/wiki/consulting/) than [auditing](/wiki/audit-opinion/), incentivizing them to overlook client misbehavior.

## Additional provisions

**PCAOB creation**: The Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, a [regulator](/wiki/securities-and-exchange-commission/) independent of the accounting profession, was created to set [auditing](/wiki/audit-opinion/) standards, inspect [audit](/wiki/audit-opinion/) [firms](/wiki/broker/), and discipline [auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/). Before SOX, [auditing](/wiki/audit-opinion/) was self-regulated by the [AICPA](/wiki/american-institute-of-certified-public-accountants/); SOX shifted oversight to a public regulator.

**Criminal penalties**: Section 906 created new crimes—falsifying [financial statements](/wiki/income-statement/) carries up to 20 years prison; securities fraud, conspiracy, and obstruction carry severe penalties. [Auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/) who destroy documents face fines and imprisonment. These teeth, though rarely deployed, sent a signal.

**Debt vs. equity**: Section 102 banned [audit firms](/wiki/audit-opinion/) from holding [equity](/wiki/equity-financing/) or [debt](/wiki/debt-structure/) [stakes](/wiki/common-stock/) in [audit clients](/wiki/audit-opinion/), preventing conflicts from [capital investments](/wiki/capital-allocation/).

**Whistleblower protection**: SOX protected employees who reported violations to [auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/) or law enforcement from [retaliation](/wiki/retaliation/).

## The compliance burden and [cost-benefit](/wiki/cost-of-carry/) debate

Section 404 compliance became expensive. A [Fortune 500](/wiki/market-capitalization/) company might spend $15–50 million annually on [internal control](/wiki/internal-control-assessment/) documentation, testing, and [auditor](/wiki/audit-opinion/) certification. Smaller [public companies](/wiki/public-company/) faced proportionally larger costs; a $100 million [company](/wiki/public-company/) might spend $500,000–$1 million on SOX compliance—a 1% [tax](/wiki/capital-gains-tax/) on [earnings](/wiki/earnings-per-share/).

Critics argued the [costs](/wiki/cost-structure/) exceeded benefits. Fraud was not eliminated; subsequent [scandals](/wiki/accounting-fraud/)—[Bernie Madoff](/wiki/ponzi-scheme/), [Lehman Brothers](/wiki/lehman-brothers-collapse/)—happened under SOX's watch. Some [financial economists](/wiki/behavioral-finance/) claimed SOX discouraged [IPOs](/wiki/initial-public-offering/) and [companies](/wiki/public-company/) going [public](/wiki/public-company/), reducing US [capital market](/wiki/capital-markets/) efficiency.

Proponents countered that the [costs](/wiki/cost-structure/) were worth the [integrity](/wiki/audit-opinion/) gains. [Auditor](/wiki/audit-opinion/) independence, [CEO](/wiki/ceo-compensation/) accountability, and [internal control](/wiki/internal-control-assessment/) rigor did raise financial [statement](/wiki/income-statement/) quality. [Auditor](/wiki/audit-opinion/) misconduct became rarer. [Boards](/wiki/board-of-directors/) became more engaged.

The US eventually offered relief: [companies](/wiki/public-company/) under $75 million in [market cap](/wiki/market-capitalization/) were exempted from full Section 404(b) audits, reducing burden while preserving control over [management](/wiki/compensation-committee/)'s control [assessments](/wiki/internal-control-assessment/).

## International adoption

SOX's influence spread globally. The EU adopted similar [auditor](/wiki/audit-opinion/) independence rules and [audit committee](/wiki/audit-committee/) mandates. [Companies](/wiki/public-company/) listed on US [exchanges](/wiki/stock-exchange/) (whether US or foreign) must comply with SOX; non-US [companies](/wiki/public-company/) listing internationally tailored their governance to meet multiple [regulatory](/wiki/regulation-best-interest/) regimes.

However, most nations did not adopt SOX wholesale, instead developing homegrown frameworks. The UK's Combined Code (later UK Corporate Governance Code) offered principles-based governance; Canada's rules were less prescriptive. This fragmentation created compliance complexity for multinational [companies](/wiki/public-company/).

## Legacy: transformation of the audit profession

Pre-SOX, [auditing](/wiki/audit-opinion/) was a backend operation—prepare the audit, sign off, collect fees. Post-SOX, [auditing](/wiki/audit-opinion/) became strategic. [CEOs](/wiki/ceo-compensation/) and [CFOs](/wiki/cfo-role/) had personal skin in the game; [audit committees](/wiki/audit-committee/) became power centers; [auditors](/wiki/audit-opinion/) were viewed as [internal control](/wiki/internal-control-assessment/) architects, not rubber stampers.

Arthur Andersen dissolved in 2002, destroyed by the Enron scandal. The [Big Four](/wiki/big-four-auditors/) accounting [firms](/wiki/broker/)—Deloitte, EY, KPMG, PwC—became even more dominant. SOX's compliance burden favored large [audit firms](/wiki/audit-opinion/) with technology and [systems](/wiki/accounting-systems/) to manage [documentation](/wiki/record-keeping/); smaller firms struggled and merged or exited the [public company](/wiki/public-company/) [audit market](/wiki/audit-opinion/).
