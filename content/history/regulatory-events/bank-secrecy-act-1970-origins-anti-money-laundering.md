*The **Bank Secrecy Act of 1970** created the legal infrastructure for reporting large currency transactions and maintaining customer records, becoming the statutory bedrock of modern anti-money-laundering enforcement in the United States. Congress passed it in response to rising concern that cash movements through the banking system were obscuring the origins of illicit proceeds and enabling tax evasion.*

## The Problem Congress Addressed

Before 1970, banks had no federal obligation to report who was depositing large sums of currency or to retain records that would help authorities trace the flow of money through the system. Criminals, drug traffickers, and tax evaders exploited this gap, moving cash across borders or depositing it domestically with impunity. Law enforcement had few legal tools to detect or interrupt these flows; they could not even demand that banks keep the records needed to follow the money after the fact.

The perceived urgency reflected not only rising organized crime but also a broader shift in American financial supervision. By the late 1960s, policymakers recognized that modern crime—and financial crime especially—required information systems, not just police work. The Act emerged from that conviction.

## Core Reporting Requirements

The Bank Secrecy Act imposed two main obligations on financial institutions:

**Currency Transaction Reports (CTR).** Banks had to file a form (now FinCEN Form 104) whenever a customer conducted a cash transaction exceeding $10,000 in a single day. The report included the customer's name, address, and tax identification number, along with the amount and date. The $10,000 threshold, unchanged since 1970, remains in effect today.

**Record-Keeping.** Banks were required to maintain records of customer identity, account activity, and correspondence for at least five years. This created the paper trail that government investigators could follow if they obtained a subpoena or court order.

The Act also granted the Secretary of the Treasury authority to issue regulations for compliance, laying the groundwork for the regulatory infrastructure that later developed—including the modern anti-money-laundering examination program run by financial regulators.

## Constitutional Challenges and the Court's Acceptance

Banks and some civil liberties advocates challenged the Act on Fourth Amendment grounds, arguing that mandatory reporting of lawful transactions without a warrant violated privacy protections. In *California Bankers Association v. Shultz* (1974), the Supreme Court upheld the law, holding that depositors have no reasonable expectation of privacy in banking records shared with third parties, and that the government's interest in detecting crime justified the disclosure requirement.

This decision proved pivotal. It established that financial transparency—at least as far as banks' records are concerned—is a legitimate government objective that can override privacy objections. The principle later extended to other financial intermediaries and became the constitutional moat protecting the entire apparatus of financial surveillance that followed.

## The Structuring Problem

Almost immediately after the Act took effect, a loophole emerged: criminals split large deposits into smaller ones—just below $10,000—to avoid filing a CTR. This practice, called "structuring" or "smurfing," allowed money launderers to slip proceeds through the system undetected. By the 1980s, structuring had become so widespread that it undermined the Act's effectiveness.

Congress responded in 1986 by amending the Act to make structuring itself illegal, regardless of whether the underlying deposit was legitimate. Banks were also directed to file a *Suspicious Activity Report (SAR)* if they suspected a customer was deliberately avoiding the reporting threshold. This expanded the information burden on financial institutions but made it far harder to move illicit cash undetected.

## Evolution Into the Modern AML Framework

The Bank Secrecy Act was the first domino. Throughout the 1980s and 1990s, Congress and regulators built on its foundation:

- The **Money Laundering Control Act of 1986** criminalized the act of moving money with knowledge that it came from a crime.
- The **Annunzio-Wylie Anti-Money Laundering Act of 1992** introduced Know Your Customer (KYC) rules, requiring banks to verify the identity and beneficial ownership of account holders.
- The **USA PATRIOT Act of 2001** dramatically expanded reporting obligations and reached beyond banks to casinos, securities brokers, and other financial players.
- The **Financial Action Task Force**, an international body established in 1989, adopted the Bank Secrecy Act as a model for coordinated global anti-money-laundering standards.

Today, the Bank Secrecy Act framework—CTRs, SARs, KYC, enhanced due diligence—is the skeleton on which the entire U.S. and international AML regime hangs. Thousands of financial institutions employ teams of compliance officers whose sole job is to monitor for suspicious activity and file reports under authorities ultimately grounded in the 1970 statute.

## Practical Impact and Criticism

The Act succeeded in creating a data trail. Federal agencies can now match deposits against law enforcement databases and subpoena records to trace proceeds. Major prosecutions—from organized crime to terrorism financing to sanction evasion—rely on CTRs and bank records obtained under the Act's authority.

Critics argue the regime has grown unwieldy and costly. Compliance expenditure by financial institutions has ballooned into billions annually, and false-positive rates in SAR filings remain high; the vast majority of flagged transactions turn out to be innocent. Some argue that the burden falls disproportionately on small banks and that large institutions have better resources to manage it. There is also a persistent debate about whether the regime has meaningfully deterred major financial crimes or simply redistributed cost and complexity across the system.

Nevertheless, the statutory foundation remains intact and accepted. The Bank Secrecy Act's central premise—that financial transparency and government visibility into money flows is both constitutional and necessary—has not been seriously challenged since 1974.
