*The **Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) crisis** of 1998 was a near-catastrophic failure of a $4.7 billion [hedge fund](/wiki/hedge-fund/) staffed with Nobel Prize winners and financial engineers. LTCM had built massive [leveraged](/wiki/leverage-ratio-forex/) bets on the theory that [bond yield spreads](/wiki/yield-curve-shape/) would converge to historical norms—a bet that imploded when the [Russian financial crisis](/wiki/russian-financial-crisis-1998/) triggered global [risk-off](/wiki/risk-on-risk-off/) panic. Within weeks, LTCM lost $4.6 billion, and its [counterparty risk](/wiki/counterparty-risk/) threatened to bankrupt a dozen major [investment banks](/wiki/investment-company-act-of-1940/). The [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/) orchestrated a $3.6 billion private [bailout](/wiki/aig-bailout/) to prevent [systemic collapse](/wiki/systemic-risk/).*

## The strategy: convergence and relative-value arbitrage

LTCM was founded in 1994 by John Meriwether, a legendary [bond trader](/wiki/bond-trader/) from Salomon Brothers, alongside Robert Merton and Myron Scholes, both of whom had won Nobel Prizes in Economics. The fund's core thesis was that [yield spreads](/wiki/yield-curve-shape/)—the [gaps](/wiki/gap/) between [Treasury yields](/wiki/treasury-bill/) and [corporate bond](/wiki/corporate-bond/) yields, or between emerging market [bonds](/wiki/bond/) and US [Treasuries](/wiki/treasury-bill/)—would converge to long-term historical averages.

This was a **[relative-value arbitrage](/wiki/hedge-fund-relative-value/)** strategy. If the [spread](/wiki/credit-spread/) between [US Treasuries](/wiki/treasury-bill/) and [Italian government bonds](/wiki/government-bond-auction/) was 150 basis points but historical average was 50 bps, LTCM would:
- **Sell** (short) [US Treasuries](/wiki/treasury-bill/) (or equivalently, buy [Treasuries](/wiki/treasury-bill/) and hedge with [interest rate swaps](/wiki/interest-rate-swap/))
- **Buy** Italian [bonds](/wiki/bond/)
- If the [spread](/wiki/credit-spread/) converged, the [long](/wiki/long-call-ladder/) Italian position would outperform the short US position, locking in a profit

The genius was that this trade was "market-neutral"—if [rates](/wiki/federal-funds-rate/) rose or fell, the long and short positions moved together, offsetting interest-rate risk. The profit came purely from spread convergence, not from directional bets. Theoretically, this was low-risk, even though spreads were small.

## The leverage problem

The catch: LTCM applied **massive leverage**. The [spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) it was betting on—50 to 100 basis points—seem tiny. To turn them into meaningful [returns](/wiki/return-on-equity/), LTCM had to scale positions vastly. With $4.7 billion in [capital](/wiki/capital-allocation/), the fund controlled $100+ billion in notional [positions](/wiki/position-trading/) (roughly 20–25x leverage). This was not exceptional by [derivatives](/wiki/option/) market standards, but it meant that a small adverse move in [spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) would wipe out the fund.

In 1997–early 1998, the strategy was printing money. Spreads did converge; the fund returned 20%+ annually. LTCM became renowned, attracting massive [capital](/wiki/capital-allocation/) from [pension funds](/wiki/pension-obligation/), [endowments](/wiki/endowment-fund-structure/), and [institutional investors](/wiki/qualified-institutional-buyer/). The fund and Meriwether became celebrities.

The danger was that success bred complacency. LTCM assumed that [spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) followed historical distributions; that past correlations would hold; that [funding markets](/wiki/repo-margin-framework/) would always be open to refinance [positions](/wiki/position-trading/). These assumptions had not been tested in a severe [crisis](/wiki/financial-crisis/).

## The Russian crisis and contagion (August 1998)

On August 17, 1998, Russia [defaulted](/wiki/sovereign-default/) on its domestic [debt](/wiki/sovereign-debt/) and devalued the [ruble](/wiki/currency-pair/). The move shocked markets. Russia was a major [emerging market](/wiki/emerging-market-type/); the [default](/wiki/sovereign-default/) was sudden and unexpected. [Risk appetite](/wiki/risk-on-risk-off/) evaporated instantly.

Capital that had been chasing [emerging market](/wiki/emerging-markets-fund/) yield (buying [Russian bonds](/wiki/bond/), [currency forwards](/wiki/currency-future/), etc.) fled. The "risk-off" [contagion](/wiki/financial-crisis/) spread: investors dumped Asian assets, emerging market [currencies](/wiki/currency-pair/), and [high-yield bonds](/wiki/high-yield-bond/). [Spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) widened violently—exactly opposite to what LTCM was betting on.

Within days, spreads that LTCM expected to narrow started blowing out. A [Treasury](/wiki/treasury-bill/)-to-Italian-bond [spread](/wiki/credit-spread/) that should have stayed near 50 bps widened to 200 bps. LTCM's [leveraged](/wiki/leverage-ratio-forex/) [long](/wiki/long-call-ladder/) position in Italian [bonds](/wiki/bond/) and [short](/wiki/short-selling/) [Treasury](/wiki/treasury-bill/) position meant losses compounded. The [capital](/wiki/capital-allocation/) on the [hedge](/wiki/currency-hedging/) side (short Treasuries) lost as [Treasury](/wiki/treasury-bill/) prices rose (yields fell) during the risk-off panic. The [capital](/wiki/capital-allocation/) on the bet side (long credits) lost as credit [spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) widened. It was a double loss.

## The fund's collapse and [counterparty](/wiki/counterparty-risk/) panic

By late August, LTCM had lost $1.5 billion. Lenders and [counterparties](/wiki/counterparty-risk/)—banks that had [credit exposure](/wiki/counterparty-credit-risk/) to LTCM—began demanding [collateral](/wiki/collateral-ratio/) [margin](/wiki/forex-margin/) calls. LTCM's [capital](/wiki/capital-allocation/) was eroding; the [leverage](/wiki/leverage-ratio-forex/) ratio was spiraling. A 25:1 leveraged position with 5% [capital](/wiki/capital-allocation/) loss becomes 50:1 overnight.

LTCM faced a vicious cycle: [losses](/wiki/loss-severity/) trigger [margin calls](/wiki/margin-call-forex/); [margin calls](/wiki/margin-call-forex/) force [liquidation](/wiki/liquidation/) of positions; [liquidations](/wiki/liquidation/) push [spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) wider (moving against the fund); wider [spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) trigger larger [losses](/wiki/loss-severity/). Within weeks, the fund's $4.7 billion [capital](/wiki/capital-allocation/) had shrunk to near zero.

But the real danger was [systemic](/wiki/systemic-risk/). LTCM was a [counterparty](/wiki/counterparty-risk/) to nearly every major [investment bank](/wiki/investment-company-act-of-1940/): Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Merrill Lynch, Bear Stearns, Chase, Bank of America, and others. The fund owed them billions and could not pay. If LTCM was forced to unwind its $100+ billion in [positions](/wiki/position-trading/) simultaneously across illiquid markets, the [liquidations](/wiki/liquidation/) would cause [spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) to widen further, triggering [contagion](/wiki/financial-crisis/) in [credit markets](/wiki/credit-derivatives/). [Banks](/wiki/bank-reserve-injection/) holding [positions](/wiki/position-trading/) similar to LTCM's would face massive [losses](/wiki/loss-severity/). Some might fail.

[Systemic risk](/wiki/systemic-risk/) was high. The [Federal Reserve](/wiki/federal-reserve/), under [Alan Greenspan](/wiki/alan-greenspan/), feared a cascading collapse. [Banks](/wiki/bank-reserve-injection/) would become reluctant to lend to each other; [credit markets](/wiki/credit-derivatives/) would seize up; [leverage](/wiki/leverage-ratio-forex/) would evaporate across the financial system.

## The [bailout](/wiki/aig-bailout/): coordinated [liquidation](/wiki/liquidation/)

Rather than allow a [bankruptcy](/wiki/bankruptcy/) and [liquidation](/wiki/liquidation/), the Fed orchestrated a [bailout](/wiki/aig-bailout/). Fourteen [banks](/wiki/bank-reserve-injection/) collectively contributed $3.6 billion to take over LTCM's positions and wind them down in an orderly manner. This prevented a sudden market shock and gave the banks time to unwind [positions](/wiki/position-trading/) gradually as [spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) normalized.

The [bailout](/wiki/aig-bailout/) was controversial. Critics argued that the Fed was subsidizing reckless risk-taking by LTCM and its [banks](/wiki/bank-reserve-injection/), creating a [moral hazard](/wiki/moral-hazard/) (if you know the Fed will rescue you, you'll take more risk). Defenders argued that preventing systemic meltdown was worth the cost.

## Aftermath and lessons

LTCM was ultimately wound down by 2000. The [positions](/wiki/position-trading/) were gradually unwound; [spreads](/wiki/credit-spread/) did eventually normalize (validating the fund's original thesis—but too late and too late to matter). [Shareholders](/wiki/shareholder-proposal/) lost nearly everything; creditors recovered much of their [capital](/wiki/capital-allocation/).

The LTCM crisis had lasting impacts:

1. **[Leverage](/wiki/leverage-ratio-forex/) regulations**: The [Basel III](/wiki/basel-iii/) [capital](/wiki/capital-adequacy/) accords included specific restrictions on [leverage](/wiki/leverage-ratio-forex/) for [banks](/wiki/bank-reserve-injection/) and [hedge funds](/wiki/hedge-fund/).

2. **[Counterparty risk](/wiki/counterparty-risk/) management**: [Institutions](/wiki/qualified-institutional-buyer/) became more rigorous about [credit exposure](/wiki/counterparty-credit-risk/) to large [trading counterparties](/wiki/counterparty-credit-risk/).

3. **[Correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) risks**: The crisis showed that correlations assumed in calm periods (e.g., Treasuries and corporates moving differently) can break down in [stress](/wiki/stress-testing/). Diversification cannot be relied upon during true [crises](/wiki/financial-crisis/).

4. **[Central bank](/wiki/central-bank/) lender-of-last-resort role**: The Fed's intervention in a private [hedge fund](/wiki/hedge-fund/) crisis set a precedent for later interventions (2008 [Bear Stearns](/wiki/bear-stearns-collapse/) [bailout](/wiki/aig-bailout/), 2008 AIG [bailout](/wiki/aig-bailout/)), establishing the Fed as backstop to systemic risks.

The LTCM crisis remains the canonical example of how [leverage](/wiki/leverage-ratio-forex/), [correlation](/wiki/correlation-coefficient/) assumptions, and [contagion](/wiki/financial-crisis/) can combine to create [systemic risk](/wiki/systemic-risk/) even in the hands of brilliant mathematicians and [traders](/wiki/trading-halts/).
